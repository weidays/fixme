## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a blinking fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Trane condenser (the outdoor unit). When cooling is called for, the compressor and the outdoor fan motor should start together. The fan pulls air across the condenser coil to dump heat outside.

If the compressor runs but the top fan blade sits still (or just hums), the unit can't reject heat. Pressure and temperature climb fast, and Trane's internal protections will eventually trip the compressor on high-pressure or thermal limits. On communicating XV-series systems you may also see a related fault on the thermostat, but the underlying issue is mechanical or electrical at the outdoor unit.

Leaving it running risks permanent compressor damage — the single most expensive part in the system.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it weakens or fails, the fan hums but won't spin, or won't start at all. This is by far the most common cause.
2. **Seized or worn fan motor bearings** — Age, heat, and debris cause the motor shaft to bind. The motor draws high current, hums, and trips out.
3. **Burned-out condenser fan motor** — Windings fail from age or overheating; the motor is dead with no hum.
4. **Debris blocking the blade** — Sticks, ice, or an animal jamming the fan blade prevents rotation.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — Loses power to the whole outdoor unit; compressor won't run either.
6. **Failed contactor** — The relay that sends power to the fan and compressor is pitted or stuck.
7. **Control board or relay fault** — On XL/XV communicating boards, a failed fan output can leave the motor without a signal. This varies by model.
8. **Loose or corroded wiring** — A disconnected fan lead or corroded terminal.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe with the system powered normally — do **not** open the electrical panel on the condenser.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model and the screen is dim.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the indoor system to shut down and stop the cooling call. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker in your main panel. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Confirm the pull-out disconnect box beside the condenser is fully seated.
- **Visible debris:** With the system **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for obvious sticks, leaves, or ice on the blade. Never reach in with power on.
- **Supply vents:** Make sure interior vents are open and unblocked so the system isn't short-cycling.
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan can trip a float switch and stop cooling — check for standing water near the indoor unit.

If the fan still won't spin after these, it's a pro repair. Turn the system off at the thermostat to protect the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will work in this order — use it to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirm the call:** Verify the thermostat is signaling cooling and 24V reaches the outdoor contactor.
2. **Test the capacitor:** Discharge and measure it with a meter against its microfarad rating. A reading far below spec (or zero) confirms a failed cap — the most common fix.
3. **Check the contactor:** Look for pitted contacts and verify it pulls in and passes voltage to the fan.
4. **Spin test the motor:** With power off, spin the blade by hand. A gritty or stiff shaft indicates bad bearings.
5. **Verify motor windings:** Measure winding resistance and check for an open or shorted motor.
6. **Check amp draw:** A motor pulling high amps points to bearings or a locked rotor.
7. **Inspect wiring and board:** Confirm the fan lead is connected and, on communicating XL/XV boards, that the fan output is active.

A capacitor test is a five-minute check. If a tech quotes a full motor replacement without ever measuring the capacitor, ask why.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums, won't spin; compressor runs | Failed run capacitor | Shut off at thermostat | Test and replace capacitor |
| Fan won't start, gritty when spun by hand | Worn motor bearings | None | Replace condenser fan motor |
| No hum, fan dead, compressor also silent | Tripped breaker / blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Diagnose circuit, contactor, fuse |
| Blade physically jammed | Debris or ice | Power off, remove visible debris | Clear blade, inspect for damage |
| Fan and compressor both won't start | Failed contactor | None | Replace contactor |
| Intermittent fan, communicating fault shown | Board/relay fault (XL/XV) | None | Test fan output, replace board if needed |
| Fan starts then trips breaker | Shorted/failing motor | None | Replace motor, verify wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350. The part is cheap ($15–$40); most of the cost is the service call.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350.
- **Condenser fan motor (with capacitor):** $350 – $650, depending on motor size (typically 1/6–1/2 HP) and whether it's a universal or OEM Trane part.
- **Clearing debris / minor wiring repair:** $100 – $250.
- **Control board (XL/XV communicating models):** $400 – $900+. Boards vary widely by model — get the part number confirmed before approving.
- **DIY breaker reset or debris removal:** $0.

If a tech finds the fan failed because it ran with a stuck blade or bad capacitor, ask them to verify the compressor hasn't been damaged before you approve a repair.

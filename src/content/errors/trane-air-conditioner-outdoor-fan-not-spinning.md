## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Trane outdoor unit (the condenser). The large fan on top of the condenser is supposed to pull air across the coil to dump heat outside. When it won't spin, the system can't reject heat, and your house won't cool no matter how long the compressor runs.

On communicating Trane systems (XV series with a color thermostat), a fan or motor fault may also log an error on the control board or thermostat display. On older single-stage units, there's no code at all — you simply notice the compressor humming while the fan sits still.

**Important:** A non-spinning fan while the compressor runs is a "shut it off now" situation. Continued operation overheats the compressor and can turn a $200 repair into a compressor replacement costing thousands.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The single most common cause. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. When it weakens, the fan hums but won't turn, or won't start at all.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, overheating, or age can lock the motor or open its windings. Often smells burnt.
3. **Faulty contactor** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts can starve the fan of voltage.
4. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, ice, or a bird's nest physically blocking the blade.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — Loss of power to the outdoor unit or its control circuit.
6. **Control board or fan relay failure** — More common on variable-speed/communicating XV units where the board drives the ECM fan motor.
7. **Failed high-pressure or thermal safety switch** — A tripped safety can cut the outdoor unit, which may look like a "dead" fan.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no panels off, no tools inside the electrical box.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the separate outdoor A/C breaker (or disconnect box near the condenser). Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter can cause freeze-ups and pressure trips that shut down the outdoor unit. Replace if dirty.
- **Visible debris:** With the system OFF at the thermostat, look through the top grille for sticks, leaves, or a nest jamming the blade. Clear only what you can reach from outside without removing panels.
- **Ice on the unit:** If the coil or refrigerant line is iced over, turn the system to OFF and the fan to ON at the thermostat to thaw it. Persistent icing points to a refrigerant or airflow problem for a pro.
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan/float switch can shut the system down — check that the drain isn't clogged and the pan isn't overflowing.

Do **not** open the electrical box, spin the fan by hand while powered, or discharge a capacitor yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will work through a logical sequence so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the symptom** — Verify the fan doesn't run while the compressor calls for cooling, and check for humming.
2. **Kill power and test the capacitor** — After discharging it safely, they measure microfarads against the rating printed on the cap (e.g., 45+5 µF). Out-of-spec = replace.
3. **Test the fan motor** — Check windings for continuity/open circuit, spin the blade by hand for bearing drag, and look for burnt smell or discoloration.
4. **Check the contactor** — Inspect for pitting and verify it pulls in and passes voltage to the fan.
5. **Verify voltage and low-voltage control** — Confirm 240V line power, 24V control signal, and any inline fuse.
6. **On communicating XV units** — Read board/thermostat fault history and check the ECM motor's control signal before condemning the motor.
7. **Check safeties and pressures** — Confirm no high-pressure lockout or thermal trip is holding the unit off.

A trustworthy quote names the specific failed part after testing — not just "replace the fan."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't turn | Failed run capacitor | Turn system OFF; don't force blade | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan can be spun by hand, then keeps running | Weak capacitor | Turn off; note behavior | Confirm & replace capacitor |
| Fan blade won't turn by hand | Seized motor bearings | None — power off | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Burnt smell from outdoor unit | Burned-out motor | Shut off system | Replace motor & inspect wiring |
| Compressor and fan both dead | Contactor / breaker / fuse | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, fuse, control |
| Visible debris in blade | Physical jam | Clear debris with power OFF | Inspect blade & motor balance |
| Ice on unit, fan off | Airflow/refrigerant issue | Fan ON to thaw | Diagnose refrigerant/airflow |
| Fault on XV thermostat | ECM motor / board | Note the code | Read board history, test ECM |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (standard PSC):** $300 – $650 installed
- **ECM / variable-speed fan motor (XV units):** $500 – $1,100+ installed
- **Control board (communicating units):** $400 – $900
- **Clearing debris / minor cleanup:** $0 DIY – $120 if part of a service call

Capacitors and contactors are inexpensive parts, so if a tech recommends a full motor or board on an older unit, it's fair to ask them to show the failed test reading first.

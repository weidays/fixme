## What this code means

Trane air conditioners don't usually flash a specific "outdoor fan not spinning" fault code — this is a **symptom**, not a stored error. On most single-stage Trane condensers (XR13, XR14, XR16, XL16i, etc.) there's no fault display outside; you simply notice the top fan sitting still while the compressor hums or the house won't cool.

On communicating variable-speed units (XV18, XV20i) paired with a Trane thermostat, the system *may* log a related fan or motor fault code that appears on the thermostat or the equipment control board. The behavior varies by board and model, so treat any code shown as a supplement to the physical diagnosis below.

The bottom line: the outdoor (condenser) fan pulls heat out of the refrigerant. If it isn't turning while the compressor runs, pressures and temperatures climb fast — so this is a **pro-level problem** you should not run through.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — the single most common cause. The dual capacitor (or a separate fan capacitor) supplies the starting torque. When it weakens, the motor hums but won't spin, or won't start at all.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — worn bearings or a shorted winding. Often smells burnt; the fan may be hard to turn by hand (power off).
3. **Contactor not pulling in** — the 24V contactor that sends 240V to the outdoor unit is stuck, pitted, or its coil is dead, so neither fan nor compressor gets power.
4. **Debris jamming the blade** — leaves, a stick, or ice locking the fan physically.
5. **Tripped high-pressure switch or float safety** — the unit cut power to protect itself (often triggered by an earlier fan or airflow problem).
6. **Low-voltage / control-board fault** — blown 24V fuse on the indoor board, bad relay, or a communication fault on variable-speed models.
7. **Damaged wiring or loose terminals** — heat-degraded connections at the motor, capacitor, or contactor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no panels off, no touching capacitors.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and drop the setpoint 3–4°F below room temp. Confirm the indoor blower kicks on.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter chokes airflow and can trigger safeties. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — call a pro.
- **Thermostat batteries:** Replace them if your thermostat uses them and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Supply/return vents:** Make sure indoor vents are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch (the safety that shuts cooling off when the drain backs up). Clear standing water if you can safely reach it.
- **Visible debris:** With the system OFF at the breaker, look through the top grille for leaves or sticks blocking the blade. Do not reach in past the grille or remove panels.

If the fan still won't run after these, it's a technician job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will typically:

1. Confirm the outdoor unit is getting 240V at the disconnect and that the contactor pulls in when there's a cooling call.
2. Measure the **run capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads (µF) — a weak reading is the smoking gun for a humming fan.
3. Check the **fan motor** for continuity, shorted windings, and free rotation with power off; test amp draw when running.
4. Inspect the **contactor** contacts and test the 24V coil for pull-in.
5. Verify the indoor **24V control fuse** and board relays, and read any codes on communicating systems.
6. Check high-pressure and float safeties to see if the fan stall was a symptom of another fault.

Ask the tech to show you the capacitor µF reading or motor amp draw — that's how you sanity-check whether a full motor replacement is really needed versus a $30 capacitor.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but doesn't spin | Weak/failed run capacitor | Shut unit off; don't run it | Test & replace capacitor |
| No fan, no compressor, no sound outdoors | Contactor or 24V power/fuse | Check breaker & disconnect once | Test contactor coil, replace fuse/contactor |
| Fan blade won't turn by hand (power off) | Debris jam or seized motor | Clear visible debris safely | Replace motor if seized |
| Burnt smell, fan dead | Burned-out motor | Turn off system | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Fan stops after a few minutes, house warm | High-pressure/float safety trip | Check filter & condensate | Diagnose root cause, reset safety |
| Fan intermittent, buzzing terminals | Loose/heat-damaged wiring | None — leave it off | Repair/replace connections |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400 (part is $15–$40; most of the cost is the service call).
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $350–$650, higher for variable-speed ECM motors on XV18/XV20 units, which can run **$700–$1,200+**.
- **24V control fuse / minor wiring repair:** $120–$250.
- **Diagnostic / service call alone:** $75–$200 (often credited toward the repair).
- **Debris clearing during a tune-up:** $0 DIY if you can reach it safely.

Get an itemized quote. If a tech quotes a full motor but the capacitor tested fine and vice versa, ask for the meter readings before approving.

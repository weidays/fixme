## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a lettered Trane fault code — most Trane air conditioners don't flash a specific code for this at the condenser. Instead you notice the fan blade on top of your outdoor unit (the condenser) sitting still while the system is calling for cooling.

The outdoor fan pulls air across the condenser coil to release the heat your system removed from indoors. If that fan stops, the coil can't shed heat, pressures climb, and the compressor is at real risk. Some Trane units will trip a high-pressure switch and shut the compressor down as protection; others may keep the compressor running until it overheats and locks out.

Because a stalled fan can quickly cause expensive compressor damage, this is a **pro-level** problem. Do the safe checks below, then get it repaired promptly.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The most common cause by far. The capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start spinning. When it weakens or fails, the fan hums, buzzes, or sits dead while the compressor may still run.
2. **Burned-out condenser fan motor** — Bearings seize or windings fail with age and heat. The motor won't turn even with a good capacitor.
3. **Contactor not pulling in** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit. Pitted, stuck, or non-energizing contacts can starve the fan (and often the compressor too).
4. **Tripped high-pressure or safety switch** — Some units cut the outdoor components as protection after a fault, dirty coil, or overcharge.
5. **Loose, corroded, or chewed wiring** — Rodents, vibration, and moisture can break the fan's power or ground connection.
6. **Obstructed or damaged fan blade** — Debris, ice, or a bent blade physically jamming the motor.
7. **Control board or low-voltage fault** — On variable-speed XV models with ECM motors, a board or module fault can prevent fan operation.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop at anything requiring you to open the electrical panel of the condenser.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the indoor coil to freeze and back up the whole system; replace a dirty filter.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor condenser breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unobstructed indoors.
- **Visible debris:** Look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest jamming the fan blade — clear obvious debris **only with the disconnect pulled and power off**.
- **Ice:** If the outdoor unit or indoor coil is iced over, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw before running again.

Do **not** open the condenser's electrical access panel, test capacitors, or spin the blade with power on. Capacitors store a dangerous charge even when the unit is off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A quote should reflect a logical process, not a guess:

1. **Visual and mechanical check** — Confirms the blade turns freely by hand (power off) and looks for burned wires, ants in the contactor, or rodent damage.
2. **Voltage at the contactor** — Verifies 240V line power and 24V control signal are reaching the outdoor unit when cooling is called.
3. **Capacitor test** — Measures the run capacitor's microfarad (µF) rating against the data-plate spec. A weak cap is the number-one culprit and a cheap fix.
4. **Motor test** — Checks the fan motor windings for continuity and shorts, and confirms it isn't drawing excessive amps or seized.
5. **Contactor test** — Inspects contacts for pitting and confirms the coil energizes.
6. **Pressure and safety-switch check** — Confirms a high-pressure switch hasn't tripped due to a dirty coil, overcharge, or airflow problem.
7. **Control diagnostics (XV/variable-speed)** — On communicating systems, the tech reads fault data from the board or module.

If a tech quotes a motor replacement without first testing the capacitor, ask why — the capacitor is far more likely and far cheaper.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan sits still | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off to protect compressor | Test and replace capacitor |
| Fan and compressor both dead, thermostat calling | Contactor not energizing or no 24V | Check breakers/disconnect | Test contactor and control voltage |
| Fan hums but won't turn, hot motor | Bad capacitor or seizing motor | Shut off, don't force blade | Test cap, then replace motor if needed |
| Fan blade physically jammed | Debris or bent blade | Clear visible debris, power off | Straighten/replace blade, check motor |
| Whole unit dead after storm | Tripped breaker or lightning damage | Reset breaker once | Inspect board, wiring, components |
| Ice on coil, fan issues | Airflow/refrigerant problem | Thaw with fan ON | Diagnose charge and airflow |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices including parts and labor; your area and model may vary.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $350 – $650 (more for ECM/variable-speed motors on XV units)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Wiring repair (rodent/corrosion):** $150 – $400
- **High-pressure switch or control board:** $250 – $700+ depending on part
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80 – $180, often applied toward the repair

The good news: the most common cause — a capacitor — is also among the cheapest. Running a unit with a stalled fan risks a compressor failure that can cost $1,500–$3,000+, so don't delay.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a blink code — it's a symptom. On a Trane air conditioner, the **outdoor unit (condenser)** contains a fan that pulls air across the coil to dump the heat your system pulled out of the house. When that fan stops turning, the condenser can't reject heat, pressures climb fast, and the compressor is at serious risk.

On single-stage units (XR13, XR14, XR16 and older Weathertron models) there is usually no fault light for this — you simply hear the compressor and find a still fan blade. On variable-speed and communicating models (XV18, XV20i) the control board may log a fan or motor fault and report it through the thermostat or the outdoor board LED, so behavior varies by model.

**Do not keep the system running.** If the compressor is humming or running while the fan sits still, shut the thermostat off now to protect the compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed dual run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. A bulged, leaking, or dead capacitor leaves the motor humming or dead.
2. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, ice, or a warped blade physically blocking rotation.
3. **Bad condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings or a burned winding. The motor may be hot, silent, or spin freely by hand but not power up.
4. **Failed or pitted contactor** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit. Burned contacts can cut power to the fan.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse / disconnect** — Loss of high-voltage power to the whole condenser.
6. **Control board or relay fault (variable-speed models)** — On XV18/XV20i communicating systems, the ECM fan or its driver board can fault out and refuse to run.
7. **Loose or corroded wiring** — At the motor, capacitor, or contactor terminals.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Never open the outdoor electrical panel to poke at the capacitor or contactor — the capacitor stores a lethal charge even with power off.**

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. If battery-powered, replace the batteries.
- **Indoor filter:** A filthy filter can cause airflow faults and freeze-ups that stop the outdoor unit. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box on the wall near the condenser should be fully seated. Reseat it if loose.
- **Visible debris (power OFF):** With the breaker and disconnect both OFF, look through the top grille for obvious sticks or leaves and clear them by hand. Do not force the blade or remove panels.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the outdoor coil or lines, turn the system off and let it thaw — running it frozen won't help and often points to airflow or refrigerant issues.
- **Condensate:** Confirm the indoor drain isn't backed up and tripping a float switch that shuts the system down.

If none of these resolve it, this is a **pro-level repair**.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify power** at the disconnect and contactor with a meter — confirming 240V is reaching the condenser.
2. **Test the capacitor** with a capacitance meter against the microfarad (MFD) rating printed on it. A reading well below spec confirms replacement.
3. **Push-start test:** With the system on, a tech may briefly nudge the fan blade with an insulated tool. If it then spins on its own, the capacitor is the culprit; if not, the motor is suspect.
4. **Check the contactor** for pitted or welded contacts and confirm the 24V coil pulls it in.
5. **Test the fan motor** for continuity, resistance, and shorted windings; check bearings for drag.
6. **Inspect wiring** for loose, corroded, or heat-damaged connections.
7. **On XV18/XV20i units,** read board fault codes and test the ECM motor and driver module before condemning parts.

A good tech confirms the capacitor with a meter rather than replacing it blindly — ask to see the reading.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan won't spin | Failed dual run capacitor | Shut system off; do not open panel | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Clear visible debris (power off) | Replace blade or motor if bent |
| Fan silent, no hum, unit dead | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once; reseat disconnect | Test power path, replace contactor |
| Fan spins by hand but won't power | Bad motor or capacitor | None — call a pro | Test motor windings; replace |
| Ice on coil, fan stopped | Airflow / refrigerant issue | Turn off, replace filter, let thaw | Diagnose airflow or charge |
| Thermostat fault code (XV models) | Board/ECM fault | Note code; power cycle once | Read code, test board & ECM |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Prices vary by region and model.

- **Dual run capacitor:** $150 – $300
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300 – $650
- **Fan blade:** $120 – $300
- **Breaker or disconnect repair:** $150 – $400
- **Control board / ECM driver (XV18/XV20i):** $400 – $900+ — get a second opinion before authorizing, as communicating boards are expensive.
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180, often credited toward the repair.

If a tech quotes a full motor when the capacitor tested bad, ask them to justify it — capacitors are cheap and often the real fix.

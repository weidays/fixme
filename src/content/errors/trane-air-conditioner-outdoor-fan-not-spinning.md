## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered Trane diagnostic code — it's a physical symptom. Your indoor system may still call for cooling, the compressor may even hum, but the fan on top of the outdoor condenser unit sits still. That fan's job is to pull air across the condenser coil to dump heat outside. When it stops, the system can't cool and internal pressures climb fast.

This is a **pro-level** problem. The most common causes involve a capacitor that stores a dangerous charge even with power off, or a motor and contactor that require electrical testing. There are only a few safe checks a homeowner should attempt before calling a technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. When it weakens or fails, the motor hums but won't spin, or won't start at all.
2. **Burned or stuck contactor** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit. Pitted, welded, or non-pulling contacts can leave the fan (and compressor) dead.
3. **Failed condenser fan motor** — Bearings seize or windings burn out with age and heat. If the capacitor tests good but the motor won't run, the motor itself is the culprit.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — No power to the outdoor unit means no fan.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, or ice can physically block the blade.
6. **Overheated compressor / high-pressure lockout** — On some Trane models, protective controls cut the outdoor unit when it senses a fault, which can present as a dead fan.
7. **Bad control board or thermostat wiring** — Less common, but a failed board or a broken 24V wire can stop the cooling call from reaching the outdoor unit. This varies by model and board revision.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Everything else involves stored electrical energy or refrigerant — leave those to a pro.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the indoor coil to freeze and shut cooling down. Replace it if it's dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician — repeated trips signal a real fault.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the pull-out disconnect box beside the condenser is fully seated.
- **Visible obstructions:** With the unit off at the breaker, look through the top grille for sticks or leaves jamming the blade. Clear only what you can reach without opening panels.
- **Condensate drain:** A full condensate pan with a safety float switch can shut the system down. Check that the drain line isn't clogged.
- **Vents:** Confirm supply and return vents inside are open and unblocked.

Do **not** open the electrical panel on the outdoor unit, discharge the capacitor, or spin the fan blade by hand with power on.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A quote should reflect these steps. Here's what a competent Trane tech does:

1. Confirms the cooling call reaches the outdoor unit by measuring 24V at the contactor coil.
2. Checks incoming line voltage at the disconnect and contactor.
3. **Safely discharges and tests the run capacitor** with a meter, comparing readings to the rated microfarads printed on it.
4. Tests the contactor — checks for pull-in, pitted contacts, and voltage across the load side.
5. If capacitor and contactor are good, tests the fan motor windings for resistance and continuity, and checks for a seized bearing.
6. Verifies system pressures with gauges to rule out a high-pressure lockout or refrigerant issue.
7. Inspects the control board and low-voltage wiring on models where the board manages the fan.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | Shut off at breaker; don't touch capacitor | Discharge & replace capacitor, verify mfd rating |
| Outdoor unit fully dead, indoor runs | Contactor or lost power | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test/replace contactor, trace 24V circuit |
| Fan hums then stops, gets hot | Weak capacitor or failing motor | Shut down to prevent damage | Test capacitor & motor windings |
| Blade physically stuck | Debris or seized bearing | Power off, clear visible debris | Replace motor if bearing seized |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted motor or wiring fault | Stop resetting; leave off | Locate short, replace faulty component |
| Ice on unit, no airflow | Frozen coil / airflow fault | Replace filter, turn to FAN only to thaw | Diagnose airflow or refrigerant cause |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Regional rates and unit age affect the total.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $350 – $650 (more for ECM or hard-to-source Trane-specific motors)
- **Control board replacement:** $350 – $700, varies by model and board revision
- **Clearing debris / minor fix:** $0 DIY if you can reach it safely, otherwise the service-call fee

Because a failed fan lets pressures and compressor heat climb, don't keep running the unit hoping it recovers — a $200 capacitor fix is far cheaper than a compressor.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a blink code — it's a symptom you can see. On a Trane split-system air conditioner, the outdoor unit (the condenser) contains a fan that pulls air across the condenser coil to dump heat outside. When that fan stops, the compressor loses its ability to shed heat and will quickly overheat.

You may notice the outdoor unit is humming, buzzing, or dead silent while the indoor blower still runs and the thermostat still calls for cooling. Some Trane units with communicating controls will log a fault; most conventional single-stage units give no code at all — the stopped fan is the only clue.

This is a **pro-level** repair. The most common causes involve capacitors and wiring that carry line voltage (208–240V), which can hold a lethal charge even with power off.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common. The dual-run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it fails, the fan often hums but won't turn, or won't move at all.
2. **Seized or failed condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings or a burned winding. Sometimes the motor spins freely by hand when off but won't start under load.
3. **Contactor not pulling in** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted, stuck, or a bad 24V coil means no power reaches the fan.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown high-voltage fuse** — Loss of power to the whole condenser. The indoor unit may still run on a separate circuit.
5. **Overheated compressor lockout** — If the unit tripped on an internal overload, the whole outdoor unit may pause, including the fan.
6. **Debris, ice, or a physically jammed blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade blocking rotation.
7. **Bad control board or low-voltage wiring fault** — Less common; more likely on communicating XV-series units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks you should do yourself. **Do not open the condenser access panel** — capacitors store dangerous voltage.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to Cool and drop the setpoint several degrees below room temperature to confirm it's actually calling.
- **Air filter:** A clogged indoor filter can cause the system to short-cycle or trip on pressure. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) breaker and the separate outdoor AC breaker in your main panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — that's a fault, not a fluke.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box on the wall near the condenser should be fully seated.
- **Visible debris:** With power off at the breaker, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or ice on the blade. Never reach in to spin the fan while power is on.
- **Batteries:** If you have a battery-powered thermostat that's blank or flickering, replace the batteries.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, **turn the system off** to protect the compressor and call an HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work in roughly this order — use it to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the call** — Verify 24V is reaching the contactor coil when the thermostat calls for cooling.
2. **Check line voltage** — Confirm 208–240V is present at the disconnect and contactor.
3. **Test the capacitor** — With power off and the capacitor safely discharged, they measure microfarads (µF) against the rating printed on the can. This is the single most common find.
4. **Test the motor** — Check windings for continuity and ground, and spin the blade by hand (power off) to feel for bearing drag or seizure.
5. **Inspect the contactor** — Look for pitted contacts and test the coil.
6. **Check for overload lockout** — On some units, they'll verify the compressor isn't tripped on thermal overload.

A good diagnosis takes 20–40 minutes. Be wary of a quote to replace the whole motor without a capacitor test first — the capacitor is cheaper and fails far more often.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — do not open panel | Discharge and replace capacitor |
| Fan dead silent, compressor also off | Contactor not engaging or power loss | Check breaker/disconnect | Test coil, replace contactor |
| Fan spins by hand but won't start | Seized bearing or weak capacitor | None | Test capacitor, replace motor if needed |
| Whole outdoor unit off, indoor runs | Tripped outdoor breaker/fuse | Reset breaker once | Find cause of overcurrent |
| Fan blade physically jammed | Debris or bent blade | Kill power, remove visible debris | Replace bent blade, realign |
| Unit runs then shuts off in minutes | Compressor overheating / lockout | Turn off, stop running it | Diagnose airflow and pressures |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $350 – $700 (higher for ECM or communicating motors)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Control board (communicating XV units):** $400 – $900

The capacitor is the cheapest and most common fix. If a tech insists on a full motor swap, ask whether the capacitor was tested first — replacing a good motor because of a $25 capacitor is a common overcharge.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is not a blink code — it's a physical symptom on your Trane condenser (the outdoor unit). The compressor may still be running or trying to run, but the large fan on top of the unit that blows heat out of the coil is stopped.

This matters because the condenser fan is what rejects the heat your system pulls from inside your home. Without it, refrigerant pressure and compressor temperature climb fast. Most Trane units will either trip a high-pressure switch, overheat the compressor, or eventually lock out — and continued operation risks a compressor failure that can cost **$1,500–$3,000+** to repair.

If you can hear a hum or the compressor running but the fan blade is dead, **turn the system off at the thermostat and outdoor disconnect** and keep reading.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual run capacitor supplies the starting torque for the fan motor (and often the compressor). When it weakens, the motor hums but won't spin, or won't start at all.
2. **Debris or obstruction jamming the blade** — Sticks, a bird's nest, ice, or a bent blade physically stopping rotation. If the fan turns freely by hand with power off, this is ruled out.
3. **Failed condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, an open winding, or a burnt-out motor. Often smells hot or shows scorched wiring.
4. **Contactor not pulling in** — The contactor is the relay that sends 240V to the fan and compressor. A pitted or stuck contactor can leave the fan without power.
5. **Control board or ECM fault (variable-speed Trane units only)** — On XV18, XV20i and communicating systems, the fan is an ECM driven by the board. A board or module fault, not a capacitor, may be the culprit here.
6. **Tripped high-pressure or float safety upstream** — On some configurations a safety trip can interrupt the outdoor call, though this usually stops the compressor too.
7. **Low-voltage / 24V control issue** — A blown transformer or open thermostat wire can prevent the contactor from energizing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical panel of the condenser or touch the capacitor** — it holds a dangerous charge.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the system to short-cycle or trip safeties. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor furnace/air handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker in your main panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated.
- **Visible debris (power OFF):** With the system OFF at the thermostat and the outdoor disconnect pulled, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest against the blade. Remove obvious debris.
- **Spin test (power OFF only):** With power removed, the fan blade should turn freely by hand. If it's seized, note it for the tech.
- **Condensate:** Check the indoor drain pan and float switch — a full pan can shut the system down.

If the fan hums, is seized, won't start after one breaker reset, or you smell something hot — **stop and call a licensed HVAC tech.**

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will work in this order, and their invoice should reflect it:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling** and check 24V is reaching the contactor.
2. **Measure the run capacitor** with a meter (microfarads). A cap rated 5 µF reading 3 µF or lower is failed. This is the fastest, most common find.
3. **Check the contactor** for pull-in, pitted contacts, and correct voltage out to the fan motor.
4. **Test the fan motor** — verify 240V at the motor leads, check windings for continuity and shorts, and confirm the blade isn't seized.
5. **On variable-speed/communicating Trane units,** they'll pull fault codes from the board or thermostat and test the ECM motor/module rather than a capacitor.
6. **Check pressures** if the compressor has been running without the fan, to assess whether the compressor was stressed.

Ask the tech to show you the capacitor reading or the failed component. A capacitor swap should not be billed like a motor replacement.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | Turn off system; don't force blade | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan starts if you nudge the blade | Weak capacitor or worn motor | Turn off; note for tech | Replace capacitor and/or motor |
| Blade seized, won't turn by hand | Debris jam or bad bearings | Remove visible debris (power off) | Clear jam or replace motor |
| No hum, no fan, compressor also silent | Contactor, breaker, or 24V loss | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test contactor, transformer, wiring |
| Fan dead on XV/XL variable unit | ECM motor or board fault | Note thermostat error codes | Diagnose board/ECM, replace module |
| Fan runs then trips off quickly | High-pressure safety, dirty coil | Rinse debris from coil exterior | Check pressures, clean coil, find root cause |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Rates vary by region and by whether you're on a service plan.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400 (part is cheap; labor and diagnosis drive the cost)
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor (standard PSC):** $350–$650
- **ECM fan motor / module (variable-speed Trane XV/XL):** $500–$900
- **Control board (communicating units):** $400–$900
- **Debris removal / minor cleaning:** often folded into the service call, $80–$150

DIY note: a capacitor is inexpensive as a part (~$15–$40), but **discharging and replacing it safely requires training** — the stored charge can injure or kill. We list the part for informed homeowners, not as a recommendation to open a live condenser.

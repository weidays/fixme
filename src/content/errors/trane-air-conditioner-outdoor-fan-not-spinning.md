## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a stored fault code — most Trane air conditioners don't flash a dedicated blink code for this. The fan you see on top of the outdoor condenser unit is supposed to spin whenever the compressor runs, pulling air across the coil to reject heat.

When that fan stays still while the compressor hums (or while the unit is calling for cooling), heat can't escape the condenser. The compressor quickly overheats and either trips on its internal overload or fails on high refrigerant pressure. This is why the condition is rated **pro**: most causes are inside a 240-volt cabinet with a charged capacitor, and running the system this way risks the most expensive part in the whole AC.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The single most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor its starting "kick." When it weakens, the fan won't start on its own (sometimes it starts only if nudged) or won't start at all.
2. **Bad condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, a burned winding, or an open thermal overload. Often accompanied by a humming or grinding noise, or a hot, seized motor.
3. **Contactor not pulling in or welded** — The contactor is the relay that sends 240V to the fan and compressor. A pitted, stuck, or non-engaging contactor cuts power to the fan.
4. **Lost 24V control signal / thermostat problem** — If the outdoor unit never gets the call to run, neither fan nor compressor starts.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse / pulled disconnect** — Loss of power to the outdoor unit.
6. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — A stick, ice, or bird nest physically blocking the blade.
7. **Control board fault (variable-speed / communicating models)** — On XV-series and communicating units, the fan may be board-driven; a board or module fault can stop it. Behavior here varies by model.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Never open the outdoor unit's electrical panel** — the capacitor can hold a dangerous charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and lower the setpoint several degrees to confirm it's actually calling for cooling. Replace thermostat batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause other cooling faults; swap it if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-block near the outdoor unit should be fully seated. Push it in firmly if it's loose.
- **Visible debris:** With the unit **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird nest jamming the blade. Clear only what you can reach from outside without removing panels.
- **Supply vents:** Make sure interior vents are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan can trip a float switch and stop the call for cooling; check that the drain isn't backed up.

If the fan still won't spin after these, shut the system off at the thermostat and call a licensed technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence you can sanity-check against their quote:

1. **Confirm the call:** Verifies 24V is present at the outdoor unit when the thermostat calls for cooling.
2. **Check line power:** Confirms 240V reaches the contactor and that the disconnect and breaker are good.
3. **Test the contactor:** Checks that the coil pulls in and that contacts pass voltage without excessive pitting.
4. **Test the capacitor:** Uses a meter to measure microfarads against the printed rating — a weak fan side confirms a bad capacitor. This is the most common finding.
5. **Test the motor:** Checks windings for resistance/continuity, spins the blade by hand for bearing drag, and verifies the motor isn't tripped on internal overload.
6. **Inspect the board (communicating/variable-speed models):** On XV units, checks the fan module or control board and any fault codes displayed at the indoor board or thermostat.

Expect a diagnostic fee of roughly **$85–$175**, often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan sits still | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't spin it by hand to run it | Test and replace capacitor |
| Fan starts only if pushed | Weak capacitor | Shut off, call pro | Replace capacitor |
| Buzzing/clicking, nothing runs | Contactor not engaging | Check breaker/disconnect once | Test and replace contactor |
| Fan hot, seized, or grinding | Failed fan motor/bearings | Shut off to prevent damage | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Nothing at outdoor unit at all | Tripped breaker / lost 24V | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Trace 24V, fuses, wiring |
| Blade physically stuck | Debris jamming blade | Power off, clear visible debris | Clear, inspect blade/bearings |
| Fault on communicating model | Board / fan module fault | Note any displayed code | Diagnose and replace board/module |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$300
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $350–$650 (higher for OEM variable-speed motors)
- **24V wiring / low-voltage repair:** $150–$400
- **Clearing debris / minor service:** $85–$175 (often just the diagnostic fee)
- **Control board / fan module (communicating XV units):** $500–$900+, varies widely by model and part availability

Capacitor and contactor jobs are quick; motor and board replacements drive the higher end. If your unit is old and the motor and capacitor are both failing, ask whether a repair still makes sense versus replacement.

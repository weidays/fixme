## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is not a numbered Trane fault code — it's a physical symptom. On a running Trane air conditioner, the outdoor unit (the condenser) should have its large top fan spinning whenever the compressor is running. That fan pulls air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system pulled out of your house.

When the compressor hums or the unit gets warm but the top fan sits still, the AC cannot dump heat. Pressures climb fast, and most Trane units will either trip a high-pressure switch, thermally protect the compressor, or simply fail to cool. **Continuing to run the system in this state risks compressor damage — the single most expensive part in the whole system.**

This is a pro-level repair because the most common causes involve capacitors that hold a dangerous electrical charge and 240V line-voltage components.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common. The fan motor's capacitor loses capacitance and can't provide starting torque. The fan won't start on its own but may spin if nudged. Often a swollen or leaking dual run capacitor.
2. **Failed condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings (seized) or a burned-out winding. Sometimes preceded by grinding, humming, or a hot-metal smell.
3. **Bad contactor** — The relay that sends 240V to the fan and compressor has pitted or welded contacts, or its 24V coil failed, so power never reaches the fan.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse** — Partial power loss can leave the compressor running on one leg while the fan sits dead, or vice versa.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Control board or wiring fault** — On single-stage units, a loose wire or corroded connection. On variable-speed models (XV18/XV20i), a fault in the ECM motor or its dedicated inverter/control board.
7. **Low-voltage / thermostat call issue** — Less common when the compressor is clearly trying to run.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do — everything past this involves stored electrical charge or line voltage.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause freeze-ups that mimic outdoor problems. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker in your main panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Indoor vents:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full condensate pan or a tripped float switch, which can shut the system down.
- **Look, don't touch:** From a few feet away, visually check the outdoor fan for obvious debris (leaves, sticks) sitting on top of the grille. **Do not reach into the unit, do not push the fan with your hand, and do not open the electrical panel.** Turn the unit off at the thermostat first if you see debris on top of the grille you can lift off safely.

If the fan still won't spin, turn the AC off at the thermostat and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Kills power** at the outdoor disconnect and confirms it's dead.
2. **Safely discharges and tests the capacitor** with a meter, comparing the reading to the printed microfarad (µF) rating. A weak or dead capacitor is the most common finding.
3. **Checks the contactor** for pitted contacts and confirms 24V is reaching the coil and 240V is passing through.
4. **Tests the fan motor** — checks windings for continuity/shorts, spins the shaft by hand to feel for seized bearings, and verifies it gets voltage.
5. **Inspects wiring** for loose, burned, or corroded connections.
6. **On variable-speed units**, reads any stored ECM/inverter faults and tests the specific control board — this is model-specific and requires Trane documentation.

A good tech replaces the cheapest likely cause first (capacitor) rather than jumping to a motor.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan won't start | Failed run capacitor | Turn AC off to protect compressor | Discharge & test capacitor, replace |
| Fan spins if nudged, then keeps going | Weak capacitor | Turn AC off; do not touch fan | Confirm µF reading, replace capacitor |
| Nothing runs outside, no hum | Contactor or blown fuse/breaker | Reset breaker once | Test contactor coil & fuses, replace |
| Grinding/humming then silence | Seized fan motor bearings | Turn AC off | Test & replace condenser fan motor |
| Burning smell from unit | Overheated motor windings | Shut off immediately | Replace fan motor, inspect wiring |
| Debris on top of fan | Jammed blade | Remove loose debris with unit off | Inspect for bent blade/motor strain |
| Fan dead on variable-speed unit | ECM motor or inverter board | Turn off; note model number | Read faults, test board/ECM motor |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices including labor; your area and unit age will vary.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward repair)
- **Run capacitor:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor:** $150 – $300
- **Condenser fan motor (single-stage):** $350 – $600+
- **ECM/variable-speed fan motor or control board:** $500 – $1,200+ (model-specific; get the part quote in writing)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $350

If your unit is 12+ years old and the failure is a motor or board on top of other worn parts, ask whether repair or replacement makes more financial sense.

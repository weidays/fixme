## What this code means

Most Trane air conditioners don't display a dedicated "outdoor fan not spinning" code — this is a **physical symptom** you observe, not a flashing fault. The outdoor unit (condenser) has one large fan on top that pulls air across the coil to reject heat. When it won't turn, the compressor loses its only way to shed heat and will quickly overheat, trip on internal overload, or lock out.

Newer Trane variable-speed units (XV20i, XV18) with communicating controls may log a fault to the thermostat or control board when the ECM fan motor fails to spin, but single-stage XR and XL units usually give you no code at all — just a running compressor with a dead fan, warm air indoors, and rising outdoor unit temperature.

**Because the compressor can be damaged within minutes of the fan stopping, treat this as a pro-level repair and stop running the system until it's fixed.**

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it weakens, the fan hums but won't start, or starts only if you nudge it.
2. **Seized or burned-out fan motor** — Worn bearings, overheating, or age can kill the motor outright. It won't spin freely by hand and may smell burnt.
3. **Failed contactor** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts can prevent the fan from getting power.
4. **Debris jamming the blade** — Sticks, grass, or ice can physically block the fan. Sometimes the blade set screw loosens and the blade slips on the shaft.
5. **Bad capacitor connections or wiring** — Corroded terminals, chewed wires (rodents), or a loose spade connector cut power to the fan.
6. **Control board or ECM module fault** (variable-speed models) — On communicating Trane units, a failed fan control module or board can stop the ECM motor.
7. **Tripped high-pressure or motor overload** — A protective switch may cut the fan or whole unit after an overheat event.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser, touch the capacitor, or bypass any switch — a capacitor can hold a lethal charge even with power off.**

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Try lowering it a few degrees to force a call for cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor condenser breaker (and the outdoor disconnect box). Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter can cause the system to overheat and shut down protective components. Replace it if it's dirty.
- **Visible debris:** From outside the unit, look through the top grille for obvious sticks, leaves, or a bird's nest jamming the blade. Do not reach inside — turn power off at the disconnect first, and if you can't clear it easily by hand from the top with power off, leave it.
- **Ice:** If the outdoor unit or refrigerant lines are iced up, shut the system off and let it thaw. Run the indoor fan (fan ON) to speed thawing.
- **Condensate:** A backed-up condensate drain can trip a safety float switch and shut the whole system down. Check that the indoor drain line isn't clogged.

If the fan still won't spin after these steps, call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling** and verify 24V is reaching the outdoor unit.
2. **Measure the run capacitor** with a meter — a healthy dual cap reads within about 6% of its printed µF rating. A weak or dead fan section is the smoking gun.
3. **Test the fan motor** — check windings for continuity/resistance, spin the blade by hand for bearing drag, and look/smell for burnt windings.
4. **Inspect the contactor** — check for pitted or welded contacts and confirm the coil pulls in when energized.
5. **Check wiring and connections** for corrosion, loose spades, or rodent damage.
6. **On variable-speed units,** read fault codes at the thermostat/board and test the ECM fan module.
7. **Verify refrigerant pressures and compressor status** — since a stopped fan may have already stressed the compressor, a good tech will confirm it still runs safely.

A capacitor swap is a 20–30 minute job; a motor replacement takes longer and requires matching motor HP, voltage, RPM, and rotation.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin; starts if nudged | Failed run capacitor | Shut unit off; don't nudge repeatedly | Test and replace dual run capacitor |
| Fan totally silent, compressor also dead | Failed contactor or no power | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test/replace contactor, check 24V |
| Fan won't turn by hand, burnt smell | Seized/burned fan motor | Shut off; do not run | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Blade physically jammed | Debris or loose blade | Power off, clear obvious debris from top | Clear jam, tighten set screw, inspect motor |
| Ice on unit, fan stopped | Airflow/refrigerant issue | Shut off, thaw with indoor fan on | Diagnose low refrigerant or airflow fault |
| Fault code on smart thermostat | ECM/board fault (variable-speed) | Note the code | Test ECM module or control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $350 – $650 (higher for OEM ECM motors on variable-speed units, sometimes $700+)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Wiring/connection repair:** $100 – $300
- **Control board / ECM module (communicating units):** $400 – $900+
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75 – $200 (often credited toward the repair)

DIY capacitor swaps are possible for experienced homeowners with a meter and proper discharge tools, but the shock hazard is real — most people should leave it to a pro. If the stopped fan already damaged the compressor, costs rise sharply, which is why fast shutdown matters.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a blink code — it's a symptom. On a Trane air conditioner, the outdoor unit (the condenser) contains a fan that pulls air across the coil to reject heat. When this fan stops, the compressor may still run, but the system can't shed heat and refrigerant pressure climbs quickly.

Most Trane condensers do not display a numbered fault for this. Instead you'll notice warm air indoors, a humming or clicking outdoor unit, and a fan blade that's stationary or barely moving. Variable-speed communicating models (XV series) may log a related fault at the indoor board, but the underlying causes are the same.

**This is a pro-level problem.** The most common failures — capacitor, motor, contactor — all involve high-voltage components. Continuing to run the unit risks a far more expensive compressor failure.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor the torque it needs to start. When it weakens or fails, the fan hums but won't spin, or won't start at all. Often the same dual capacitor also serves the compressor.
2. **Seized or worn fan motor** — Bearings dry out and lock up over years of heat and weather. A motor that gets hot, cuts out, then restarts when cool is a classic sign of a failing motor.
3. **Failed or stuck contactor** — The contactor is the relay that sends 240V to the fan and compressor. Pitted or welded contacts, or a dead 24V coil, can starve the fan of power.
4. **Debris jamming the blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade can physically block rotation.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse to the outdoor unit** — The disconnect or breaker may be off, or the low-voltage fuse on the control board may be blown.
6. **Control board or wiring fault** — Less common; loose or corroded wires, or a failed board on communicating XV systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical access panel on the outdoor unit** — the capacitor holds a lethal charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A badly clogged filter can cause the whole system to behave abnormally. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breaker / disconnect:** Check the breaker in your main panel labeled for the AC or condenser. If tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Indoor vents:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked.
- **Visible debris around the fan:** With the unit powered OFF at the breaker, look through the top grille for leaves or sticks. Do not reach in to spin the blade or clear jams with power on.
- **Condensate:** Check that the drain isn't overflowing, which can trip a float switch on some systems and shut the unit down.

If the fan still won't run after these checks, turn the system off at the thermostat and call an HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work through the high-voltage side of the outdoor unit in a predictable order. Knowing this helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify power** at the disconnect and confirm 240V is reaching the unit.
2. **Discharge and test the capacitor** with a meter — checking microfarad rating against the label. A weak reading is a smoking gun.
3. **Test the fan motor** — checking windings for continuity and manually spinning the blade (power off) to feel for seized bearings.
4. **Inspect the contactor** for pitted contacts and test the 24V coil.
5. **Check wiring and connections** for corrosion, burn marks, or loose terminals.
6. **On communicating XV systems,** read board fault history and confirm the module isn't reporting a motor or control fault.

A good tech should show you the capacitor reading or the failed component before quoting a replacement.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but doesn't spin | Failed run capacitor | Shut off at thermostat | Discharge & replace capacitor |
| Fan starts then stops when hot | Failing fan motor | Turn system off | Test & replace motor |
| No hum, no compressor either | Contactor or power loss | Check breaker/disconnect once | Test contactor, coil, wiring |
| Blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Power off, look (don't reach in) | Clear debris, straighten/replace blade |
| Whole unit dead | Blown fuse / breaker / board | Reset breaker once | Replace fuse, diagnose board |
| Warm air, outdoor unit loud | Fan stalled, compressor running | Shut off to protect compressor | Full diagnosis before restart |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Regional rates and model complexity vary.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $350 – $650 (higher for variable-speed or hard-to-source Trane motors)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Low-voltage fuse / wiring repair:** $100 – $250
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Control board (communicating XV models):** $400 – $900+

DIY cost is effectively $0 for the safe checks above (thermostat, filter, breaker reset, clearing visible debris with power off). Anything inside the electrical panel should go to a pro because of the capacitor's stored charge.

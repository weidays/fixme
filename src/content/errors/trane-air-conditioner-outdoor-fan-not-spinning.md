## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a lettered Trane fault code — most residential Trane condensers don't flash a blink code for this. When you call for cooling, the outdoor unit (condenser) should power up and the top fan should spin to pull air across the coil while the compressor runs. If the compressor is running or humming but the fan blade sits still — or nothing on the outdoor unit runs at all — this page is for you.

A non-spinning outdoor fan matters because the fan removes heat from the refrigerant. Without it, head pressure and compressor temperature climb fast, which can trip internal overloads and, over time, kill the compressor. **Shut the system off if the compressor is running while the fan is dead.**

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run/start capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. When it weakens or fails, the fan won't start (or only starts if pushed). Many Trane units use a dual-run capacitor shared by the fan and compressor.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, water intrusion, or age. The motor may hum, get hot, or do nothing.
3. **Contactor not pulling in or welded** — The 24V contactor is the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit. Pitted or stuck contacts can stop the fan (and compressor) from getting power.
4. **Tripped breaker or disconnect / lost power** — The outdoor disconnect pulled, a tripped breaker, or a blown low-voltage fuse.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — A stick, ice, or bent blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Failed fan motor overload or capacitor overheating in extreme heat** — Motors with thermal protection may cut out and reset when cool.
7. **Control board or low-voltage fault** — On variable-speed/communicating Trane systems (XV series), a board or wiring issue can withhold the fan command.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for a homeowner. **Do not open the condenser electrical panel or touch the capacitor** — capacitors store a lethal charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A badly clogged filter can trip freeze/pressure protection that stops the outdoor unit. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check the AC breaker in your main panel. If tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop — call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser should be seated fully. If it was pulled, reseat it.
- **Look, don't touch:** From outside, look through the top grille for obvious debris, ice, or a bent blade. Clear leaves and grass from around the unit for airflow. Do not reach in.
- **Indoor vents & condensate:** Confirm supply vents are open and the condensate drain isn't backed up (a full pan float switch can shut the system down).

If the compressor is humming but the fan is dead, **turn the system off at the thermostat and breaker** and call a technician — running it further risks the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A pro will typically:

1. Verify 240V power at the disconnect and 24V control voltage at the contactor.
2. **Discharge and test the capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads (uF). A capacitor reading well below its rating gets replaced.
3. Check the contactor for pitted/welded contacts and confirm the coil pulls in.
4. Test the fan motor windings for continuity and shorts, and spin it by hand (power off) to feel for seized bearings.
5. On communicating XV/XL systems, read fault data at the thermostat or board and check the motor module.
6. Inspect wiring for burned terminals, chewed insulation, or loose connections.

Sanity check: a good tech should confirm the actual failed part with a meter before quoting a motor. Being told "you need a new motor" without a capacitor test is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan still | Failed capacitor or seized motor | Shut unit off at breaker | Test/replace capacitor or motor |
| Nothing on outdoor unit | Breaker, disconnect, or contactor | Reset breaker once, reseat disconnect | Test contactor & low-voltage circuit |
| Fan spins slowly then stops | Weak capacitor or failing bearings | Shut off, don't force it | Replace capacitor and/or motor |
| Fan blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Clear visible debris (power off) | Straighten/replace blade, check motor |
| Fan cuts out in extreme heat | Thermal overload / weak cap | Let cool, note pattern for tech | Replace capacitor, check motor amps |
| No fan on XV/communicating unit | Board or motor module fault | Note thermostat error messages | Read codes, test/replace module |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Regional rates vary widely.

- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$300
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $300–$700 (more for ECM/variable-speed motors)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150–$350
- **Low-voltage fuse or wiring repair:** $100–$300
- **Control board / motor module (XV series):** $400–$900+
- **Service diagnostic fee:** $80–$180 (often applied toward the repair)

DIY note: some experienced homeowners replace a matched capacitor, but only after fully discharging it — the stored charge can injure or kill. If you're unsure, this is a pro job.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a blink code — it's a symptom. On a Trane
air conditioner, the condenser fan sits on top of the outdoor unit and blows
air across the coil to dump heat the system pulled from your home. When it
stops, the compressor keeps running but has no way to reject that heat.

Within minutes the outdoor unit gets very hot, high-side pressure climbs, and
the compressor's internal overload will trip — or, worse, the compressor
overheats. That's why this is a **pro-severity** problem: running the AC with
a dead fan can turn a cheap part failure into a compressor replacement.

On variable-speed units (XV18, XV20i) the outdoor fan is driven by a
communicating control board and ECM motor, so a fan fault often shows as a
code on the indoor board or the ComfortLink thermostat rather than a plain
dead fan. The diagnosis on those models differs from single-stage units.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual capacitor
   starts both the fan and compressor. A weak or blown fan section leaves the
   motor humming or dead.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, water intrusion,
   or age can lock or kill the motor. It may spin freely by hand but not start,
   or not turn at all.
3. **Debris or ice jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade can
   physically stop the fan. Ice forming on the coil in certain fault conditions
   can also lock it.
4. **Failed contactor** — The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit can pit,
   stick, or fail open, cutting power to the fan and compressor together.
5. **Bad control board or ECM (variable-speed models)** — On XV-series units the
   board commands fan speed; a board or motor-module fault stops it.
6. **Loss of power to the outdoor unit** — Tripped breaker, blown disconnect fuse,
   or a chewed wire will kill the whole condenser.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not** open the electrical
panel on the condenser or touch the capacitor — it holds a lethal charge.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temp.
  For ComfortLink thermostats, check for any fault message on screen.
- **Breaker:** Look for a tripped breaker for the outdoor unit at your main
  panel. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Many Trane units have a pull-out disconnect box near
  the condenser. Make sure it's fully seated.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause coil freezing that stops the fan.
  Replace a dirty filter.
- **Visible debris:** With the system **off at the breaker**, look through the
  top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird nest against the blade. Don't reach
  into the unit while powered.
- **Vents:** Confirm indoor supply and return vents aren't blocked.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, it needs a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the steps helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm power** at the disconnect and contactor with a meter (208–240V).
2. **Test the run capacitor** with a capacitance meter against its rated µF.
   A reading well below the label value confirms a failed cap.
3. **Test the contactor** — checking for pulled-in coil and clean, conducting
   contacts when the thermostat calls for cooling.
4. **Bench/spin test the motor** — if the cap is good but the fan won't start,
   they'll check motor windings for continuity and shorts, and confirm the
   blade turns freely.
5. **On variable-speed units**, they'll pull codes from the board or thermostat
   and test the ECM module rather than a simple capacitor.
6. **Verify amp draw** after repair to confirm the motor runs within spec.

A good tech won't replace a motor before proving the capacitor and contactor
are good — the cap is a $15 part that fixes most no-spin calls.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't turn | Failed run capacitor | Turn off at breaker | Test and replace dual capacitor |
| Fan dead, no hum, compressor also silent | Contactor or power loss | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test contactor, wiring, and power |
| Fan won't start, spins freely by hand | Weak capacitor or motor | Turn off system | Test cap; replace motor if cap is good |
| Fan blade won't turn by hand | Seized motor bearings | Turn off system | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Blade jammed by debris | Sticks/leaves/nest | Remove visible debris (power off) | Clear jam, inspect blade balance |
| Fault message on ComfortLink screen | Board/ECM fault (XV models) | Note the code | Diagnose control board / ECM module |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$300. The part is cheap; you're paying
  for the diagnostic visit and safe handling.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor (single-stage):** $300–$650 installed, depending on
  motor size and whether the capacitor is replaced with it (recommended).
- **Clearing debris / minor jam:** $100–$200 for a service call.
- **Control board or ECM module (variable-speed XV units):** $500–$1,200+.
  Communicating parts and diagnostics push these higher.
- **Diagnostic service call alone:** $90–$180, often credited toward the repair.

If a shop quotes a full motor replacement without testing the capacitor first,
ask them to confirm the cap reading — it's the most common and cheapest fix.

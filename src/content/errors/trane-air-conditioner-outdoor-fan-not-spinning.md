## What this code means

On most Trane air conditioners, "outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a lettered fault code. The condenser fan — the large fan on top of your outdoor unit — should spin whenever the compressor is running to blow heat out of the coil. When it doesn't spin, the system can't reject heat, and the compressor quickly overheats.

Some newer Trane variable-speed and communicating systems (with a control board or a linked indoor board) may log a related fault such as an outdoor fan fault, low-airflow, or high-pressure lockout. **Exact codes and blink patterns vary by board and model**, so check the diagnostic label inside the outdoor unit's access panel rather than assuming.

This is rated **pro severity**: most causes involve electrical components inside a panel carrying line voltage and a capacitor that stores a dangerous charge even when power is off.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. Many Trane units use a dual run capacitor that serves both the fan and compressor. When the fan side weakens, the fan hums but won't spin (or needs a push). Never diagnose this yourself — capacitors hold a lethal charge.
2. **Bad condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, a burned winding, or a tripped internal thermal overload. The motor may hum, run hot, or do nothing.
3. **Failed or pitted contactor** — The contactor sends power to both the compressor and fan. Burned or stuck contacts can cut power to the fan circuit.
4. **Seized fan motor or obstructed blade** — Debris, ice, or bearing failure physically blocking the blade.
5. **Control board / ECM module fault (variable-speed units)** — On communicating or variable-speed Trane systems, a bad fan module or board can stop the fan even when everything else looks fine.
6. **Loss of low-voltage signal** — A wiring, transformer, or thermostat problem that never energizes the outdoor unit at all (compressor also won't run in that case).
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse at the disconnect** — If the whole outdoor unit is dead.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these only. Do **not** open the electrical panel on the outdoor unit — the capacitor can shock you even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Set to Cool and lower the setpoint several degrees below room temperature. Confirm it's calling for cooling.
- **Thermostat batteries:** Replace them if your thermostat uses them and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause airflow-related lockouts. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor air-handler/furnace breaker and the outdoor AC breaker in your main panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Confirm the pull-out disconnect box near the unit is fully seated (some get bumped loose).
- **Visible obstructions:** With the system **off**, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or ice against the fan blade. Clear loose debris only — don't force the blade.
- **Supply vents:** Make sure indoor registers aren't blocked, which can cause airflow faults.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, **turn the system off at the thermostat** to protect the compressor and call an HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech should:

1. Confirm the thermostat is calling and measure the 24V signal reaching the outdoor unit.
2. Safely **discharge and test the run capacitor** with a meter, comparing microfarad (µF) readings to the rating printed on it.
3. Inspect the **contactor** for pitting, welding, or a chattering coil, and verify line voltage passes through it.
4. Test the **fan motor** windings for continuity and shorts, and check for a tripped thermal overload or seized bearings.
5. On variable-speed/communicating Trane units, pull **fault codes** from the board and check the ECM fan module.
6. Verify **high-pressure/low-pressure switch** status — a fan failure often shows up alongside a high-pressure trip.

Ask them to show you the capacitor reading or the failed part. A $150–$350 capacitor swap should not be quoted as a full motor job.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan doesn't turn (or needs a push) | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't push the blade | Discharge & test capacitor, replace |
| Whole outdoor unit dead, no hum | Tripped breaker / loose disconnect | Reset breaker once; reseat disconnect | Test transformer, wiring, contactor |
| Fan hums, gets hot, then stops | Failing fan motor / thermal overload | Turn off to prevent burnout | Test windings, replace motor |
| Clicking but nothing energizes | Burned/stuck contactor | None (line voltage) | Test and replace contactor |
| Ice or debris jamming blade | Obstruction / iced coil | Turn off, clear loose debris | Find cause of icing, inspect motor |
| Fault code on communicating unit | Board / ECM fan module fault | Note the code, call pro | Read board codes, replace module/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $75 – $175
- **Run capacitor (single or dual):** $150 – $350 installed
- **Contactor:** $150 – $300 installed
- **Condenser fan motor (single-speed PSC):** $300 – $650+ installed
- **Variable-speed ECM fan motor or module:** $500 – $1,200+ (varies widely by model and parts availability)
- **Control board (communicating units):** $400 – $900+

Prices vary by region, unit age, and whether the part is under Trane's parts warranty (register your unit — many carry a 10-year limited warranty that covers the part but not labor).

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a lettered Trane fault code. On most Trane split systems the outdoor unit (the condenser) contains the compressor and a large fan that blows air across the coil to reject heat. When you hear the compressor humming or the unit energize but the top fan blade sits still — or the whole unit is silent — you have this problem.

This matters because the condenser fan is what keeps head pressure and compressor temperature in a safe range. **Running the compressor with a dead fan for more than a few minutes can burn out the compressor**, which is the single most expensive part in the system. Turn the system off at the thermostat until it's diagnosed.

On communicating Trane systems (XV / variable-speed platforms with a linked indoor board), a fan or motor fault may register a diagnostic code at the thermostat or air handler. On single-stage XR/XL units there is usually no code at all — you diagnose by observation and meter readings.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The most common cause by far. The dual-run capacitor gives the fan (and compressor) motor the jolt it needs to start and keep spinning. A bulged, leaking, or out-of-spec capacitor leaves the fan humming or dead.
2. **Seized or worn condenser fan motor** — Bad bearings, an internal winding fault, or a tripped internal thermal overload will keep the blade from turning. Sometimes it spins briefly then quits as it overheats.
3. **Failed or pitted contactor** — The contactor is the relay that sends line voltage to the outdoor unit. Burned or stuck contacts can prevent the fan (and compressor) from getting power.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse / disconnect** — A tripped double-pole breaker or a pulled/blown outdoor disconnect leaves the whole condenser dead.
5. **Debris jamming the blade** — Sticks, leaves, or ice from a prior fault can physically block the fan.
6. **Control-board or low-voltage fault** — On communicating Trane systems a board fault, a lost 24V signal, or a wiring problem can stop the fan command. Less common but real.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the electrical panel on the condenser or touch the capacitor — it can hold a lethal charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. If it's battery-powered, replace the batteries.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter can cause the indoor coil to freeze and trip the system into odd behavior. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (often a double-pole). Reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box on the wall near the condenser should be fully seated. If someone pulled it for service, push it back in.
- **Visible debris:** With the unit **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest jammed against the blade. Only clear what you can reach from outside the grille — don't remove panels.
- **Condensate:** Check that the indoor drain isn't overflowing and tripping a float switch, which can shut the system down.
- **Ice:** If the outdoor unit or refrigerant lines are iced over, shut the system off and let it thaw fully before restarting.

If none of these restore the fan, it's a line-voltage repair — call an HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheapest to most expensive, and you can sanity-check the quote against these steps:

1. **Confirms 240V power** at the disconnect and contactor line side.
2. **Tests the contactor** — checks the 24V coil and looks for pitted or welded contacts.
3. **Measures the run capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads (µF). Anything more than about 6–10% off is failed.
4. **Manually spin-tests the fan blade** (power off) to feel for a seized bearing.
5. **Powers the motor directly / measures amperage and winding resistance** to isolate a bad motor from a bad capacitor.
6. **Checks low-voltage signal and board** on communicating units, reading any stored diagnostic codes at the thermostat or air handler.

Expect a diagnostic/service-call fee of roughly **$90–$180** that's often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan blade won't start (or spins if you flick it) | Failed run capacitor | Turn off system; don't open the unit | Test & replace dual-run capacitor |
| Fan spins a few seconds then stalls, motor hot | Failing motor / weak capacitor | Shut off to protect compressor | Replace motor and/or capacitor |
| Whole outdoor unit silent, no hum | Tripped breaker, pulled disconnect, bad contactor | Reset breaker once; reseat disconnect | Test contactor & line voltage |
| Fan physically won't budge, grinding | Seized bearing or jammed debris | Clear visible debris (power off) | Replace fan motor |
| Fan won't start, thermostat shows a fault | Board/low-voltage fault (communicating units) | Note the code; power cycle once | Diagnose board & 24V circuit |
| Unit iced over, fan struggling | Frozen coil from another fault | Turn off, let thaw | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant issue |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices including parts and labor; your market and unit size vary.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$300
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $300–$650 (higher for variable-speed/ECM motors on XV units)
- **Breaker or disconnect repair:** $100–$300 (electrician may be involved)
- **Control-board replacement (communicating units):** $400–$800+
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90–$180, often credited toward the repair

If the compressor was run without the fan long enough to fail, that's a much larger repair — often **$1,500–$2,800+**, which is why shutting the unit off early matters.

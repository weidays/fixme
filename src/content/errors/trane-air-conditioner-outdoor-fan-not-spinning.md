## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a blinking fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Trane condenser (the outdoor unit). The compressor may hum or start, but the large fan on top of the unit stays still. On most Trane split systems the outdoor fan and compressor share power through a contactor and, on single-fan units, a dual run capacitor.

When the fan stops, the outdoor coil can't shed heat. Your Trane may still blow air indoors, but it won't cool well and the compressor is at serious risk. This is a **pro-level repair** because the run capacitor stores a dangerous high-voltage charge even after power is cut, and the diagnosis involves live 230V measurements.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common. The capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. When it weakens or fails, the fan won't start on its own (but may spin if nudged).
2. **Bad condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, a burnt winding, or an overheated motor that has tripped its internal thermal cutout.
3. **Faulty contactor** — The relay that sends 230V to the fan and compressor can have pitted or stuck contacts, cutting power to the fan.
4. **Seized fan blade or debris** — Sticks, ice, or a warped blade physically blocking rotation.
5. **Tripped high-pressure switch or safety lockout** — A dirty coil or other fault can cut the outdoor unit; behavior varies by model and board.
6. **Wiring or terminal damage** — Corroded, loose, or chewed (rodent) wiring to the fan motor.
7. **Control board fault** — On variable-speed Trane units (XV series) an ECM/module fault can stop the fan; this varies by board.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do — everything at the condenser involves stored high voltage.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Try lowering it 3–4 degrees.
- **Air filter:** A clogged indoor filter can cause pressure trips that shut the outdoor unit. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker (and the disconnect box near the unit). Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Visible obstruction:** From a safe distance, look for sticks, leaves, or ice jammed in the fan blades. Do **not** reach in or push the blade — if you must clear debris, turn off power at the disconnect first and use a tool, not your hand.
- **Vents and airflow indoors:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan/float switch usually stops the indoor blower, but check it isn't overflowing.

Do **not** repeatedly cycle a locked-out unit, and never bypass a pressure or safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a predictable order — use this to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Visual and mechanical check** — Spins the fan by hand (power off) to feel for seized bearings or a dragging motor.
2. **Voltage at the contactor** — Confirms 230V is present when the unit calls for cooling.
3. **Capacitor test** — Discharges the capacitor safely, then measures microfarads against the printed rating. A reading well below spec confirms a bad cap.
4. **Motor test** — Checks motor windings for continuity and correct resistance, and looks for a tripped internal overload.
5. **Contactor inspection** — Looks for pitted, burnt, or stuck contacts and tests the 24V coil.
6. **Safety/control checks** — Tests the high-pressure switch and, on variable-speed models, reads any board fault codes.

Expect a diagnostic fee of roughly **$80–$150**, often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan won't start; spins if nudged | Failed run capacitor | Shut off system, don't push-start repeatedly | Discharge & replace capacitor with matching µF |
| Fan silent, no hum, unit dead | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, check disconnect |
| Fan tries to start, buzzes, stops | Weak capacitor or seizing motor | Turn off, call pro | Test cap and motor, replace as needed |
| Fan blade physically won't turn | Debris or seized bearings | Power off, clear visible debris only | Replace motor if bearings are seized |
| Unit runs a while then fan/compressor cut out | High-pressure trip / dirty coil | Replace filter, hose off outer coil (power off) | Diagnose pressure fault, clean/repair |
| Warm air indoors, outdoor fan still | Any of the above | Shut off to protect compressor | Full diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$300
- **Condenser fan motor (with new blade if needed):** $300–$650, higher on larger tonnage or hard-to-source variable-speed models
- **Fan blade only:** $100–$250
- **Wiring repair:** $150–$400 depending on extent
- **Diagnostic fee:** $80–$150 (often waived with repair)

Prices vary by region, tonnage, and whether your unit is under Trane's parts warranty. A registered Trane condenser often carries a **10-year limited parts warranty** — confirm coverage before paying for parts.

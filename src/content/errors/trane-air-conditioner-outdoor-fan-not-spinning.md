## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a blink-code on most Trane systems — it's a physical symptom you notice at the condenser (the big outdoor unit). The compressor may be running (you'll hear a hum) while the fan on top sits still, or the whole unit may be silent.

The outdoor fan's job is to pull air across the condenser coil so the refrigerant can dump heat. When it stops, the system loses its ability to reject heat: pressures spike, the compressor overheats, and cooling drops off or stops entirely. This is why we rate it **pro** — a stalled fan can destroy a compressor within minutes.

On variable-speed **XV-series** Trane units, the fan is an ECM motor driven by the control board, and a fault may set an actual diagnostic code at the indoor board. On standard single-stage units (most **XR/XL** and 2TTx/4TTx models), the fan is a simple PSC motor run off a capacitor with no code.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The most common cause by far. The dual capacitor that starts both the fan and compressor bulges, weakens, or dies. Symptom: humming, no spin.
2. **Bad condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, a burned winding, or an open thermal overload. Sometimes it runs hot, stops, then restarts once cooled.
3. **Failed contactor** — The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit has pitted or welded contacts, so the fan (and compressor) never get power.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse** — No power reaching the condenser at all; unit is completely silent.
5. **Obstructed or seized fan blade** — Debris, ice, or a bent blade jamming the motor. Bearings can seize after years of exposure.
6. **Control board or ECM fault (XV variable-speed only)** — On high-end units, a board fault or failed ECM module stops the fan and may set a code.
7. **Low-voltage / wiring problem** — A broken 24V control wire or loose lug prevents the contactor from pulling in.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do with the power respected:

- **Thermostat:** Set to COOL and the setpoint several degrees below room temperature. Confirm it's calling for cooling. Replace batteries if it's a battery model and the screen is dim.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter and a frozen indoor coil can shut things down; replace a dirty filter and let any ice fully melt.
- **Breaker & disconnect:** Check the AC breaker in your main panel and reset it once if tripped. If it trips again immediately, stop and call a pro. Do not repeatedly reset.
- **Visible obstruction:** With the unit **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest jammed in the fan blade. Remove obvious debris.
- **Ice or water:** If the outdoor coil or line is iced, turn the system off and let it thaw. Check the indoor condensate drain isn't backed up and tripping a float switch.

Do **not** open the electrical panel on the condenser, discharge or replace a capacitor, or spin the blade on a live unit. Capacitors hold a dangerous charge even with the power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from power in toward the motor, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verifies power** at the disconnect and confirms 240V is reaching the unit.
2. **Checks the contactor** — looks for a pulled-in coil, measures voltage across the contacts, inspects for pitting or welding.
3. **Tests the run capacitor** with a meter, comparing microfarad (µF) reading against the label rating. A capacitor 10% or more below spec gets replaced.
4. **Tests the fan motor** — checks winding resistance, verifies it isn't drawing locked-rotor amps, and confirms the thermal overload isn't open.
5. **Spins the blade by hand (power off)** to feel for seized bearings.
6. **On XV units**, reads the control-board fault code and checks the ECM harness and module.

A straightforward capacitor diagnosis takes minutes. Be wary of a quote that jumps straight to a full motor or compressor replacement without a capacitor test.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | Turn unit off to protect compressor; call pro | Test and replace dual run capacitor |
| Fan runs briefly, stops, restarts later | Overheating fan motor / weak overload | Shut off, don't keep resetting | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Whole outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, or bad contactor | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Replace contactor or fuse, trace power |
| Fan blade physically stuck | Debris or seized bearings | Power off, clear visible debris | Free/replace blade or motor |
| Buzzing at contactor, no power out | Pitted/welded contactor contacts | None — leave sealed | Replace contactor |
| XV unit, fan dead + error code | ECM/control board fault | Note the code | Diagnose board and ECM motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$350. The part is cheap ($15–$40); most of the cost is the service call and diagnosis.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 depending on horsepower and whether a new capacitor is included.
- **Breaker or disconnect fuse:** $100–$250.
- **ECM motor or control board (XV variable-speed):** $600–$1,200+ — variable-speed parts are expensive and model-specific.
- **DIY:** $0 for clearing debris or resetting a breaker once. Capacitor and motor work involves stored electrical charge and 240V — we recommend a pro.

Diagnostic fees ($80–$180) are often credited toward the repair. Ask before booking.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a lettered Trane fault code. On most Trane air conditioners the outdoor unit (condenser) contains a fan that pulls air across the coil to reject heat. When the compressor is trying to run but that fan sits still, the condenser can't shed heat, pressures climb, and the system either stops cooling or shuts down to protect itself.

This behavior can occur on conventional single-stage and two-stage Trane condensers (XR, XL families) as well as variable-speed communicating units (XV series). On conventional units the cause is usually a capacitor, contactor, or fan motor. On variable-speed/ECM outdoor units the motor is driven by an electronics module and the diagnosis runs through that module and the thermostat rather than a start capacitor — so the parts and checks differ. If you have a communicating XV system, expect a fault to show on the thermostat rather than as a silent stalled fan.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor** — The dual run capacitor provides starting torque to the fan (and compressor). This is the single most common reason a Trane outdoor fan won't spin while the compressor hums.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings or a shorted winding can jam the motor. Often accompanied by a burnt smell or a hot, tripping breaker.
3. **Failed contactor** — The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit can have pitted or stuck contacts, so the fan never gets full voltage.
4. **Tripped high-pressure or float switch / lockout** — A safety switch may have cut the outdoor unit due to a prior problem (dirty coil, low airflow).
5. **Debris or ice jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade can physically block rotation.
6. **Control board or module fault (variable-speed units)** — On XV/communicating condensers, a failed ECM module or wiring fault stops the motor and typically posts a thermostat alert.
7. **Loss of power to the condenser** — Tripped breaker, blown fuses in the outdoor disconnect, or a broken low-voltage wire.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim.
- **Air filter:** A clogged indoor filter causes airflow and pressure problems that can trip outdoor safeties. Replace it if it's dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal a real fault.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Look at the pull-out disconnect box near the condenser; make sure it's fully seated.
- **Visible obstructions:** With the system OFF and the disconnect pulled, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest against the fan blade. Clear debris by hand only.
- **Supply vents:** Make sure indoor registers aren't closed or blocked, which can trigger pressure-related shutdowns.
- **Condensate:** If your system has a float switch and the drain pan is full, clearing a clogged condensate line may restore operation.

Do **not** open the electrical panel on the condenser, touch the capacitor, or bypass any safety switch. Capacitors hold a dangerous charge even with power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. Confirm 24V call to the contactor and verify line voltage at the outdoor unit.
2. Safely discharge and **test the run capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads — a reading well below spec confirms a bad cap.
3. Inspect the **contactor** for pitting and verify it pulls in and passes voltage.
4. Check the **fan motor** — spin it by hand for bearing drag, measure winding resistance, and test for a ground fault.
5. Inspect the blade for damage and the motor for a burnt smell.
6. On XV/communicating units, read fault codes at the thermostat and test the **ECM module** and its wiring rather than a capacitor.
7. Verify system pressures once running to confirm the fan is actually cooling the coil.

This sequence is quick; a straightforward capacitor diagnosis should take well under an hour.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan still | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't force it | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan won't move, motor hot/burnt smell | Seized/burned fan motor | Shut off at disconnect | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Nothing runs outside, indoor blower works | Contactor or lost power | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test/replace contactor, trace wiring |
| Blade blocked by debris | Sticks/leaves/nest | Clear debris with power off | Inspect blade & bearings for damage |
| Fan stops, thermostat shows alert | ECM module fault (XV) | Note the code; power cycle once | Diagnose module/wiring per Trane data |
| Breaker trips when fan should start | Shorted motor or wiring | Do not repeatedly reset | Isolate short, replace faulted part |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your quote varies by region and unit.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90 – $180
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (PSC, conventional):** $350 – $650
- **Variable-speed ECM outdoor motor / module:** $500 – $1,200+ (parts alone are costly on XV units)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Wiring / disconnect repair:** $120 – $300

A weak capacitor is the cheapest and most common fix. If the fan ran with a bad cap for a while, ask the tech to verify the compressor wasn't damaged by overheating.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a blink-code on Trane equipment — it's a physical symptom you observe at the condenser (the unit outside). The large fan on top of the outdoor unit should spin whenever the compressor is running to reject heat picked up from your home.

When that fan stops but the rest of the system keeps trying to run, high-pressure refrigerant has nowhere to dump its heat. Pressures and temperatures climb quickly, and the compressor — the most expensive part in the system — is at real risk. Many Trane units have internal high-pressure or thermal protection that will shut the compressor down on overload, so you may also notice the AC "not cooling" at the same time.

Because the outdoor unit runs on 208–240V and capacitors hold a lethal charge even after power is off, most repairs here are a pro-level job. The safe checks below are limited to things you can do without opening the electrical panel.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** The single most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor the torque to start spinning. When it weakens or dies, the motor hums but won't turn, or won't start at all. Cheap part, but it lives beside live 240V terminals.
2. **Seized or worn fan motor.** Bearings dry out and lock up, especially on older units. The motor may buzz, trip the breaker, or do nothing.
3. **Debris jamming the blade.** Sticks, leaves, ice, or a bent blade physically block rotation. Sometimes obvious, sometimes not.
4. **Contactor not pulling in.** The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts, or a bad coil, can starve the fan of power.
5. **Tripped high-pressure / overload protection.** The unit shuts down to protect itself; on some Trane models the fan and compressor cycle off together.
6. **Loose or burnt wiring.** Overheated terminals or a chewed wire (rodents) at the fan motor or capacitor.
7. **Control board or ECM module fault.** On variable-speed and two-stage Trane units (XV, some XL), an ECM fan motor or its control board can fail rather than a simple capacitor. Behavior varies by model — these do not use a conventional run capacitor on the fan.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps that don't require opening a live electrical compartment:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Indoor filter:** A clogged filter can cause the whole system to overheat and lock out. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the **outdoor disconnect / condenser breaker**. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — that's a fault, not a fluke.
- **Visible debris:** With the system **off at the thermostat and the outdoor breaker**, look through the top grille for obvious sticks, leaves, or ice on the blade. Do not reach in or force the blade.
- **Airflow around the unit:** Clear tall grass, mulch, or clutter within about 2 feet of the condenser.
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan can trip a float switch and shut the system down; check for standing water or an overflow at the indoor unit.

Do **not** open the outdoor electrical panel, touch the capacitor, or spin the fan blade by hand on a powered unit. Repeatedly resetting a unit that keeps shutting off can damage the compressor — call a pro instead.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Visual and safety check** — kills power at the disconnect, inspects the blade, wiring, and terminals for burns or rodent damage.
2. **Capacitor test** — measures the fan capacitor's microfarad (µF) rating against the rating printed on it. A weak capacitor is condemned even if it "kind of works."
3. **Contactor check** — inspects the contacts for pitting and verifies the coil pulls in and passes voltage.
4. **Motor test** — checks the fan motor windings for resistance and grounds, and spins the shaft to feel for seized bearings.
5. **Electrical supply** — confirms 240V is reaching the unit and the fan circuit.
6. **On ECM/variable-speed models** — pulls fault data from the control board or module; the fix may be a motor module rather than a capacitor.
7. **Pressure check** — if protection kept tripping, verifies refrigerant charge and that a high-pressure lockout hasn't masked another problem.

A good tech tells you *which* part failed and shows you the reading. Be wary of a "replace the whole motor" quote when only the capacitor was tested.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan won't turn | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't touch | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan dead, motor won't buzz or spin | Seized motor or no power to fan | Check outdoor breaker once | Test motor windings; replace motor |
| Blade visibly blocked | Debris, ice, or bent blade | Power off; remove obvious debris | Clear jam; straighten/replace blade |
| Fan and compressor both off | Contactor fault or lockout | Check thermostat & breaker | Test/replace contactor; find lockout cause |
| Breaker trips when fan tries to start | Shorted motor or wiring | Reset once; if it re-trips, stop | Diagnose short; replace motor/wiring |
| Fan runs slow then stops (variable-speed) | ECM module/board fault | None safe | Pull codes; replace ECM module/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor, for a Trane condenser fan issue:

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180
- **Run capacitor (single or dual) replaced:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor replaced:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (standard PSC):** $350 – $650
- **ECM / variable-speed fan motor or module (XV, some XL):** $500 – $1,200
- **Debris removal / blade straightening:** often within the service-call fee, up to ~$200 if the blade is replaced
- **Wiring / terminal repair:** $150 – $400

Prices vary by region, unit age, and whether your Trane is under parts warranty (registered Trane systems often carry a 10-year parts warranty — labor is usually separate). If the compressor was damaged by running without the fan, costs rise steeply, so shut the unit down promptly.

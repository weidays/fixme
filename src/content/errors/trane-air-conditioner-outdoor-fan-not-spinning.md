## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Trane condenser (the outdoor unit). When cooling is called, the outdoor fan should spin along with the compressor to reject heat outside. If the compressor runs (or hums) but the fan blade sits still, the system can't shed heat and will quickly overheat.

Most Trane condensers don't flash a specific "fan" code for this. The board simply energizes the contactor, and both compressor and fan should power up together. On variable-speed **XV-series** units with an ECM fan motor, the control board may log a communication or motor fault instead — check the indoor board's diagnostic display on those models.

**Important:** Do not keep the system running with a stalled fan. Turn cooling off at the thermostat to protect the compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. When it weakens, the fan hums but won't spin, or spins if you nudge it.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings or a shorted winding stop the motor entirely. Often smells burnt or the blade won't turn freely by hand (power off).
3. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a warped/bent blade physically blocking rotation.
4. **Faulty contactor** — The relay that sends power to the fan and compressor can have pitted or stuck contacts, cutting power to the fan.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — Partial power loss can run the compressor circuit but not the fan on some wiring layouts.
6. **Control board / relay fault** — More common on ECM/variable-speed XV units where the board drives the motor directly.
7. **Loose or corroded wiring** — A disconnected fan lead at the capacitor, motor, or terminal block.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Everything else involves stored electrical charge or line voltage.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it has them.
- **Breakers:** Check the main panel and the outdoor disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the indoor system to freeze and shut down; replace if dirty.
- **Visible debris (power OFF):** Turn the unit off at the breaker, then look for leaves, sticks, or debris resting in the fan blades. Clear only what you can safely reach by hand.
- **Vents:** Make sure supply and return registers indoors are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate:** Check that the drain line and pan aren't overflowing, which can trigger a safety float switch that stops the system.

**Do NOT** spin the fan with a stick to "help" it start while powered, open the capacitor cover, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping. Those are pro-only or unsafe.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path. Knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Visual and manual check** — Power off, spin the blade by hand to feel for bearing drag or a seized motor.
2. **Capacitor test** — With a multimeter (after safely discharging it), they measure microfarads against the rated value on the label. A reading well below spec confirms a bad capacitor.
3. **Voltage checks** — Confirm 240V line voltage reaching the contactor and 24V to its coil, then verify power flows through to the fan motor.
4. **Motor test** — Check windings for continuity and shorts to ground; a failed winding means motor replacement.
5. **Contactor inspection** — Look for pitted, burned, or stuck contacts.
6. **ECM/board diagnostics (XV units)** — On variable-speed models, they read fault codes and test the motor's control signal before condemning the motor or board.

Expect a diagnostic/service-call fee of **$85–$180**, often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | Turn unit off to avoid overheating | Safely discharge & replace capacitor |
| Fan won't turn, blade stiff by hand | Seized fan motor | Power off, don't force it | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Blade physically blocked | Debris in fan | Power off, remove visible debris | Clear jam, check blade balance |
| Compressor hums, no fan, all power on | Bad capacitor or contactor | None safe | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Nothing runs outside | Tripped breaker/blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Find cause of overload, replace fuse |
| XV unit fan dead, board shows fault | ECM motor or control fault | Note the code, call pro | Diagnose motor vs. board |
| Fan runs then quits, buzzing relay | Failing contactor | None safe | Replace contactor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor (standard PSC):** $350–$650
- **ECM/variable-speed fan motor (XV units):** $600–$1,200+
- **Clearing debris / minor wiring repair:** $85–$200
- **Control board (XV units):** $600–$1,000+
- **Diagnostic fee alone:** $85–$180 (often waived with repair)

Prices vary by region, unit age, and part availability. On older units, weigh a motor-and-capacitor repair against overall system condition.

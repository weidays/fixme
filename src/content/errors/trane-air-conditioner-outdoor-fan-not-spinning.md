## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a Trane fault code — it's a physical symptom you observe at the condenser (the outdoor unit). When the system calls for cooling, the compressor and the outdoor fan should both start. The fan pulls air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system pulled out of your house.

If that fan sits still while the compressor runs, heat has nowhere to go. Pressures climb, the compressor overheats, and most Trane units will trip an internal thermal or high-pressure safety within minutes. Running the system in this state is one of the fastest ways to destroy a compressor, which is by far the most expensive part of the machine.

**Turn the system off at the thermostat** until the fan is confirmed working. This is a "pro" repair for most causes because the outdoor unit stores dangerous high voltage even after power is cut.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** The single most common cause. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor (and compressor) the jolt it needs to start. When it weakens or fails, the fan won't start — you'll often hear the compressor hum instead.
2. **Seized or worn fan motor.** Bearings dry out and lock up, especially on older units. The motor tries to start, can't, and overheats. Sometimes the fan spins if you nudge it (do NOT do this with power on).
3. **Failed contactor.** The contactor is the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit. Pitted, stuck, or burned contacts can cut power to the fan or the whole condenser.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse / disconnect issue.** No power to the outdoor unit means no fan. The pull-out disconnect near the condenser can also be loose or corroded.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade.** Sticks, ice, or a warped blade can physically block rotation.
6. **Bad control board or thermostat wiring (communicating XV models).** Variable-speed Trane systems with ECM fan motors rely on the control board to command fan speed. A board or communication fault can leave the fan idle.
7. **Failed compressor overload or high-pressure lockout.** A protective device may be cutting the whole unit, and the "no fan" is a symptom, not the root cause.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Everything else at the outdoor unit involves stored high voltage.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and lower the setpoint several degrees so the system actually calls for cooling. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the indoor system to short-cycle or trip protections. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again immediately, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals a short.
- **Visible debris:** With the system **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or ice on the fan blade. Clear anything loose you can reach without opening the unit.
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan or clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut the system down. Check that the drain line is clear and any pan isn't overflowing.
- **Disconnect:** Confirm the pull-out disconnect box next to the outdoor unit is fully seated.

Do **not** spin the fan blade by hand to "help" it start, open the electrical panel of the condenser, or discharge the capacitor yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will work through this sequence — use it to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirm the call and power.** Verify 24V control signal to the contactor and 240V line power at the disconnect.
2. **Test the capacitor.** With power off and the capacitor safely discharged, they measure microfarad (µF) rating against the label. A capacitor reading well below spec gets replaced.
3. **Check the contactor.** Inspect for pitted/burned contacts and verify it pulls in and passes voltage when energized.
4. **Test the fan motor.** Measure winding resistance and check for a locked rotor or open windings. On ECM/variable-speed models, they'll check the motor module and control-board commands.
5. **Rule out protections.** Verify no high-pressure or overload lockout is active, and check refrigerant charge if the unit was overheating.

A good tech should show you the capacitor reading or the failed part. Be cautious of anyone replacing the whole motor without first testing the (much cheaper) capacitor.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan won't start | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; call pro | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan tries to start, then stops; motor hot | Seized fan motor / bad bearings | Turn system off; call pro | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Replace contactor / trace power fault |
| Buzzing at contactor, no fan | Stuck or pitted contactor | Turn system off; call pro | Replace contactor |
| Fan blade physically blocked | Debris or bent blade | Kill power, clear loose debris | Replace/balance blade |
| Fan idle on communicating XV unit, no fault light | Control board / motor module fault | Note error on thermostat; call pro | Diagnose board & ECM motor |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Short in motor or wiring | Stop resetting; call pro | Isolate and repair short |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices (parts + labor). Your area and unit age affect the total.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (PSC, single-stage units):** $400 – $700
- **ECM/variable-speed fan motor or module (XV communicating units):** $600 – $1,200+
- **Breaker or disconnect repair:** $150 – $350
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair)

If the compressor was run hot for a long time before the fan issue was caught, factor in the risk of compressor damage — a repair that can run $1,500–$2,800+ and often makes replacement worth considering on older units.

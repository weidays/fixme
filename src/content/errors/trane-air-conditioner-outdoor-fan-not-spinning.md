## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is not a numeric fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Trane outdoor unit (condenser). The fan on top of the condenser is supposed to pull air through the coil to reject heat that your system removed from the house. When it stops, the compressor can still try to run, but pressures and temperatures climb fast.

On most Trane split systems the outdoor fan motor is a single-phase PSC (permanent split capacitor) motor powered through a run capacitor and switched by a contactor. Higher-end variable-speed Trane models (XV series) may use an ECM motor with its own control module, which fails and is diagnosed differently. **Because the exact motor type and control vary by model and board, confirm your unit's data plate before ordering parts.**

If the fan is not spinning, the safest thing you can do is **turn the system off at the thermostat** and stop cooling calls until it's fixed — running a compressor without condenser airflow is a fast way to a very expensive compressor failure.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The single most common cause. A weak or bulged capacitor can't give the fan motor the starting torque it needs. You may hear the compressor hum while the fan sits still.
2. **Seized or burned-out fan motor** — Worn bearings, overheating, or age can lock or kill the motor. Sometimes it spins slowly, wobbles, or only runs when you nudge it (a sign it's near the end).
3. **Failed contactor** — Pitted or stuck contacts interrupt power to the fan (and compressor) circuit. A buzzing or non-pulling contactor is a classic culprit.
4. **Debris blocking the blade** — Leaves, sticks, a bird, or ice can physically jam the fan.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse / lost 24V control** — No power to the outdoor unit, or a failed low-voltage circuit, keeps the contactor from closing.
6. **Control board / ECM module fault** — On variable-speed Trane units, a failed fan control module or board can stop the motor with no obvious mechanical problem.
7. **Overheated motor thermal cutout** — A motor that's cycling on its internal overload will stop and only restart after cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser, touch the capacitor, or spin the blade on a powered unit — capacitors store a dangerous charge.**

- **Thermostat:** Set it to Cool and a temperature several degrees below room temperature to confirm the system is actually calling for cooling.
- **Air filter:** A clogged indoor filter can cause the whole system to short-cycle and mask what's happening outside. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the pull-out disconnect box near the condenser is fully seated (someone may have pulled it for service).
- **Visible debris:** With the unit powered OFF at the breaker and disconnect, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or an object jamming the blade. Clear only what you can reach without removing panels.
- **Condensate & vents:** Confirm indoor supply vents are open and the condensate drain isn't backed up (a full pan can trip a safety switch and shut the system down).

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, it's an electrical or motor repair — call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A tech should work in a logical order, and knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check the bill:

1. **Confirm the call & power:** Verify 24V to the contactor coil and line voltage into the unit.
2. **Test the capacitor:** With power off and the cap safely discharged, measure microfarads against the rating printed on it. A reading well below spec means replacement.
3. **Check the contactor:** Look for pitted contacts and confirm it pulls in and passes voltage to the fan motor when energized.
4. **Test the fan motor:** Check windings for resistance/continuity, spin the de-energized blade to feel for a seized bearing, and confirm it gets proper voltage.
5. **Inspect wiring & the board:** On variable-speed models, they'll check the ECM/fan module and any diagnostic LEDs specific to the board.
6. **Verify the fix:** After the repair they should confirm the fan starts on its own, check amp draw, and read refrigerant pressures to make sure no compressor damage occurred.

A good tech won't just throw a capacitor at it — they'll measure. Be cautious of a quote that jumps straight to a full motor or board replacement without any testing.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan won't turn | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't nudge the blade | Test & replace capacitor, verify amp draw |
| Fan only starts if you push it | Weak capacitor or worn motor bearing | Shut unit off; stop running it | Replace capacitor and/or fan motor |
| No sound at all from outdoor unit | Tripped breaker, bad contactor, lost 24V | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test contactor, control voltage, wiring |
| Buzzing at the contactor | Stuck/failed contactor | None | Replace contactor |
| Blade physically jammed | Debris, ice, or object | Power off, clear visible debris | Inspect motor/bearing for damage |
| Fan runs then stops after minutes | Overheating motor / thermal overload | Turn off, let cool, call pro | Test motor, check amp draw & airflow |
| Everything else fine, fan dead (XV models) | ECM/fan control module fault | None | Read board diagnostics, replace module/board |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices including parts and labor; your area and model affect the total.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Run capacitor:** $150 – $300
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (PSC):** $400 – $650+
- **ECM fan motor / control module (variable-speed models):** $600 – $1,200+
- **Clearing debris / minor fix:** $80 – $150 (often just the service call)

If the fan was down long enough to overheat the compressor, related repairs can run into the thousands — another reason to shut the system off the moment you notice the fan isn't spinning.

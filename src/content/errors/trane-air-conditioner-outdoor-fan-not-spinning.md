## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Trane outdoor unit (the condenser). When cooling is called, the compressor and the outdoor fan motor should both energize. The fan pulls air across the condenser coil to reject heat. If the fan blade isn't turning while the compressor hums, the system can't shed heat.

On standard Trane single- and two-stage units (XR/XL series), there's usually no digital display for this — you diagnose by observation. On variable-speed and communicating Trane systems (XV models with a ComfortLink™ control), the board may log a fan-motor or communication fault that a technician reads with a service tool. Exactly what gets reported varies by board, so don't assume a specific code number.

**Important:** Do not keep the system running if the compressor is on but the fan is dead. Compressor damage can happen fast. Turn cooling off at the thermostat.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor** — The most common cause by far. The dual-run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it degrades, the fan may not start, may spin slowly, or may twitch and stop.
2. **Bad condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, a burned winding, or an internal thermal cutout that tripped from overheating.
3. **Debris or obstruction jamming the blade** — Sticks, a warped/bent blade, or ice can physically block rotation.
4. **Contactor not pulling in** — If the contactor doesn't close, neither the fan nor compressor gets power (you'd usually notice the compressor is off too).
5. **Tripped motor thermal overload** — A motor that overheated may shut off and reset when cool, giving intermittent operation.
6. **Wiring or connector fault** — A loose spade terminal, chewed wire, or corroded connection at the motor or capacitor.
7. **Control board / module fault (variable-speed & communicating units)** — On XV models, the motor module or ComfortLink control may command the fan off due to a detected fault.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Leave anything inside the electrical panel of the condenser to a pro.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace dead thermostat batteries if it uses them.
- **Air filter:** A clogged indoor filter can cause pressure and freeze problems that mimic outdoor faults. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor condenser breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Visible obstruction:** With the system OFF at the thermostat and breaker, look through the top grille for sticks, leaves, or a bent blade. Clear loose debris — but do not reach in or force the blade by hand while power is on.
- **Coil and airflow:** A heavily matted, dirty outdoor coil restricts airflow. Gently rinse from outside with a garden hose (power off) if it's caked with cottonwood or grass clippings.
- **Ice:** If the unit is iced over, turn cooling off and let it thaw before further testing.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, it's a pro job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works in a logical order — this helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the call and power:** Confirms 24V control signal reaches the contactor and that line voltage is present at the unit.
2. **Test the capacitor:** Measures microfarads (µF) against the printed rating with the power safely off and the cap discharged. Out-of-spec = replace.
3. **Test the fan motor:** Checks windings for resistance/continuity, looks for a tripped thermal, and confirms bearings spin freely.
4. **Check the contactor:** Verifies it pulls in and that contacts aren't burned or pitted.
5. **Inspect wiring:** Looks for loose, corroded, or damaged terminals at the motor and capacitor.
6. **Communicating/variable-speed units:** Reads any logged fault from the motor module or ComfortLink control with a service tool.

Ask which specific component tested bad and what its measured value was. A capacitor swap is a small job; a motor is a larger one — the diagnosis should justify the price.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan dead or barely moves | Failed run capacitor | Shut system off to protect compressor | Test & replace dual-run capacitor |
| Fan won't start; motor feels/looks fine | Bad fan motor or tripped thermal | Turn off, let unit cool, retry once | Test windings, replace motor if failed |
| Blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Power off, clear loose debris | Straighten/replace blade, check bearings |
| Neither fan nor compressor run | Contactor not closing | Reset breaker once only | Test/replace contactor, check 24V signal |
| Fan works then quits intermittently | Thermal overload or weak cap | Note pattern, keep system off | Measure cap, check motor amp draw |
| XV unit reports fan/motor fault | Motor module or control board | None — call a pro | Read fault, test module/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor combined. Regional rates and premium-model parts push toward the high end.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (standard PSC):** $300 – $650
- **Variable-speed / ECM fan motor or module (XV models):** $500 – $1,200+
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Wiring/terminal repair:** $100 – $250

If a capacitor and motor both failed together, expect the higher end of the motor range. Always get the failed part's measured value before approving a motor replacement.

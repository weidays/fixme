## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a blinking error code — it's a physical symptom you observe at the condenser (the outdoor unit). When your Trane AC calls for cooling, the outdoor fan should spin to push heat out of the refrigerant coil. If the compressor is running (you hear a hum or buzz) but the top fan blade is still, refrigerant pressure builds fast and the unit will overheat.

Most Trane residential condensers use a single-phase PSC (permanent split capacitor) fan motor powered through a **dual-run capacitor** and a **contactor**. When the fan won't turn, the fault is almost always in one of those two components, the motor itself, or the low-voltage signal telling the contactor to close.

Note: variable-speed premium models (like the **XV20i**) use ECM-driven fans with onboard electronics, so their failure behavior and diagnosis differ from the standard capacitor-start units described here.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed dual-run capacitor** — the single most common cause. This capacitor feeds both fan and compressor; a bulged, swollen, or out-of-spec cap leaves the fan unable to start (often with a hum).
2. **Seized or worn fan motor bearings** — an old motor can lock up. If the blade won't spin freely by hand (power off), the motor is likely done.
3. **Burned or pitted contactor** — the contactor's contacts can stick open, so power never reaches the fan and compressor.
4. **Failed fan motor windings** — an internally shorted or open motor won't run even with a good capacitor.
5. **Debris jamming the blade** — leaves, twigs, or a bent blade can physically block rotation.
6. **Low-voltage / control fault** — a bad thermostat call, blown low-voltage fuse, or failed control board means the contactor never energizes.
7. **Overheated motor thermal cutout** — a hot motor may shut off on its internal overload and restart when cool, giving intermittent operation.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the electrical panel on the condenser or touch the capacitor — a capacitor can hold a lethal charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Air filter:** A clogged indoor filter can cause the system to shut down on safeties. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor condenser breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Debris:** With the system OFF at the breaker, look through the fan grille for leaves or sticks blocking the blade. Clear anything obvious.
- **Vents:** Make sure indoor supply and return vents aren't blocked, which can trigger shutdowns.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged condensate line can trip a float switch and stop the system. Clear a visibly full line if you can safely reach it.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, **turn the system off and call an HVAC technician.** Running with a dead fan can destroy the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work through this sequence so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the cooling call** with a meter at the contactor's low-voltage (24V) terminals.
2. **Check the capacitor** with a multimeter's capacitance function, comparing the reading to the microfarad rating printed on the label. A weak or dead cap gets replaced.
3. **Inspect the contactor** for pitted, welded, or open contacts and confirm line voltage passes through when energized.
4. **Test the fan motor** — spin the blade by hand (power off), then check windings for continuity and correct resistance. A push-start that only works with a nudge confirms a capacitor or motor issue.
5. **Confirm blade and shaft** aren't bent or seized.
6. **Verify supply voltage** at the disconnect (208-230V on most residential units).

A good diagnosis takes 20-45 minutes. Be cautious of any quote to replace the whole condenser before the capacitor and contactor have been tested.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan still | Failed dual-run capacitor | Shut unit off; don't touch cap | Test and replace capacitor |
| Fan blade won't turn by hand (power off) | Seized motor bearings | None — call a pro | Replace condenser fan motor |
| No hum, no fan, breaker OK | Open/burned contactor | Verify breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Fan spins with a manual push, then dies | Weak capacitor or worn motor | Don't rely on it | Replace cap, test motor |
| Blade visibly jammed | Debris or bent blade | Kill power, clear debris | Straighten/replace blade |
| Fan runs, then quits after minutes | Motor thermal overload | Shut off, let cool | Replace overheating motor |
| Nothing at all outdoors | Low-voltage fuse/board fault | Check thermostat & breaker | Test 24V circuit and board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Dual-run capacitor:** $150 – $300
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (standard PSC):** $300 – $650, depending on horsepower and access
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Low-voltage fuse / control diagnosis:** $100 – $250
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75 – $200 (often credited toward the repair)

Variable-speed ECM fan motors on premium models (XV20i and similar) cost more — commonly **$600 – $1,200+** because the motor and its control module are integrated. Always get the failed part confirmed by test before authorizing a big replacement.

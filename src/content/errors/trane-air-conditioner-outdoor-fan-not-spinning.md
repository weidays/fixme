## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a blink code — it's a physical symptom on your Trane condenser (the outdoor unit). The compressor may be trying to run, but the large fan on top of the unit isn't turning to reject heat outside.

Trane condensers do not typically flash a diagnostic light for this on standard XR/XL models; you notice it by sight and sound. On communicating XV systems, the thermostat or the outdoor board **may** log a related fault, but behavior varies by model and firmware — don't assume a specific code number without checking your unit's service literature.

This is a **pro-level** problem. A condenser fan that won't spin while the compressor runs causes heat and pressure to climb fast, and can wreck the compressor — the single most expensive part in the system. Shut the AC off at the thermostat until it's fixed.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual-run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting "kick." When it weakens, the fan hums or sits still. Look for a bulged or leaking top on the capacitor.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Bearings fail, or windings burn out. The fan won't turn even with a good capacitor, and you may see scorching or smell burnt insulation.
3. **Faulty contactor** — The relay that sends 240V to the outdoor unit. Pitted or stuck contacts can starve the fan (and compressor) of power.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuses at the disconnect** — No power reaching the outdoor unit at all.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — A stick, leaves, or ice buildup physically blocking the blade.
6. **Failed control board or wiring fault** — On communicating XV units, a board or low-voltage wiring problem can cut the fan signal. Less common, and hard to confirm without tools.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser or touch the capacitor** — it holds a dangerous charge.

- **Thermostat:** Set to COOL and lower the setpoint several degrees to confirm the system is actually calling for cooling.
- **Air filter:** A badly clogged filter can cause the indoor coil to freeze and shut things down. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect / vents:** Make sure nothing obviously blocks airflow around the unit — leaves, grass, cardboard. Keep 2 ft clearance.
- **Visible debris:** With the system **off at the breaker**, look (don't reach in) for a stick or object visibly stuck in the fan blades.
- **Condensate:** Check the indoor drain line and pan aren't overflowing, which can trigger a float switch shutdown.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, turn the system off and call a licensed technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Pulls the disconnect** and confirms power is dead before touching anything.
2. **Discharges and tests the capacitor** with a multimeter, comparing microfarad (µF) reading to the rating printed on the label.
3. **Spins the fan by hand** to check for a seized motor or bad bearings.
4. **Tests the fan motor** for continuity, resistance, and grounded windings.
5. **Inspects the contactor** for pitting and verifies 24V at the coil and 240V across the contacts.
6. **Checks breaker/disconnect fuses** for correct voltage reaching the unit.
7. On XV communicating units, **reads any stored faults** at the thermostat or board.

A capacitor swap is quick and cheap; a motor or board diagnosis takes longer. Be wary of a quote that jumps straight to motor or compressor replacement without a documented capacitor test.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but doesn't spin | Failed run capacitor | Shut off AC; don't run it | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan dead, compressor humming | Bad capacitor or motor | Shut off at breaker | Test capacitor, then motor |
| No sound at all from unit | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test power, replace contactor |
| Fan blade physically stuck | Debris or ice jam | Power off, look for debris | Clear jam, inspect motor |
| Burning smell near unit | Burned fan motor windings | Shut off immediately | Replace fan motor |
| Fan starts if pushed by hand | Weak capacitor or worn bearings | Don't rely on it | Replace capacitor and/or motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor. Prices vary by region and tonnage.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400. The part is $15–$40; most cost is the service call and labor.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $350–$650, depending on horsepower and whether a matching motor is available.
- **Clearing a jammed blade:** $90–$180 (mostly the diagnostic/service fee).
- **Control board (communicating XV units):** $400–$800+ — confirm the diagnosis first, as boards are pricey and rarely the true culprit.
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $75–$180, often credited toward the repair.

DIY note: A capacitor is cheap, but replacing it means discharging a lethal charge and working near 240V. Only attempt it if you're trained and comfortable — otherwise this is a pro job.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on the control board — it's the symptom homeowners describe when a Lennox air conditioner runs (or tries to run) but the house stays warm. Depending on your setup, an iComfort thermostat may show a service reminder or a related outdoor-unit alert, while older Lennox systems with a conventional thermostat show nothing at all.

The problem falls into a few buckets: the system isn't starting, it's starting but not moving heat (airflow or refrigerant), or it's cooling weakly. This page helps you narrow it down and sanity-check any repair quote. Because behavior differs across Lennox models and thermostat pairings, we flag where things vary rather than guess.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common reason for weak or no cooling. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor coil solid.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setpoint, or a wiring/comms fault with iComfort systems.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser and indoor air handler are usually on separate circuits.
4. **Tripped condensate float switch** — a full or clogged drain pan cuts power to prevent water damage; the system won't cool until it's cleared.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant line.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a common failure that stops the compressor or fan motor from starting (often a humming or clicking outdoor unit).
7. **Dirty condenser coil** — a caked outdoor coil can't reject heat, so cooling drops off, especially on hot days.
8. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the compressor gets pitted and stops making contact.
9. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice, or a unit that runs constantly. Requires a licensed tech.
10. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive outcome, typically after other issues go unaddressed.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop before anything involving refrigerant, electrical components, or safety switches.

- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Verify the thermostat.** Set it to **Cool**, drop the setpoint 3–5°F below room temperature, and confirm the fan is on **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breakers.** Look for tripped breakers for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Look for ice.** If the indoor coil or the copper refrigerant line is iced up, turn the system to **Off** and run the fan only for a few hours to thaw it. Cooling with a frozen coil can damage the compressor.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A full drain pan or a tripped float switch stops cooling. Clear standing water and check that the drain line isn't clogged. Drain tablets help prevent buildup.
- **Confirm airflow at vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.

Do **not** open the outdoor unit's electrical panel, add refrigerant, or bypass a float or safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works methodically — here's what a legitimate visit looks like so you can judge the quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** and checks thermostat settings, mode, and call for cooling.
2. **Verifies power** to both indoor and outdoor units and checks the disconnect.
3. **Inspects airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition; a frozen coil is thawed and investigated.
4. **Tests electrical components** — capacitor microfarad reading, contactor operation, and motor amp draw.
5. **Attaches refrigerant gauges** and measures superheat/subcooling to judge the charge. Low readings prompt a **leak search** (dye, electronic detector, or bubble test).
6. **Checks the compressor** for proper start-up and windings if it won't run.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "top off" refrigerant without looking for a leak — refrigerant doesn't get consumed, so low charge means it's leaking somewhere.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower, ductwork |
| Ice on indoor coil/line | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw with fan | Diagnose airflow or leak |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None (electrical) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker/float switch | Reset breaker once, clear drain | Trace circuit or switch fault |
| Runs constantly, cools poorly | Low refrigerant or dirty condenser coil | Rinse debris off outdoor coil (power off) | Leak search & recharge |
| Thermostat blank/unresponsive | Dead batteries or comms fault | Replace batteries | Test C-wire / iComfort link |
| Water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, check float switch |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Bad capacitor or motor | None (electrical) | Test capacitor / fan motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Regional rates and model vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 professional
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$300
- **Blower or condenser fan motor:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher for R-410A on newer units and more with buried leaks)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,200+ (often worth weighing against full unit replacement on older systems)

If your Lennox unit is still under its **factory parts warranty** (typically 5–10 years, registration-dependent), covered parts can significantly cut these costs — you'll usually still pay labor and refrigerant.

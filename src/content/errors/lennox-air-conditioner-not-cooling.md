## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Lennox air conditioner runs but the house never reaches the set temperature, or the air from the vents isn't cold. On newer Lennox systems with iComfort or S30/S40 thermostats, you may see an actual alert or error code on the display that points to a demand-not-met condition, a communication fault, or an outdoor unit lockout. On older Merit and Elite units with a standard thermostat, there's no display code at all — just warm air.

The root cause falls into one of a few buckets: airflow problems, refrigerant problems, electrical component failure (capacitor, contactor, compressor), or a control/thermostat issue. This page walks through each so you can tell a real problem from a five-minute fix, and sanity-check any quote you're given.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, cutting cooling and often icing the coil.
2. **Thermostat setting or wiring issue** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," wrong schedule, dead batteries, or a tripped/mis-wired thermostat.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the outdoor fan or compressor won't start, so no heat is rejected outside.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with dirt, grass, or cottonwood can't dump heat, so cooling drops off.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharged systems can't absorb heat; often paired with ice on the lines or long run times.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks cooling entirely until it thaws.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the condenser never energizes.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor disconnect or 24V control fuse has failed.
9. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive outcome, usually after long neglect or repeated hard starts.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the outdoor unit's electrical panel, touch refrigerant lines, or add refrigerant.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of poor cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool** and the setpoint is at least a few degrees below room temperature. On battery-powered thermostats, replace the batteries.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping means a real electrical fault.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the pull-out disconnect box next to the outdoor unit is fully seated.
- **Supply and return vents:** Confirm they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Closed vents starve airflow.
- **Condensate drain:** If your indoor unit has a float switch and the drain is clogged, the system may shut off cooling. You can pour a cup of water down the drain line access or use drain tablets, but don't disassemble the pan.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, **turn the system off** (set thermostat to Off, fan to On) and let it thaw fully before running again. Running an iced unit can damage the compressor.

If none of these fix it, it's a pro-level job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will work through a logical sequence — this is what you're paying for, and it helps you judge whether a quote makes sense:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirm the thermostat is sending 24V to the outdoor unit and reads correctly.
2. **Check the outdoor unit power** — measure voltage at the disconnect and contactor; test the run/start capacitor with a meter.
3. **Test electrical components** — inspect the contactor for pitting, verify the compressor and fan motor draw correct amperage.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures** — connect gauges to the service ports to measure suction and liquid pressures, and calculate superheat/subcooling. This reveals undercharge (leak), overcharge, or a restriction.
5. **Inspect the coils and airflow** — check the condenser and evaporator for dirt, and measure the temperature split across the indoor coil (a healthy split is roughly 14–22°F).
6. **On iComfort/S30 systems** — pull active and stored codes from the thermostat's diagnostics menu, which can point to specific faults.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add refrigerant" without leak-testing — that's a temporary patch, not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, unit running | Dirty filter or low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp split, check blower |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or contactor | None (electrical) | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, let thaw fully | Find cause, leak-test, recharge |
| Indoor blower runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker/disconnect | Reset breaker once, reseat disconnect | Diagnose electrical fault |
| Runs constantly, never reaches setpoint | Low refrigerant or dirty condenser | Clean debris from around unit | Pressure test, clean coil, repair leak |
| No cool air, thermostat blank | Dead batteries or control fuse | Replace batteries | Replace 24V fuse, find short |
| Loud hum then shutdown outside | Failing compressor or hard start | None | Test compressor, amp draw |
| Water near indoor unit, cooling stops | Clogged condensate drain, float trip | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, check float switch |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US ballparks for a Lennox system; your region, unit age, and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22, which is expensive) all affect price.

- **Air filter:** $0–$40 DIY
- **Thermostat batteries / settings:** $0–$15 DIY
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0–$150 (DIY tablets to pro service)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 (higher for R-22 or evaporator/condenser coil leaks)
- **TXV / metering device:** $500–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $2,000–$2,800+ (often worth weighing against full system replacement on older units)
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$200, frequently credited toward the repair

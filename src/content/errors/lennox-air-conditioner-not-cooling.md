## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Lennox air conditioner is running (or trying to run) but the house isn't getting cooler. On communicating **iComfort** systems you may see an alert or a numbered error at the thermostat; on older **Merit and Elite** units with a standard contactor board, there's often no code at all — just warm air.

Because "not cooling" can point to anything from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, this page ranks the likely causes and shows you what's safely checkable versus what needs a licensed HVAC tech. Refrigerant, electrical, and compressor work are **pro-only** — that's why this is rated a pro-severity problem.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, ices the indoor coil, and kills cooling capacity. Most common and cheapest fix.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, cottonwood, and dust blanket the coil so it can't shed heat. Very common in summer.
3. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — set to "fan" instead of "cool," wrong schedule, or dead batteries on a battery-powered stat.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit has lost power while the indoor blower still runs.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start; a bulged or blown capacitor is a frequent culprit.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the condenser never engages.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced capacity, ice on the lines, longer run times. Requires leak repair, not just a top-off.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks the coil until it thaws.
9. **Clogged condensate drain / float switch** — a full pan or tripped safety float shuts the system down to prevent water damage.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the outdoor unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or touching wiring.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter alone causes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the target is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model. Try lowering it 3–4 degrees and listen for the outdoor unit to start.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. You may reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — don't keep resetting.
- **Vents & registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or leaves smothering the condenser. Clear the area around it. Do **not** remove panels or spray the electrical components.
- **Condensate drain:** If your indoor unit sits over a drain pan and it's full of water, the float switch has likely shut the system off. Clearing a visible pan of standing water can restore operation; if it refills fast, the drain line is clogged and needs service.
- **Check for ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** (fan can stay on) and let it thaw fully before calling — this helps the tech diagnose faster.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Test electrical** — measures voltage at the contactor, tests the capacitor with a meter, and checks the contactor for pitting or stuck contacts.
4. **Read pressures** — connects gauges to measure high/low side refrigerant pressures and superheat/subcooling. Abnormal readings point to a charge or leak problem.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to locate the leak before adding refrigerant.
6. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw and windings if the compressor won't start or trips the breaker.
7. **iComfort systems** — pulls stored fault codes from the communicating control to confirm the failure.

A reputable tech should show you gauge readings or the failed part. Be cautious of anyone who "just adds refrigerant" without a leak search — that's a temporary and non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air from vents, indoor fan runs | Dirty filter or thermostat set wrong | Replace filter, verify COOL mode | Airflow and control check |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker or failed contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, check wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — capacitors hold a charge | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on refrigerant lines or coil | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Weak cooling, long run times | Low refrigerant or dirty condenser | Rinse outdoor coil gently, clean area | Pressure test and leak search |
| Water around indoor unit, system off | Clogged condensate drain / float | Clear standing water in pan | Clear drain line, test float switch |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted compressor or wiring fault | Stop resetting, call pro | Amp/winding test, compressor eval |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0–$10 (DIY)
- **Outdoor coil cleaning:** $75–$250 (service visit)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$2,000 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,900
- **Compressor replacement:** $2,000–$3,500+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

Diagnostic/service call fees typically run $80–$180 and are sometimes credited toward the repair.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint homeowners have about a Lennox air conditioner. Your thermostat calls for cooling, but the air coming out of the vents is warm, weak, or the house never reaches the setpoint.

On basic single-stage Lennox units (many Merit and Elite models), there's no display to give you a code — you diagnose by behavior. On communicating **iComfort** systems, the thermostat may show an alert or the outdoor control board may store a fault. If your Lennox is throwing a numbered code, look that up specifically; this page covers the general "runs but won't cool" and "won't run at all" scenarios.

Because refrigerant, compressor and electrical faults are common causes here, we've marked this **pro** severity. Several checks are safe to do yourself, but the repair often needs a licensed technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause of weak or no cooling. Restricted airflow drops capacity and can freeze the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. Ice blocks heat transfer, so air blows but stays warm.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common outdoor-unit failure. The fan and/or compressor won't start, or hums and trips.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Lennox units are sealed; low charge always means a leak, not "topping off."
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with dirt, grass or cottonwood can't reject heat.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit gets pitted or stuck, so the compressor never energizes.
7. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor fan doesn't spin, compressor overheats and shuts off on internal protection.
8. **Thermostat or wiring fault** — no cooling call reaches the equipment; common after a battery dies or a wire loosens.
9. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor disconnect or the 3–5 amp fuse on the control board.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the unit runs but never produces cold refrigerant.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening a sealed panel or touching capacitors.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers in your panel. Reset one that's tripped **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a tech.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the thermostat to **Off**, set the fan to **On**, and let it thaw for 2–4 hours before running cooling again. Running iced is what damages compressors.
- **Condensate line:** A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and shut cooling down. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear the line if you're comfortable doing so.

Do **not** repeatedly reset a breaker that keeps tripping, open the outdoor unit's electrical panel, or attempt to add refrigerant.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path — here's what a fair diagnosis looks like so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verifies the complaint** and checks the thermostat call and 24V signal reaching the equipment.
2. **Inspects airflow** — filter, blower operation, and whether the coil is iced.
3. **Measures temperature split** (return vs. supply air); a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F.
4. **Checks capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads.
5. **Tests the contactor and fan motor** for proper voltage in and out.
6. **Attaches gauges and reads pressures/superheat/subcooling** to judge refrigerant charge and look for a leak.
7. **On iComfort systems, reads stored fault codes** from the board or thermostat.
8. **Amp-draws the compressor** to confirm it's actually running and pumping.

A quote should name the failed part and the measured reason for it — not just "it's low on Freon."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil on fan-only | Confirm airflow, check charge |
| Air blows but never cold | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call a tech | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cooling | Bad capacitor or compressor | Note behavior only | Test capacitor, amp compressor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Tripped breaker / contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, low-voltage fuse |
| Unit hums but won't start | Failed capacitor | None | Replace capacitor |
| Ice on refrigerant lines | Low airflow or low charge | Filter + thaw | Diagnose airflow vs. charge |
| Breaker trips on startup | Electrical short / bad compressor | Do not keep resetting | Isolate fault, test compressor |
| No cooling call at all | Thermostat / wiring | New batteries, check settings | Trace 24V wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Prices vary by region and unit size.

- **Air filter:** $10 – $40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0 – $20 (DIY)
- **Condensate line clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 professional
- **Coil cleaning (condenser):** $100 – $300
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150 – $400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400 – $700 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500 – $1,800 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,200 – $2,500+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500 – $2,800+ (often worth comparing to a new system on older units)

If your Lennox is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, ask for a replacement-system quote too — the math sometimes favors a new unit.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a numbered fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Lennox air conditioner runs but the house won't reach the setpoint, or the air from the vents feels warm or only mildly cool. On communicating Lennox systems with an iComfort thermostat, you may see a related alert or a diagnostic code stored in the thermostat's service menu, but many cooling problems produce no code at all because the equipment believes it is operating normally.

A Lennox AC that isn't cooling usually falls into one of three buckets: **airflow problems** (dirty filter, frozen coil, blower issues), **heat-rejection problems** (dirty condenser, failed fan, bad capacitor), or **refrigerant/compressor problems** (leaks, a stuck contactor, or a dead compressor). The severity is rated **pro** because most true "no cooling" faults involve refrigerant or high-voltage components that homeowners should not service.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can freeze the evaporator solid.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from a dirty filter, low airflow, or low refrigerant; ice blocks all cooling.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — a coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't dump heat.
4. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a miscalibrated/failed iComfort thermostat.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan hums but won't start; a very common pro repair.
7. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit fails to close, so the compressor never runs.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces capacity and can freeze the coil; requires certified repair.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor overheats and shuts down on its internal limit.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome, often the end of the road for older units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Stop here if anything looks damaged or if the unit keeps tripping the breaker.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the single most common cause of weak or no cooling.
- **Breaker:** Check the breaker for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. If a breaker has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, **stop and call a pro** — do not keep resetting.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking airflow. All return grilles should be unobstructed.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for a thick layer of dirt, grass, or leaves on the condenser fins, and clear away weeds or debris within two feet of the unit. Do not remove panels.
- **Ice on the lines:** If you see ice on the copper refrigerant line or the indoor coil, **turn the system to Off (fan Auto)** and let it thaw for a few hours. Running a frozen unit can damage the compressor.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trigger a float switch that shuts down cooling. Check that the drain pan isn't overflowing; drain tablets can help prevent buildup.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Lennox technician follows a repeatable sequence, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirms the thermostat is sending a Y (cooling) signal and the indoor blower runs.
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, coil, and blower; measures temperature split across the evaporator (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
3. **Inspect the outdoor unit** — tests the contactor, run capacitor, and condenser fan motor with a multimeter.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to check suction and liquid pressures and superheat/subcooling. Abnormal readings point to a leak or a metering/compressor issue.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, a reputable tech finds the leak (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just "topping off."
6. **Check the compressor** — measures amp draw, windings, and start components before condemning it.
7. **Pull stored codes** on iComfort/communicating systems via the thermostat or the SureLight/control board.

Be wary of any quote that recommends adding refrigerant without a leak search, or replacing a compressor without proving the old one has failed.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vents blow warm, outdoor unit not running | Tripped breaker, bad contactor or capacitor | Reset breaker once; check thermostat mode | Test/replace contactor or capacitor |
| Weak airflow, house won't cool | Clogged filter or dirty coil | Replace filter, clear vents | Clean evaporator/condenser coils |
| Ice on refrigerant line or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find/repair leak, recharge to spec |
| Outdoor fan spins but air stays warm | Low refrigerant or failing compressor | None — call a pro | Check pressures, leak search, compressor test |
| Compressor hums then stops | Failed run capacitor | None — call a pro | Replace capacitor |
| Unit trips breaker repeatedly | Electrical short or seized compressor | Do not keep resetting | Diagnose short/compressor |
| Water around indoor unit, cooling stops | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US prices including labor; your region and system size affect the total.

- **Air filter (DIY):** $10 – $40
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150 – $400 (higher for communicating iComfort thermostats)
- **Run capacitor:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350 – $700
- **Coil cleaning (evaporator or condenser):** $150 – $450
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600 – $1,800 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000 – $2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500 – $2,500+ (often makes replacing an older unit more economical)

If your system uses older R-22 refrigerant, recharge costs are significantly higher and a full replacement is often the smarter long-term choice.

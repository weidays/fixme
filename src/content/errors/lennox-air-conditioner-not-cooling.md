## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom homeowners describe when a Lennox air conditioner is powered on and the indoor blower runs, but the air coming from the vents isn't cold (or isn't cold enough). Depending on your system, the control board may or may not log an actual error.

- On **standard 24V Lennox units** (Merit and many Elite models), there's usually no display, so "not cooling" is diagnosed by symptoms and meter readings — not a screen code.
- On **communicating iComfort systems** (Signature series with iComfort thermostats), the thermostat or the outdoor board may report a specific alert code (for example, a compressor lockout or a demand-not-met alert). If you see a numbered code, look it up specifically rather than treating it as a generic "not cooling" issue.

Bottom line: "not cooling" points to airflow, electrical, or refrigerant problems. Some you can rule out yourself; the refrigerant and compressor issues require a licensed technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, which reduces cooling and can freeze the coil solid.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or dead batteries** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," wrong schedule, or a battery-powered thermostat that's lost power.
3. **Tripped breaker to the outdoor unit** — the indoor blower runs on a separate circuit, so you get airflow but no compressor.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** (outdoor unit) — dirt, grass, and cottonwood block heat rejection, so the system can't cool.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or outdoor fan won't start; often a buzzing or humming outdoor unit.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks cooling entirely.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — gradual loss of cooling, higher bills, possible hissing or icing. Requires a pro.
8. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that energizes the outdoor unit doesn't pull in.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit tries to start but the compressor is seized or electrically dead.
10. **Control board or communicating fault** (iComfort) — a board or thermostat communication error preventing a cooling call.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here if none of them fix it.

- **Check the thermostat.** Set the mode to **Cool** and the temperature a few degrees below room temperature. Set the fan to **Auto**. If it's battery-powered, replace the batteries.
- **Replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the #1 cause. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, swap it. Then give a frozen coil time — see below.
- **Check both breakers.** There's usually one for the indoor air handler/furnace and one for the outdoor condenser. Reset any tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Look for ice.** If you see ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** (fan can stay on **Auto**) and let it thaw for a few hours before restarting. Persistent icing means a real problem.
- **Clear the outdoor unit.** Turn it off at the breaker, then remove leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Make sure there's at least 2 feet of clearance. Do not remove panels or spray water into electrical components.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and stop cooling. If your indoor unit's drain pan is full, the line is likely clogged.
- **Check supply and return vents.** Make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the process helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the cooling call** — confirms the thermostat is sending a Y (cooling) signal and, on iComfort systems, reads any stored fault codes.
2. **Check airflow** — inspects the filter, blower, and evaporator coil; measures temperature split (return vs. supply air).
3. **Measure electrical** — tests the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, and verifies voltage at the compressor and fan motor.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to measure suction and liquid pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to determine charge level.
5. **Locate leaks** — if the charge is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find where refrigerant is escaping (a simple refill without leak repair is a red flag).
6. **Test the compressor** — checks windings and amp draw to determine if it's electrically failed or seized.

A good tech will show you the readings — not just quote a part.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor fan runs, air not cold | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp split, clean coil |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Outdoor unit buzzes, fan won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find cause, check charge |
| Gradual loss of cooling, high bills | Refrigerant leak | None | Locate leak, repair, recharge |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or no power | Replace batteries, check breaker | Check wiring/transformer |
| Unit short-cycles on and off | Capacitor, contactor, or control fault | Note the pattern | Diagnose electrical/board |
| Full drain pan, water leaking | Clogged condensate line | Clear line, use tablets | Clear clog, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** under $10 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 installed
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often worth comparing to a full system replacement on older units)
- **Control board (iComfort):** $400–$900

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type (R-410A units cost more to recharge), and whether the part is under Lennox warranty. If your unit is over 12–15 years old and needs a compressor or coil, ask about replacement quotes too.

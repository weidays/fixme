## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Lennox air conditioner runs but the house stays warm, or the unit won't start a cooling cycle at all. On Lennox systems with a communicating **iComfort** thermostat, you may see an active alert or fault code stored on the thermostat or outdoor control board. On older or non-communicating Lennox units (standard 24V control), there's no display — you diagnose by behavior.

The root problem falls into one of a few buckets: airflow is blocked, the electrical components that drive the compressor and fan have failed, the refrigerant charge is low from a leak, or the control/thermostat isn't calling for cooling correctly. Some of these are safe to check yourself; the refrigerant and compressor issues are strictly professional work.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, freezes the indoor coil, and kills cooling output. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** (the outdoor unit) — caked with dust, grass, or cottonwood, it can't reject heat.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a failed thermostat not calling for cool.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure. Outdoor fan or compressor won't start, or hums and shuts off.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor disconnect fuse or panel breaker off.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — weak or no cooling, ice on the copper lines, longer run times. Requires a certified tech.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; blows warm air until it thaws.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor or compressor** — the most expensive failures.
10. **Control board or wiring fault** — more likely to log a code on iComfort systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. Stop at anything requiring you to open sealed electrical panels or touch refrigerant.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling.
- **Breaker/disconnect:** Check the AC breaker in your electrical panel. Both the furnace/air handler circuit and the outdoor AC circuit must be on. Flip a tripped breaker fully off, then on — once.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Blocked airflow can freeze the coil.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch. A clogged condensate line can shut the system down. Clear standing water only if you can reach it safely.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or a blanket of cottonwood on the coil. Gently hose the outside of the coil with the power OFF at the disconnect. Never remove panels or straighten fins with power on.

If the unit still won't cool after these checks, or you see ice on the refrigerant lines, it's time for a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirms 24V signal from the thermostat reaches the outdoor unit and, on iComfort systems, reads any stored fault codes.
2. **Check airflow and coils** — inspects filter, blower, and both coils for restriction or ice.
3. **Electrical testing** — measures the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, and confirms line voltage at the disconnect.
4. **Amp draw** — clamps the compressor and fan motor to see if they're pulling correct current or locked/failing.
5. **Refrigerant gauges** — connects manifold gauges to read high- and low-side pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to judge the charge.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, uses electronic detector or dye to find the leak before adding refrigerant.

A good tech won't just "top off" refrigerant without finding the leak — that's a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blows warm air, unit running | Dirty filter/coil or low charge | Replace filter, clean outdoor coil | Check charge, find leak, service coils |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Failed capacitor or compressor | None — call a pro | Test/replace capacitor or compressor |
| Ice on copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter, turn off to thaw | Diagnose charge, repair leak |
| Nothing turns on | Tripped breaker, dead thermostat | Reset breaker once, swap batteries | Test contactor, board, wiring |
| Unit hums then shuts off | Weak capacitor or stuck contactor | None — call a pro | Replace capacitor/contactor |
| Short cycling | Frozen coil, control fault | Thaw coil, replace filter | Check charge, board, and sensors |
| Water around indoor unit | Clogged condensate/float switch | Clear drain if reachable | Clear line, test float safety |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$450
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Condensate line clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (varies by leak location)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth weighing against a new unit if out of warranty)

Lennox parts are covered under a limited warranty (typically 5–10 years on registered units), which can significantly lower parts cost — but labor is usually still billed.

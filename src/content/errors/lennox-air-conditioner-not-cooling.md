## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a fault code in the classic sense — it's the symptom you notice when your Lennox air conditioner runs (or tries to run) but your home never reaches the thermostat setpoint. Depending on your system, the control board or thermostat may or may not display a specific error.

On conventional 24V Lennox systems (many Merit and Elite units), there's often no digital diagnostic display at all — you diagnose by behavior. On communicating **iComfort** thermostats paired with Signature-series equipment, you may see an alert or error code on the thermostat screen that points to a specific subsystem (airflow, compressor, or sensor fault). Because the exact readouts vary by board and thermostat firmware, treat any code you see as a starting clue, not a diagnosis — confirm the meaning against your unit's manual.

The bottom line: cooling loss can come from airflow, refrigerant, electrical, or mechanical failures. Several of these require a licensed technician and refrigerant certification, which is why this is rated **pro**.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or restricted airflow.** The single most common cause. Low airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or failing.** Wrong mode (heat vs. cool), a dead battery, or a bad C-wire connection can stop a cooling call.
3. **Tripped breaker or lost power to the outdoor unit.** If the condenser has no power, the indoor blower may still run and blow warm air.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor.** A very common outdoor-unit failure that keeps the compressor or condenser fan from starting.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil.** Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer and cooling stops.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** Refrigerant doesn't get "used up" — low charge means a leak that a pro must find and repair.
7. **Dirty condenser coil.** A caked outdoor coil can't reject heat, so the system runs but barely cools.
8. **Contactor failure.** A pitted or stuck contactor prevents the outdoor unit from energizing.
9. **Blower motor or capacitor failure (indoor).** No indoor airflow means no cool air at the vents.
10. **Compressor failure.** The most expensive cause; the unit hums or trips but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If none of them fix it, stop and call a pro.

- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or overdue, replace it. This is the #1 fix and costs a few dollars.
- **Verify the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check your breakers.** Look for a tripped breaker for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset it once. If it trips again, stop — that's an electrical fault for a pro.
- **Look at your supply and return vents.** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and shut down cooling. Look for standing water in the drain pan; drain-cleaning tablets can help prevent future clogs.
- **Look for ice.** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** and set the fan to **On** to thaw it (can take several hours), then call a technician — ice means an airflow or refrigerant problem.

Do **not** open the outdoor unit's electrical panel, add refrigerant, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap-and-likely to expensive:

1. **Confirms the complaint** — measures the temperature split (return vs. supply air); a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F.
2. **Inspects airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition.
3. **Checks electrical** — tests the capacitor(s), contactor, and voltage at the outdoor unit with a meter.
4. **Reads any thermostat/board codes** — on iComfort systems, pulls fault history for airflow or compressor alerts.
5. **Connects refrigerant gauges** — checks pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge charge and find whether the system is low (a leak) or has another mechanical issue.
6. **Leak search if low** — dye, electronic sniffer, or nitrogen pressure test to locate the leak before any recharge.
7. **Tests the compressor** — amp draw and windings if all else checks out.

If a quote jumps straight to "recharge" without a leak search, or to "compressor replacement" without capacitor/electrical testing first, ask questions.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower, ductwork |
| No cooling, thermostat blank | Dead thermostat / C-wire | Replace batteries | Repair wiring, replace stat |
| Indoor blows warm, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or no power | Reset breaker once | Diagnose electrical fault |
| Outdoor fan runs, compressor won't start | Failed capacitor | None (electrical) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or coil | Frozen evaporator | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Runs constantly, barely cools | Low refrigerant leak | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor coil caked with dirt | Dirty condenser | Gently rinse fins with hose | Deep coil cleaning |
| Humming, trips breaker, no cooling | Compressor failure | None | Test & replace compressor |
| Standing water, system off | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 installed
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher for R-410A/newer refrigerants and hard-to-reach leaks)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often worth comparing to full system replacement on older units)

If your unit is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison — Lennox parts warranties (typically 5–10 years on registered systems) may cover the part even when labor is not.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single fault code — it's a symptom. Your Lennox air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, thermostat calling) but the air coming out of your vents is barely cool or the house never reaches setpoint. On iComfort S30/E30 controllers you may see an active alert or a specific alarm code; with a standard thermostat there's no code at all, just warm air.

The underlying cause falls into one of a few buckets: reduced airflow (filter, coils, blower), a refrigerant problem (leak or low charge), an electrical/component failure (capacitor, contactor, compressor), or a controls issue (thermostat, wiring, board). Some of these are safe homeowner checks; the rest are licensed-technician territory because they involve refrigerant or high-voltage components.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil solid.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, cottonwood, and dirt block heat rejection so the system can't dump heat outside.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks airflow entirely and you get warm or no air.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown outdoor disconnect** — the indoor blower runs but the condenser is dead, so you circulate warm air.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor and/or fan motor won't start, often with a humming outdoor unit.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck and won't close.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity and freezes coils; requires leak detection and repair, not just a "top-off."
8. **Thermostat or iComfort configuration/wiring fault** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a communication error on iComfort systems.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit runs but produces no cooling, sometimes tripping the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. On battery models, replace the batteries.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 avoidable cause.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and the outdoor condenser breakers in your panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** Turn the system off and gently rinse leaves and grass off the condenser fins with a garden hose (low pressure, top down). Keep 2 ft of clearance around the unit.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF but leave the fan ON to thaw it (can take a few hours), then replace the filter.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water in the pan and clear a visible clog.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, remove electrical panels on the condenser, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — knowing it helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirms thermostat/iComfort is actually signaling the outdoor unit (measures 24V at the contactor).
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Electrical checks** — measures the run capacitor's microfarads against the rating, inspects the contactor, and checks amp draw on the compressor and fan motor.
4. **Refrigerant readings** — connects gauges to read suction/liquid pressures and takes superheat/subcooling measurements to determine if the charge is correct.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak (it should never just be "topped off").
6. **Controls/board** — on iComfort systems, reads active and stored alarm codes from the S30/E30 and checks communication wiring.

Expect a technician to spend 30–60 minutes diagnosing before quoting a repair. Be wary of anyone who adds refrigerant without finding the leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or failed contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, find trip cause |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (high voltage) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Weak cooling, ice on lines/coil | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace filter, thaw coil | Check charge, find and fix leak |
| House never reaches setpoint on hot days | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Rinse condenser, check clearance | Clean coil, verify charge |
| No cooling, standing water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, verify float switch |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries or wiring/comms fault | Replace batteries | Diagnose wiring / iComfort comms |
| Runs constantly, breaker trips repeatedly | Failing compressor | Stop resetting | Test compressor, quote replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and by model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 (more for iComfort S30/E30)
- **Refrigerant leak repair:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Refrigerant recharge (with leak fixed):** $200–$700+
- **Fan motor replacement:** $300–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,200+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

If your unit uses R-22 refrigerant (older systems), recharge costs are significantly higher due to the phaseout — factor this into repair-vs-replace decisions.

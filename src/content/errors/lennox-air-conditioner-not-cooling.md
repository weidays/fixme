## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific numeric fault code on most Lennox equipment — it's a symptom. Depending on your system, the air conditioner may be running with no cold air, short-cycling, or not turning on at all. On communicating **iComfort** systems (Signature and some Elite models), the thermostat may display a specific alert or an equipment error, while older **Merit** and single-stage units with a standard 24V control board give no code at all.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, the goal here is to help you rule out the easy, safe stuff before you pay for a service call — and to help you sanity-check the diagnosis if you do.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops efficiency, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode/setpoint, dead batteries, or a failed thermostat not calling for cooling.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the indoor coil or copper lines and warm air from vents.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — very common on Lennox condensers; the compressor or condenser fan hums but won't start.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the condenser gets no power, so only the indoor blower runs.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling that gets worse over time; requires a licensed tech.
7. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling capacity drops in hot weather.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the outdoor fan doesn't spin, causing the compressor to overheat and trip.
9. **Contactor failure** — worn or pitted contactor won't energize the condenser.
10. **Compressor failure** — the worst case; the unit draws power but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or touch capacitors — they store a dangerous charge even with power off.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If it's gray or you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering it 3–4 degrees to force a call for cooling.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser may have separate breakers). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers, and that return grilles are clear.
- **Condensate drain/pan:** A full drain pan can trip a safety float switch that shuts the system down. If you see standing water, the drain line is likely clogged — a common cause on humid days.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper line at the outdoor unit, turn the system OFF (fan can stay ON) and let it thaw for a few hours before running again. Persistent freezing means low airflow or low refrigerant — call a tech.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — knowing it helps you spot a rushed or padded quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat call, filter, and airflow first.
2. **Measure voltage** at the condenser contactor and confirm the unit is getting power.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter (a cheap, common failure they should always check).
4. **Check the contactor and fan motor** for the outdoor unit.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and measure suction/liquid pressures plus **superheat and subcooling** — this tells them if the charge is correct or if there's a leak or restriction.
6. **Inspect coils** (indoor and outdoor) for dirt and airflow blockage.
7. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low — using electronic detectors or dye, not just a top-off.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "add a pound of Freon" without measuring pressures or finding the leak. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up" — if it's low, there's a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor fan runs, air isn't cold | Dirty filter, frozen coil, low refrigerant | Replace filter; check for ice and thaw | Measure charge, find/repair leak |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None (capacitor holds charge) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker, bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Ice on coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter, turn off to thaw | Check charge, airflow, blower |
| Weak cooling on hot days only | Dirty condenser coil, low charge | Rinse outdoor coil gently with hose | Clean coil, verify charge |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed condenser fan motor or capacitor | None | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| System keeps tripping breaker | Electrical fault or failing compressor | Stop resetting; call pro | Diagnose short/compressor |
| Nothing runs at all | Thermostat, control board, power | Check batteries, mode, breaker | Test board and low-voltage wiring |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your cost varies by region, model, and refrigerant type (older R-410A vs newer systems).

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $100–$350 depending on model; iComfort communicating thermostats cost more
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant amount)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often makes replacing the whole condenser worth considering)
- **Full condenser/system replacement:** $4,000–$12,000+

If your unit is over 12–15 years old and needs a compressor or major refrigerant work, ask about replacement — the repair may not be worth it, especially with older refrigerant.

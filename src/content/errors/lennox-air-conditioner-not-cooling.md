## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single fault code — it's a symptom. Your Lennox air conditioner may be running (the outdoor unit hums, the indoor blower moves air) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

On **communicating iComfort systems**, you may see a specific alert or fault code on the thermostat screen that points to a demand mismatch, compressor lockout, or sensor fault. On **older, non-communicating Lennox units** (most Merit and many Elite installs), there's no display — you're diagnosing purely from behavior.

Because "not cooling" can stem from anything from a $20 filter to a $2,000 compressor, this page walks you from cheapest and safest to most serious.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — Restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, causing it to freeze or the system to short-cycle. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat settings or wiring** — Set to "fan" instead of "cool," wrong temperature, dead batteries, or a scheduling conflict.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — Grass, leaves, and dirt block heat rejection so the unit can't cool efficiently.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — A very common electrical failure. The compressor or fan hums but won't start, or the fan spins while the compressor stays off.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Warm air, ice on the copper lines, and long run times. Requires a certified tech; never a DIY fix.
6. **Failed contactor** — The relay that sends power to the compressor pits and burns, so the outdoor unit won't energize.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Result of low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil turns to a block of ice and blocks all cooling.
8. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — The most expensive outcome; often preceded by breaker trips or a burnt-electrical smell.
9. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — Outdoor unit gets no power at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are homeowner-safe. Stop at anything requiring you to open the electrical panel of the AC or touch refrigerant lines.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Check monthly during cooling season.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and (for battery models) swap in fresh batteries.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (unit powered off at the breaker).
- **Condensate drain:** If the indoor drain pan is full or overflowing, a clogged drain line can trigger a float switch that shuts down cooling. Check for standing water.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, **turn the system off** and let it fully thaw (several hours) before running again — and change that filter.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A quote should reflect a logical diagnostic sequence. Here's what a competent Lennox tech does:

1. Verifies the complaint and checks thermostat call for cooling with a meter.
2. Inspects the filter and measures airflow across the evaporator coil.
3. Checks incoming voltage, the contactor, and tests the **capacitor with a multimeter** (a bad cap is a fast, cheap find).
4. Reads iComfort fault history on communicating systems.
5. Connects gauges to measure **suction and liquid pressures** and calculates superheat/subcooling — this reveals a refrigerant charge or leak problem.
6. If low on refrigerant, performs a **leak search** (electronic detector or dye) rather than just topping off.
7. Tests compressor windings and amp draw if a compressor fault is suspected.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without pressure readings and a leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but isn't cold | Dirty filter, low refrigerant, dirty coil | Replace filter, clean condenser | Check charge, leak search, clean coils |
| Outdoor unit silent, no power | Tripped breaker, blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, transformer, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan/compressor won't start | Failed capacitor | None — electrical | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, change filter | Diagnose airflow/charge, find leak |
| System short-cycles | Clogged filter, oversized load, control fault | Replace filter | Read fault codes, check controls |
| Indoor blower runs, outdoor dead | Contactor, capacitor, or float switch | Check drain pan for water | Test outdoor components |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor/electrical fault | Stop resetting, call pro | Test windings, amp draw, wiring |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices including labor; your region and unit tier affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 (depends on leak location; coil leaks cost more)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,500+ (often the point where whole-unit replacement makes more sense)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400

If your Lennox unit is 12+ years old and facing a compressor or coil failure, ask your tech to compare the repair cost against a new system — repairs above roughly $1,500 on an aging unit often favor replacement.

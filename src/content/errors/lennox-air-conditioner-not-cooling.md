## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom homeowners describe most often. Your Lennox air conditioner may run, blow air, and even sound normal, but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

On newer Lennox systems paired with an **iComfort** thermostat, the equipment may log an actual alert or fault code you can read at the wall control. On older Merit-series units and non-communicating setups, there is no display — you diagnose by symptom only. This page covers the mechanical and electrical reasons a Lennox AC stops cooling, ranked by how often we see them.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — restricted airflow is the single most common cause. It reduces cooling and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or dead batteries** — set to "fan" instead of "cool," wrong programming, or a dead battery on battery-powered stats.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — if the outdoor unit has no power, the indoor blower runs but nothing cools.
4. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or coil, and warm air at vents.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or condenser fan won't start; you may hear humming or clicking at the outdoor unit.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling and coil freezing. Refrigerant does not get "used up," so low charge always means a leak.
7. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with dust, grass, or cottonwood, the unit can't reject heat.
8. **Failed contactor or capacitor relay** — outdoor unit won't energize.
9. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive cause; the unit runs the fan but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Turn the system to **off** at the thermostat before inspecting anything.

- **Filter:** Pull it. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "no cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or a covering blocking airflow. Gently clear leaves — don't bend the fins.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper lines, turn the AC **off** and set the fan to **On** to thaw it (can take hours). Running frozen makes it worse.
- **Condensate:** If your air handler has a float switch and the drain is clogged, the system may refuse to cool. Check for standing water in the drain pan.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work through this order so you can sanity-check the visit:

1. **Verify the complaint** — confirm airflow, thermostat call for cooling, and measure supply/return temperature split (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check power and controls** — voltage at the contactor, capacitor microfarad reading against the rated value, and thermostat call.
3. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil condition; identify any icing.
4. **Read pressures** — connect gauges to measure suction and liquid pressures and superheat/subcooling. This reveals low charge, restrictions, or a failing compressor.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, a proper diagnosis includes finding the leak (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test), not just adding gas.
6. **Component test** — contactor, capacitor, condenser fan motor, and compressor amp draw.

If a shop wants to "just add refrigerant" without a leak search, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vents blow warm, fan runs | Wrong thermostat setting, dirty filter | Set to Cool/Auto, replace filter | Verify call, check temp split |
| Ice on coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw, replace filter | Find restriction or leak, recharge |
| Outdoor unit humming, not spinning | Failed run capacitor | None — shut off | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, no sound | Tripped breaker, bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, wiring, transformer |
| Weak cooling, high energy bills | Dirty condenser coil, low charge | Clear debris around unit | Clean coil, check pressures |
| Runs constantly, never satisfies | Undersized/leaking charge, dirty coils | Replace filter, clear vents | Full charge and airflow diagnosis |
| Blower runs but no cooling at all | Compressor failure | None | Amp-test compressor, quote repair |
| System won't start, water in pan | Clogged condensate/float switch | Clear visible drain, use tablets | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual price varies by region and labor rates.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Service/diagnostic call:** $80–$200
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed
- **Coil cleaning (condenser or evaporator):** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$2,000+ depending on leak location; evaporator coil replacement can push this higher
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often makes replacing an older unit worth considering)
- **Full system replacement:** $5,000–$12,000+ for an aging or R-22 unit

Because Lennox coils and compressors can be pricey, always ask whether the repair is covered under the parts warranty (typically 5–10 years if registered).

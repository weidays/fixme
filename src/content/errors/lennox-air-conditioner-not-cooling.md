## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on the display — it's the symptom homeowners report most often with Lennox central air conditioners. The unit may be running, blowing air, or completely silent, but the house isn't getting cooler.

Because this covers a wide range of problems, the cause can be anything from a $15 air filter to a failed compressor. On newer communicating Lennox systems (XC20, XC21, XC25 with iComfort thermostats), the thermostat or outdoor control board may display an actual alert or error number — if yours does, look that specific code up, because it will narrow things down faster than this general guide.

Diagnosis behavior varies significantly by model. Single-stage units (ML14, EL16) are simpler; two-stage and variable-capacity units (XC16 and up) have more sensors and electronics that can also cause a "no cooling" complaint.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, ices the indoor coil, and kills cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser or evaporator coil** — a filthy outdoor coil can't reject heat; a frozen indoor coil blocks airflow entirely.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setpoint, or a wiring/programming issue.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler has lost power.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the lines, and eventually no cooling. Always indicates a leak, never "normal loss."
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a common electrical failure; the compressor or fan hums but won't start.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
8. **Clogged condensate drain** — a full pan can trip a float switch and shut off cooling.
9. **Bad compressor** — the most expensive failure; often the unit runs but produces no cold air.
10. **Control board or sensor fault** — more common on communicating XC20/XC25 systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or electrical panels.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Run the system 30 minutes afterward.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering it 3–4 degrees to force a call for cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler and outdoor unit. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a tech — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (never a pressure washer) with the power off.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water around the indoor unit or a float switch, the drain may be clogged. Drain-cleaning tablets and a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain line can help.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF but leave the fan ON to thaw it (can take several hours), then run with a clean filter.

If cooling doesn't return after a clean filter and a thawed coil, it's time for a professional.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verifies the complaint** and checks thermostat operation and settings.
2. **Confirms power** at the breaker, disconnect, contactor, and control board.
3. **Measures the capacitor** with a meter (a weak capacitor won't show visible damage).
4. **Checks the contactor** for pitting and proper pull-in.
5. **Takes temperature split** across the indoor coil (return vs. supply air, typically 15–20°F when healthy).
6. **Connects refrigerant gauges** and reads pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge charge. Low readings point to a leak.
7. **Performs a leak search** (electronic sniffer or dye) if refrigerant is low — recharging without finding the leak is a red flag.
8. **Tests the compressor** windings and amp draw if it won't start or run.
9. On **communicating XC20/XC25 systems**, pulls fault history from the iComfort thermostat or outdoor board.

Be cautious of any quote to "add a pound of Freon" with no leak search — that's a temporary fix on a system that's leaking.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but not cold | Dirty filter, low refrigerant, or bad compressor | Replace filter, check thermostat mode | Check charge, leak search, test compressor |
| Ice on copper lines/coil | Airflow restriction or low refrigerant | Turn off, fan on to thaw, new filter | Diagnose charge and airflow, fix leak |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, bad capacitor or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call a tech | Replace capacitor, verify compressor |
| Water/leak indoors, cooling stops | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear drain, use drain tablets | Clear line, verify float switch |
| Weak cooling, high energy bills | Dirty coils or low charge | Rinse outdoor coil, replace filter | Clean coils, check charge |
| iComfort shows an alert | Sensor/board/communication fault | Note the code | Diagnose per specific code |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor). Prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing rose sharply as it phases out)
- **Fan motor replacement:** $400–$700
- **Control board (communicating models):** $500–$1,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,200+ — often near the cost of a new outdoor unit, so weigh replacement if the system is older than 10–12 years

If your unit is under Lennox's parts warranty (typically 5–10 years registered), major parts like the compressor may be covered, but you'll still pay labor and refrigerant.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on a display — it's the symptom you're living with: the Lennox air conditioner is running (or trying to), but the house isn't getting cold. On modern Lennox systems with a communicating thermostat (like the iComfort platform), you may also see a specific alert or error number, but many single-stage and older units give you no code at all — just warm air.

This page covers the mechanical and airflow reasons a Lennox central AC fails to cool. Because the causes span from a $20 filter to a $2,500 compressor, we rank them by how likely and how expensive they are, and we're honest about which ones require a licensed pro.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, which can freeze the coil and stop cooling entirely. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, dust and debris insulate the coil so it can't reject heat. Cooling drops off, especially on hot days.
3. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," dead batteries, or a miscalibrated/failed thermostat.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. You'll see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil; the system blows warm.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system can't absorb heat with a low charge. This is a leak repair, not a "top-off," and requires an EPA-certified tech.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — the outdoor fan or compressor hums but won't start, or the fan spins slowly. A common, affordable failure.
7. **Contactor or relay failure** — the outdoor unit gets no signal to run even though the thermostat calls for cooling.
8. **Failed compressor** — the heart of the system. Expensive; usually the last diagnosis after everything else is ruled out.
9. **Clogged condensate drain (float switch trip)** — a full drain pan trips a safety float switch that shuts down cooling to prevent water damage.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first — it fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the temperature is set well below room temp, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure it's running and the fan is spinning. Clear away leaves, grass and debris, and confirm there's at least 2 feet of clearance around it.
- **Supply and return vents:** Confirm they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Blocked airflow can freeze the coil.
- **Ice on the lines or coil:** If you see ice, turn the system to **Off** but leave the fan on **Auto/On** to help it thaw. Don't run cooling until it's fully melted — and figure out why it froze.
- **Condensate drain:** If your indoor unit has a drain line, check for standing water in the pan or a full drain. A clog can trip a float switch. Drain tablets help prevent buildup.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap to expensive, and you can sanity-check their quote against this order:

1. **Airflow and filter check** — verifies the filter, blower operation and coil for ice or dirt.
2. **Thermostat and control verification** — confirms the thermostat is calling for cooling and the low-voltage signal reaches the outdoor unit.
3. **Electrical checks** — tests the capacitor, contactor, and voltage at the condenser. A capacitor failure is quick to spot with a meter.
4. **Refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to read suction and liquid pressures. Low readings point to a leak or undercharge.
5. **Leak search** — if charge is low, they'll use an electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen to find the leak before adding refrigerant. **A pro should never just top off without finding the leak.**
6. **Compressor and coil evaluation** — checks compressor windings, amp draw, and coil condition to confirm or rule out major component failure.

Ask for the measured readings (capacitor microfarads, refrigerant pressures) — a legitimate diagnosis has numbers behind it.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air from vents, unit running | Dirty filter or dirty coil | Replace filter; clear debris around outdoor unit | Deep-clean coils, check airflow |
| Ice on refrigerant lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off cooling, run fan to thaw, replace filter | Find cause of freeze; test charge and airflow |
| Outdoor fan not spinning, humming | Failed run capacitor | None — leave power off | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit completely dead | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, relays, wiring |
| Cools weakly on hot days only | Dirty condenser or low charge | Clean debris from outdoor coil | Check refrigerant pressures, find leak |
| System shuts off, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain/float switch | Clear standing water, add drain tablets | Clear drain line, test float switch |
| Blows warm, no cold at all | Compressor or refrigerant failure | None | Test compressor, pressures, and charge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Prices vary by region, brand of parts, and whether it's an emergency call.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 installed
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location (coil leaks cost more)
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $150–$600 (only valid after the leak is fixed)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ — at this point, compare against a full system replacement, especially on older units using discontinued refrigerant.

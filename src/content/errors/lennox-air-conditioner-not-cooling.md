## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint Lennox homeowners report, and it can appear with or without an error on your thermostat or control board. Your system may be running, blowing air, and using electricity, yet the air coming from the vents is warm, lukewarm, or not cold enough to keep up on a hot day.

On communicating Lennox systems (Signature series with iComfort thermostats), you may see an actual alert code on the thermostat pointing to a specific component. On non-communicating Merit and Elite systems, there's often no code at all — just an underperforming unit.

Cooling depends on three things working together: adequate airflow, a full and sealed refrigerant charge, and a functioning compressor and electrical system. A failure in any one of these produces "not cooling."

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer so vents blow warm.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — matted with grass, dust, or cottonwood, it can't reject heat.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan hums but won't start.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system runs constantly but never reaches setpoint.
6. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
8. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or wiring/communication fault.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive cause; often the last diagnosis after others are ruled out.
10. **Blocked condensate drain / safety float switch** — a full pan trips a switch that shuts down cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here — do not open the sealed refrigerant system or the outdoor electrical panel.

- **Thermostat**: Confirm it's set to COOL and the temperature is set below room temperature. If it's a battery model, replace the batteries.
- **Air filter**: Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of poor cooling.
- **Breakers**: Check your panel for both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset once if tripped. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents**: Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit**: Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Gently hose off the fins from the outside if visibly dirty (power off first at the disconnect).
- **Frozen coil**: If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON to speed thawing) and let it thaw fully — several hours. Running a frozen unit makes it worse.
- **Condensate drain**: If your unit has a float switch and there's standing water in the pan, clear the drain line.

If cooling doesn't return after these, it's a pro job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work through this so you can sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify airflow** — check filter, blower operation, and static pressure.
2. **Measure temperature split** — supply vs. return air temperature; a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F. Low split points to charge or airflow issues.
3. **Check electrical** — test the capacitor with a multimeter, inspect the contactor for pitting, and verify voltage at the outdoor unit.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — measure suction and head pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct. Low charge means a leak search follows.
5. **Leak detection** — electronic sniffer, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test if refrigerant is low.
6. **Compressor test** — check amp draw and windings if the above are ruled out.
7. **Read stored codes** — on iComfort/communicating systems, pull fault history from the thermostat or board.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "top off refrigerant" without finding the leak — that's a temporary fix and often a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vents blow warm, unit running | Low refrigerant or dirty coil | Check/replace filter, clean condenser | Leak search, recharge, coil cleaning |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw fully, replace filter | Diagnose airflow/charge, fix leak |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — safe to observe only | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, no fan | Tripped breaker, contactor, or fuse | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor/fuse, find cause |
| House warm, system short-cycles | Dirty condenser or thermostat | Clean outdoor unit | Check charge, controls, sensors |
| No cooling, water in drain pan | Clogged condensate line/float switch | Clear drain line | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Weak cooling on hot days only | Undersized/low charge/dirty coil | Clean coils, replace filter | Verify charge and airflow |
| Warm air, iComfort alert code | Component-specific fault | Note the code | Diagnose per stored code |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor). Regional pricing and Lennox parts warranty status affect these:

- **Air filter replacement** — $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing** — $75–$200
- **Coil cleaning (indoor or outdoor)** — $150–$400
- **Run capacitor replacement** — $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement** — $150–$350
- **Low-voltage fuse / control repair** — $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge** — $500–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type; R-410A vs. older R-22 is much costlier)
- **TXV / metering device replacement** — $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement** — $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement** — $1,200–$2,500+ (often the point where replacing the whole unit makes more sense)

If your Lennox unit is under 5 years old, the compressor and coil may be covered by the parts warranty — you'd typically pay only labor. Have your model and serial number ready to check coverage.

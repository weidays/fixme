## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Lennox air conditioner runs but the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents isn't cold. Some Lennox systems with communicating controls (such as the iComfort or S30/S40 thermostats paired with Signature-series equipment) may show a diagnostic alert code, but most homeowners simply feel warm air.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, the cost range is enormous. The goal of this page is to help you rule out the cheap, safe causes first and understand what a technician *should* be checking before you approve an expensive repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat issue** — wrong mode (set to "fan" not "cool"), dead batteries, or a bad setpoint.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power, so the indoor blower runs but nothing cools.
4. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked in dirt, grass, or cottonwood can't reject heat.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common electrical failure; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — never "used up"; low charge always means a leak needing repair.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor fan doesn't spin, compressor overheats and shuts off.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit hums, trips, or does nothing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Then wait — if the coil was frozen, it needs several hours (fan on) to thaw.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel. Most systems have **two** relevant breakers — indoor air handler/furnace and outdoor condenser. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for a nearby disconnect switch (a small box on the wall next to the condenser) and confirm it's on. Clear leaves, grass, and debris from the coil fins — keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Vents & registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Condensate:** If your indoor unit has a full drain pan or a safety float switch, a clogged condensate line can shut cooling off. You can pour a cup of water to confirm it drains, but leave anything past a visual check to a technician.

Do **not** open the outdoor unit's electrical panel, touch the capacitor, or attempt anything involving refrigerant lines.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Lennox tech should work through this sequence — use it to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat call for cooling with a meter.
2. **Confirm power** to indoor and outdoor units; test the contactor and 24V control voltage.
3. **Measure airflow** and inspect the evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
4. **Test the run capacitor** with a meter (microfarad reading vs. rating).
5. **Check compressor and fan motor** amperage draw against nameplate.
6. **Connect refrigerant gauges** and measure superheat/subcooling to judge charge — this is where low refrigerant or a metering-device problem shows up.
7. **Leak search** if charge is low (electronic detector, dye, or soap bubbles).
8. **Report findings** with specific readings, not just "it needs a recharge."

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or "replace compressor" without capacitor and electrical tests first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter; thaw coil | Clean coil, check blower |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn to fan-only to thaw | Diagnose airflow/charge, leak search |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Bad capacitor or compressor | None | Test/replace capacitor, check compressor |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, trace fault |
| Unit hums then trips breaker | Failed capacitor or compressor | Leave off | Electrical test, replace part |
| Cools then quits, warm air | Dirty condenser coil overheating | Clear debris | Deep coil cleaning, amp check |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or no power | Replace batteries | Check wiring/transformer |
| Water near indoor unit, no cooling | Clogged condensate/float switch | Check drain | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included (varies by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condensate line clearing:** $100–$250
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs older R-22 is far pricier)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,800+ — at this price, if the unit is 10+ years old, get a replacement quote too

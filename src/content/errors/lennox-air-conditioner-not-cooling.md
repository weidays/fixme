## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on most Lennox air conditioners — it's a symptom. Your system is either running without lowering the indoor temperature, or it isn't running at all when it should. On newer Lennox units paired with **iComfort** or **S30/S40** smart thermostats, you may see an alert or a numbered error on the thermostat screen; on older single-stage systems there's usually no display at all, just warm air from the vents.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you through the likely causes in order, the checks you can safely do yourself, and what a technician should be doing so you can sanity-check any quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A blocked filter chokes airflow, freezes the indoor coil, and kills cooling.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass, leaves, and dust insulate the coil so it can't dump heat.
3. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a scheduling/connectivity issue on iComfort thermostats.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan motor won't start (you may hear humming).
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced or no cooling, sometimes ice on the copper lines. Requires a licensed tech.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — a symptom of low airflow or low refrigerant; the unit blows warm air until it thaws.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the outdoor fan doesn't spin, causing the system to overheat and shut down.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome, usually the last suspect after everything else checks out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. If any of them doesn't fix it, stop and call a pro.

- **Air filter:** Pull it out and hold it up to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first — always.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the temperature is set below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery-powered model. On iComfort/S30 thermostats, check the schedule and Wi-Fi status.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel. AC systems usually have **two** breakers — one for the indoor air handler/furnace and one for the outdoor unit. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, do not keep resetting it — call a technician.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs. Closing too many vents can freeze the coil.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for leaves, grass clippings, or debris packed against the condenser. Gently clear it away and trim back plants at least two feet on all sides. Never insert anything into the fan.
- **Condensate drain:** If your indoor unit has a float switch and the drain is clogged, the system may refuse to cool. Check for a full or overflowing drain pan and standing water near the air handler.
- **Frozen coil / lines:** If you see ice on the copper refrigerant lines or the indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** but leave the **FAN ON** to help it thaw (this can take several hours). If it re-freezes, you have an airflow or refrigerant problem for a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you tell a thorough diagnosis from a rushed one:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — filter, blower operation, coil condition, and duct issues.
3. **Confirm power and controls** — voltage at the disconnect, contactor operation, capacitor test with a multimeter, and thermostat call for cooling.
4. **Inspect the outdoor unit** — condenser coil cleanliness, fan motor operation, and amperage draw on the compressor.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — read suction and head pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct. Low charge points to a leak, which they should locate before adding any refrigerant.
6. **Compressor test** — if all else checks out, they'll test the compressor windings and start components.

A good tech won't just "top off" refrigerant without finding the leak — that's a red flag and, for many refrigerants, not something you should keep paying for repeatedly.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor blower runs, outdoor unit off | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once; check thermostat | Test contactor, replace if pitted |
| Weak cooling, dirty outdoor coil | Clogged condenser coil | Rinse coil gently, clear debris | Deep-clean coil, check charge |
| Indoor coil or lines iced over | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter; run fan to thaw | Find airflow issue or refrigerant leak |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed fan motor or capacitor | Turn unit off to prevent overheating | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| No cooling, thermostat blank | Dead batteries or no power | Replace batteries, check breaker | Diagnose control wiring/transformer |
| Runs constantly, never reaches set temp | Low refrigerant or undersized/failing compressor | Confirm filter & vents are clear | Pressure test, leak search, compressor test |

## Repair costs

Ranges below are typical US costs including parts and labor. Your area and unit size affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often worth comparing against a new condenser or full system on older units)

If your unit is more than 12–15 years old and facing a compressor or coil repair, ask your technician whether replacement makes more financial sense.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a numeric fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Lennox air conditioner is running (or trying to run) but the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. Some newer Lennox units with communicating controls (like iComfort-equipped Signature-series systems) may display an actual error code at the thermostat, but most Merit and Elite split systems give you no code at all. You just get warm air.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, this page walks you through the full range — from free DIY fixes to jobs that require a licensed HVAC technician. Refrigerant handling, capacitor testing, and compressor work are **pro-only** for safety and legal reasons.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Clogged or dirty outdoor condenser coil** — dirt, grass clippings, and cottonwood block heat rejection so the system can't cool.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to FAN or HEAT), or a miswired/failing thermostat.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler loses power while the other keeps running.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common electrical failure; the compressor or condenser fan hums but won't start.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced capacity, warm air, and often ice on the lines. Requires a pro to find and repair.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the condenser never energizes.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — usually caused by #1, #2, or #6; a solid ice block stops airflow entirely.
9. **Failing condenser fan motor** — the compressor overheats and shuts off on its internal overload.
10. **Failed compressor** — the least common but most expensive; the "heart" of the system won't pump refrigerant.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Everything else belongs to a technician.

- **Filter:** Pull and inspect the air filter. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel. Central AC uses two breakers — one for the air handler/furnace and one for the outdoor condenser. Reset any that are tripped **once**. If a breaker trips again, stop and call a pro — that's a fault, not a fluke.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** With the system off, clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (low pressure, top to bottom). Keep 18–24 inches of clearance on all sides.
- **Condensate:** Look for a full drain pan or standing water — a clogged condensate line can trip a safety float switch and shut the system down. You can clear a visible clog at the outdoor termination, but leave internal drain work to a pro.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system OFF and set the thermostat fan to ON to thaw it before a technician visits. Do **not** run cooling while it's iced up.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verifies the complaint** and checks thermostat operation and call for cooling (24V signal reaching the outdoor unit).
2. **Inspects airflow** — filter, coil condition, blower operation, and static pressure.
3. **Checks electrical** — measures capacitor microfarads against the rated value, tests the contactor, and confirms voltage at the compressor and fan motor.
4. **Attaches refrigerant gauges** and reads suction/head pressures and superheat/subcooling. This tells them if the charge is correct or if there's a leak.
5. **Performs a leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the system is low.
6. **Tests the compressor** — amp draw, windings resistance, and whether it starts and pumps.
7. **On communicating Signature-series systems**, reads any stored fault codes from the iComfort control.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "you need a new compressor" or "we just added refrigerant" without a documented leak search — refrigerant doesn't get used up; if it's low, there's a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower & static pressure |
| Outdoor unit dirty, runs constantly | Clogged condenser coil | Rinse fins with hose | Deep coil cleaning |
| No air at all, no response | Thermostat off/dead | Set to COOL, replace batteries | Test/replace thermostat |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, find cause |
| Outdoor fan won't spin, humming | Failed run capacitor | None — pro only | Test & replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines/coil, warm air | Low refrigerant or airflow restriction | Turn off, thaw with fan ON | Leak search & recharge |
| Compressor hums then shuts off | Failing compressor or hard start | None — pro only | Test compressor, hard-start kit |
| Fan cage still, hot condenser | Failed condenser fan motor | None — pro only | Replace fan motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 (depends on leak location; a coil leak costs far more than a fitting)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800 (often worth weighing against full system replacement on older units)

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type (older R-22 systems are much costlier to recharge than R-410A), and whether parts are under Lennox warranty. Labor typically isn't covered even when parts are.

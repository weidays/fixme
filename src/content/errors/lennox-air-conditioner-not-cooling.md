## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a fault code in the traditional sense — it's a symptom. Your Lennox air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, thermostat calling) but the air coming from the vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the setpoint. On communicating **iComfort** systems the thermostat may log an actual alert code, but on most standard Lennox units there's no error stored — the equipment simply can't move enough heat outdoors.

Cooling depends on three things working together: adequate airflow across the indoor coil, a properly charged and sealed refrigerant loop, and a functioning outdoor compressor and fan. A problem in any one of these produces the same complaint. This page walks through the likely causes in order, what you can safely check, and what a technician should be doing so you can sanity-check a quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, which drops capacity and can freeze the coil into a block of ice. The single most common cause of weak cooling.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — Grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust blanket the coil so it can't reject heat. Very common late in the season.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — A weak or bulged capacitor prevents the compressor or condenser fan motor from starting. The fan may hum but not spin, or the compressor clicks and stops.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Produces warm air, ice on the copper lines, and long run times. Refrigerant is never "used up," so low charge always means a leak.
5. **Failed contactor** — The 24V relay that powers the outdoor unit gets pitted or stuck, so the compressor never energizes. Common wear item.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Caused by low airflow or low charge; the iced coil blocks air entirely. You'll see condensation or ice near the indoor unit.
7. **Thermostat or control wiring fault** — Loss of the cooling call, a dead thermostat, or a tripped float switch on the condensate line stopping the system.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — Outdoor fan doesn't run, compressor overheats and shuts off on its internal overload.
9. **Compressor failure** — The compressor won't start or runs but won't compress. The most expensive outcome, usually on older units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop at anything that requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or the electrical panel of the outdoor unit.

- **Air filter** — Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the top cause of poor cooling.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery powered. If the screen is blank, that's your problem.
- **Breakers** — Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler and the outdoor unit (they're often separate). Reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents** — Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't choking airflow. Return vents especially need to be clear.
- **Condensate drain and float switch** — A clogged drain trips the float safety and stops cooling. If you see standing water in the pan, clear the drain line and clean the pan.
- **Ice on the copper lines or indoor coil** — If you see ice, turn the system to OFF (fan can stay ON) and let it thaw completely for a few hours before restarting. Running an iced unit damages the compressor.

Do **not** open the outdoor disconnect, discharge or replace a capacitor unless you're comfortable with electrical safety, or add refrigerant — that requires EPA certification.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Lennox tech should work systematically, not just "add a pound of Freon." Expect them to:

1. **Verify the call and airflow** — Confirm the thermostat is sending a cooling signal, check the filter, and measure temperature drop (split) across the indoor coil (a healthy system is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Inspect the outdoor unit** — Check the condenser coil for debris, test the capacitor with a meter, and check the contactor for pitting.
3. **Take refrigerant readings** — Connect gauges and measure suction/liquid pressures and superheat/subcooling against Lennox spec for that model. This tells them if charge is correct.
4. **Leak search if charge is low** — With electronic detector or dye. A pro should never just top off without finding the leak.
5. **Check the compressor and fan motor** — Measure amp draw and confirm the compressor actually pumps.
6. **On iComfort systems** — Pull any stored alerts from the thermostat or control board to pinpoint communication or component faults.

If a quote jumps straight to "recharge" or "replace compressor" without pressure readings and a leak search, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Verify coil isn't iced or dirty |
| Ice on lines / indoor coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, let thaw, replace filter | Find airflow or refrigerant cause |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Long run times, house won't cool | Low refrigerant leak | None | Leak search and repair, recharge |
| System off, water in drain pan | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain line | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Blank thermostat | Dead thermostat / no power | Replace batteries | Check control wiring and transformer |
| Loud, then shuts off | Compressor or overload fault | None — call pro | Test compressor, amp draw |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area and unit age will move them.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$300 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A costs more)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$500 (more for iComfort communicating models)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,200+ — on units over 10–12 years old, weigh this against full replacement

If your unit uses older R-22 refrigerant, leak repairs get expensive fast due to refrigerant cost and scarcity; replacement often makes more sense.

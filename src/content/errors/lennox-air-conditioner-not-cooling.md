## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on the display — it's the symptom homeowners report most often. Your Lennox air conditioner may be running (the outdoor fan spins, the indoor blower moves air) but the air coming out of your vents is warm, lukewarm, or just not getting the house to the setpoint.

On communicating **iComfort** systems, the thermostat may show an active alert or a numeric error tied to the outdoor board — check the thermostat's alert/notifications menu. On standard 24V Lennox systems (Merit, most Elite models), there's no on-board display, so the diagnosis relies on symptoms, gauges, and electrical measurements.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a $0 filter to a $2,500 compressor, this page walks you from the cheapest, safest checks up to what a technician will actually measure.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode (Heat vs Cool), dead batteries, or a scheduling/hold conflict.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — a partially tripped double-pole breaker can leave the outdoor unit dead while the indoor blower still runs.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow (dirty filter) or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor and/or outdoor fan hums but won't start.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced capacity, longer run times, ice buildup. Requires an EPA-certified tech.
8. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit gets pitted or stuck and won't engage.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor runs but the outdoor fan doesn't, causing high-pressure shutdown.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome, usually the last thing diagnosed.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. If none resolve it, call a pro.

- **Filter:** Pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Run the system for a few hours afterward and recheck.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the fan is on **Auto**, and the setpoint is at least a few degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for the AC breakers (there are usually two — indoor air handler/furnace and outdoor condenser). If one is tripped, flip it fully OFF, then back ON, **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers. Closing too many vents starves airflow.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or a fence packed against the condenser. Clear at least 2 feet around it. Turn the unit off at the thermostat before pulling out large debris by hand.
- **Condensate:** If there's a full drain pan or a tripped float switch, the system may lock out cooling. Empty a visibly full pan and check for a clogged drain line.
- **If you see ice** on the refrigerant lines or coil: turn the system to **Fan only** for a few hours to thaw it, replace the filter, then try cooling again. If it re-freezes, you likely have a refrigerant or airflow problem for a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap to expensive, and you can sanity-check the quote against these steps:

1. **Confirms the symptom** — measures supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and both coils for restriction or ice.
3. **Verifies power and controls** — confirms 24V call for cooling reaches the outdoor unit, checks contactor, and tests the run capacitor with a meter (a weak capacitor is a common, cheap find).
4. **Measures refrigerant** — connects gauges to read pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct or if there's a leak.
5. **Leak search** (if low) — electronic detector, dye, or bubble test to find the leak before adding refrigerant.
6. **Tests the compressor and fan motor** — checks amp draw, windings, and start components.
7. **Reads board/thermostat alerts** on iComfort systems for stored fault history.

If a tech quotes a compressor or full charge without ever putting gauges on the system or measuring the temperature split, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Check coils, blower speed |
| Indoor coil/lines iced over | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set fan-only to thaw, replace filter | Find leak, recharge, check charge |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Humming, no start | Failed run capacitor | None | Test and replace capacitor |
| Compressor runs, fan doesn't spin | Failed condenser fan motor | Power off, don't force blade | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Long run times, house never cools | Low refrigerant leak | None | Leak search and recharge |
| Full drain pan / float tripped | Clogged condensate line | Empty pan, clear line | Clear drain, replace float switch |
| Thermostat blank or wrong mode | Dead batteries / settings | Replace batteries, set to Cool | Replace thermostat, check wiring |
| Nothing works, no cooling at all | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test/replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Regional labor rates and iComfort parts pricing vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $75–$200 pro; near $0 DIY
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often approaching the cost of a new outdoor unit)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$500 (higher for iComfort communicating stats)

If your unit uses R-22 refrigerant (older systems), recharge costs are significantly higher and replacement is often the better value.

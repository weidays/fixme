## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom most Lennox air conditioner owners describe when the system runs but the house won't reach setpoint, or the air from the vents isn't cold. On communicating **iComfort** systems (XC20, XC21, XC25 and matched thermostats), you may see an actual alert code on the thermostat screen; on older single-stage Merit and Elite units there's often no code at all — just warm air.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, the goal here is to help you separate the cheap, safe fixes you can do yourself from the diagnosis a licensed HVAC technician needs to perform. This is rated **pro severity** because the most expensive causes — refrigerant leaks and compressor failure — legally and safely require a certified technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, reducing cooling and eventually freezing the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer so vents blow warm.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, cottonwood, and dirt insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system runs constantly but can't cool; always indicates a leak, never "using up" refrigerant.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or outdoor fan hums but won't start, or the fan spins slowly.
6. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, loose wiring, or a failed thermostat.
7. **Tripped breaker or float switch** — a clogged condensate drain trips the safety float, cutting the system.
8. **Failed contactor or control board** — outdoor unit won't energize; more common on aging units.
9. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the most costly outcome, often the end result of ignored problems above.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If none resolve it, call a licensed technician.

- **Air filter** — Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering it 5°F to confirm the system calls for cooling.
- **Breakers** — Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers in your panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Supply and return vents** — Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow. Keep return grilles clear.
- **Condensate drain** — If you see standing water near the indoor unit or the system shut off unexpectedly, the drain may be clogged and the float switch tripped. You can pour a cup of water through an accessible drain line or add drain tablets, but don't disable any safety switch.
- **Outdoor unit** — Gently clear leaves, grass, and debris from around and on top of the condenser. Do **not** open the unit or spray water into it while it's powered.
- **Ice check** — If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **FAN ONLY** (or OFF) and let it thaw fully — this can take a few hours. Running a frozen unit can damage the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence. Use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verifies the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Reads any codes** — on iComfort systems, pulls thermostat/board alerts; on older units, checks components manually.
4. **Tests electrical** — measures capacitor microfarads, checks the contactor, and confirms voltage at the compressor and fan.
5. **Connects refrigerant gauges** — reads suction and liquid pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct.
6. **Leak-searches** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before adding refrigerant.
7. **Tests the compressor** — checks amp draw and windings if the unit won't start.

Be wary of any tech who tops off refrigerant without searching for a leak — that's a temporary fix you'll pay for again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm vents | Dirty filter | Replace the filter | Inspect blower and duct if it persists |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn to FAN/OFF and thaw fully | Find root cause; check charge and airflow |
| Outdoor unit humming, not starting | Failed capacitor | None — electrical hazard | Test and replace capacitor |
| Both fans run, house stays warm | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — EPA certified only | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan spins slowly or not at all | Fan motor or capacitor | Clear debris only | Test and replace motor/capacitor |
| System shut off, water near air handler | Clogged drain, tripped float | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, verify float switch |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, control board, wiring |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Trace wiring, replace thermostat |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Regional rates and system size vary widely.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Control board (iComfort/communicating):** $400–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ — often the point where replacing the whole unit makes more sense

Note: Lennox systems using older R-22 refrigerant cost far more to recharge, as R-22 is phased out. If your unit is R-22 and needs a leak repair, ask your technician whether replacement is more economical.

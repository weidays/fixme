## If you smell gas, leave first

**This code's worst case is raw, unburned gas. Before anything else:**

- **If you smell gas anywhere in the house, or you can see flame at the burners when the furnace should be off — get everyone out now.**
- **Do not** touch light switches, the furnace switch, the thermostat, the breaker panel, appliances, or your phone while inside. Do not look for the gas shutoff. Do not go to the furnace.
- Leave doors open behind you if that costs you no time, and walk out.
- **From outside the house** — a neighbor's, down the block — call your gas utility's emergency number or 911.
- Do not re-enter until the gas utility or fire department tells you it is safe.

Everything below assumes there is **no gas odor and no visible flame**. If either is present, stop reading and evacuate.

## What this code means

Five flashes on an American Standard furnace control board means **flame was sensed when no flame should be present** — the board is detecting a flame signal while the gas valve is commanded off.

The furnace continuously monitors the flame-sense circuit. Once a heat cycle ends and the gas valve closes, the flame signal should disappear within a couple of seconds. If the board still "sees" flame after the valve is off, it interprets this as either a genuinely burning burner (a gas valve that failed to shut) or a false electrical signal on the sense circuit. Either way it treats the situation as a fault.

On most American Standard/Trane integrated furnace control boards this fault **holds and locks the unit out** until the condition clears and power is cycled. The board will keep the inducer and/or blower running to purge in some designs. This is a **safety-critical code** because one of its causes — a gas valve that does not fully close — can allow unburned gas. It is not a DIY repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

There are really two buckets here: the board is being told there is flame when there isn't, or there actually is flame because the gas valve is leaking by.

1. **False flame signal on the flame-sense circuit (most common).** The sense circuit reports current when no flame exists. Typical sources:
   - a grounded, shorted, chafed, or pinched flame-sensor lead;
   - moisture, corrosion, or debris on the sensor or its connector, creating a leakage path that mimics a flame signal;
   - a poor ground on the burner assembly or control, which can skew flame readings.
2. **Control board (IFC) flame-detect fault.** The flame-detection circuit on the board itself misreads or latches a signal that isn't really there. Diagnosed after the sense circuit and wiring check out clean.
3. **Gas valve not fully closing (stuck open or leaking by).** Less common than a false signal, but **by far the most safety-critical**: a failing valve seat lets gas through after the cycle ends, so a small flame persists — or unburned gas escapes. Any confirmed case is an immediate repair.

Manufacturer troubleshooting for this code centers on verifying the actual burner state first, then the flame-sense circuit, then the gas valve, then the board — in that order.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should perform. Everything inside the cabinet is technician work. **Do all of these only if there is no gas odor and no visible flame** — otherwise evacuate and call the gas utility or 911 from outside.

- **Confirm the flash count.** Read the LED through the blower-door sight glass and match it to the legend printed on the door label. Make sure it's five flashes, not four.
- **Check the thermostat.** Set it to Heat and a temperature well below room temperature, then back up, to be sure a stray call for heat isn't confusing the picture. Replace thermostat batteries if it uses them.
- **Cycle power once — only if there is no gas smell and no flame visible at the burners.** Turn the furnace switch or breaker off for 30 seconds, then on, to attempt one reset. **If the 5-flash code returns, stop.** Do not keep resetting — a stuck gas valve is a real possibility.
- **Check visible vents and registers** are open and unobstructed.
- **Inspect the condensate line/pan** (high-efficiency models) for standing water or overflow.

Do **not** open the burner compartment, clean the flame sensor, touch wiring, or attempt to close the gas valve yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. **Verify actual burner state** with power on and a completed heat cycle — visually confirming whether flame truly persists after the gas valve is commanded off.
2. **Test the gas valve** for full closure and check for leak-by, replacing it if it does not seat.
3. **Inspect and meter the flame sensor and its wiring** for shorts to ground, corrosion, or moisture that fakes a flame signal.
4. **Check the control board's flame-detect circuit** and connectors, comparing the microamp signal to spec with the valve off.
5. **Confirm proper grounding** of the burner assembly and control, since a poor ground can cause false flame readings.
6. **Clear the fault and re-run several cycles** to confirm the code does not return.

If a tech quotes a full board or gas-valve replacement without first confirming whether real flame persists, ask them to show you the flame-signal readings that justify it.

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US figures including parts and labor; your area and model vary.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90 – $180
- **Flame sensor replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Flame-sense wiring or connector repair:** $150 – $350
- **Gas valve replacement:** $350 – $600
- **Integrated furnace control board (IFC):** $400 – $700, and higher on some models

Simple causes (a corroded connector, a false signal cleared after cleaning) may land near the low end. A confirmed stuck-open gas valve is the priority repair and should never be deferred.

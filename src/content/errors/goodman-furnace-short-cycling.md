## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single Goodman fault code — it's a behavior. Your furnace starts a heating cycle, then shuts down long before the thermostat is satisfied, and repeats. On Goodman units the control board may flash a diagnostic LED (commonly a limit-trip, flame-loss, or pressure-switch pattern) each time it aborts, but the exact blink code varies by board generation and model.

Short cycling almost always means the furnace is protecting itself — from overheating, from a flame it can't confirm, or from a pressure/airflow problem. Left alone, the repeated firing and cooling stresses the heat exchanger and ignition components. Because the root cause can be a safety-critical limit or a failing heat exchanger, this is rated a **pro** repair once the simple checks below are ruled out.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts return airflow, the heat exchanger overheats, and the high-limit switch cuts the burners. Most common by a wide margin.
2. **Dirty or failing flame sensor** — burner lights, then the board can't confirm flame and shuts down after a few seconds, retrying repeatedly.
3. **Blocked supply/return vents or closed dampers** — same overheating result as a dirty filter.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — a bad limit can trip early even with good airflow.
5. **Pressure switch / blocked flue or condensate** — on condensing (high-efficiency) Goodman models, a clogged condensate drain or blocked intake/exhaust makes the pressure switch open mid-cycle.
6. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats fast, satisfies the thermostat quickly, then cycles often. Common in poorly sized installs.
7. **Thermostat location or wiring** — a thermostat near a supply register, or loose/corroded wiring, causes false cycling.
8. **Control board fault** — less common, but a failing board can misread signals and abort cycles.
9. **Cracked or failing heat exchanger** — can cause the flame to distort and drop out; a safety concern that must be verified by a pro.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do:

- **Air filter** — pull it and look at it against a light. If it's gray or clogged, replace it. Run the furnace and see if cycling stops.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to Heat and a temperature above room temp. If it's battery-powered, install fresh batteries. Make sure it isn't mounted in direct sun or over a register.
- **Supply and return vents** — walk the house and open any closed registers and make sure furniture or rugs aren't blocking returns.
- **Breaker** — if the furnace lost power, reset the breaker **once**. Do not repeatedly cycle it.
- **Condensate drain (high-efficiency models)** — if you see standing water near the furnace or a full drain pan, a clogged condensate line can trip the pressure switch. Clear an obvious blockage if you can safely reach it.

Do **not** bypass limit or pressure switches, open the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a furnace that keeps locking out. If simple checks don't fix it, call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will:

1. **Read the LED flash code** on the control board and note the timing of each shutdown.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace and compare it to the rating-plate spec — high rise points to airflow restriction (filter, blower, ducting).
3. **Test the flame sensor** microamp signal and clean or replace it if weak.
4. **Check the high-limit and rollout switches** for correct operation and continuity.
5. **Test the pressure switch and inspect the flue/condensate path** on condensing models.
6. **Inspect the heat exchanger** with a camera or combustion analysis for cracks.
7. **Verify blower speed and static pressure**, and confirm the furnace is correctly sized for the home if oversizing is suspected.

Ask the tech to show you the flash code and the temperature-rise reading — those two numbers explain most short-cycling quotes.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, air feels very hot at vents | Dirty filter / airflow restriction tripping limit | Replace filter, open all vents | Measure temp rise, check blower & ducting |
| Burner lights then dies after 3-7 seconds, retries | Dirty/failing flame sensor | Replace filter first; then call pro | Test/clean or replace flame sensor |
| High-efficiency unit cycles with water near furnace | Clogged condensate / pressure switch | Clear obvious drain blockage | Clear line, test pressure switch, check flue |
| Frequent short runs, house comfortable, no fault | Oversized furnace or thermostat placement | Move nothing that's hard-wired; check t-stat location | Verify sizing, adjust staging/anticipator |
| Cycling plus burning or hot-metal smell | Overheating / possible heat exchanger issue | Shut off, call a pro | Inspect heat exchanger, limit switch |
| Cycles with no consistent pattern, LED shows board fault | Control board failure | None | Diagnose and replace control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Flame sensor replacement** — $80–$250 installed.
- **High-limit switch** — $120–$300.
- **Pressure switch** — $130–$350.
- **Condensate drain cleaning** — $75–$200.
- **Control board** — $350–$700+ depending on model (two-stage/variable boards cost more).
- **Blower motor** (if airflow-related) — $400–$900.
- **Heat exchanger** — $600–$1,600 for the part alone; on older units, often not worth it versus replacing the furnace.
- **Diagnostic/service call** — $90–$180, often credited toward the repair.

Sizing corrections (an oversized furnace) are the expensive exception — the real fix may be equipment replacement, which is a full quote conversation.

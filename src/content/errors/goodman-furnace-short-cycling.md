## What this code means

Short cycling isn't a single Goodman fault code — it's a behavior. Your furnace fires up, runs briefly (often 10 seconds to a few minutes), then shuts down and repeats. On Goodman and Amana boards you'll usually see a diagnostic LED blinking a pattern behind the blower-door sight glass. The exact pattern depends on your board revision, so **check the label inside the blower door for your model's specific blink codes** rather than assuming.

Short cycling almost always means the furnace is protecting itself: it either can't prove a stable flame, is overheating and tripping the high limit, or is being told to stop by the thermostat or a safety switch. It's rated **pro** here because chronic short cycling can crack a heat exchanger over time, and some causes (gas valve, control board, blower motor) require a technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty flame sensor.** The most common cause. A carbon-coated sensor rod can't detect flame, so the board shuts the gas valve within a few seconds of ignition, then retries. Classic pattern: burners light, then die repeatedly.
2. **Clogged air filter or blocked return.** Restricted airflow lets the heat exchanger overheat and trips the high-limit switch, cutting the burners until it cools. This is the #1 airflow-related cause.
3. **Overheating from blocked/closed supply registers or a dirty blower wheel.** Same result as a dirty filter — the limit switch trips.
4. **Faulty high-limit or rollout switch.** A failing limit switch can trip early even with good airflow.
5. **Thermostat problems.** Dead batteries, a bad location (near a register or heat source), loose wiring, or a mis-set heat anticipator can cut cycles short.
6. **Flue/venting or condensate issues.** A blocked flue or a clogged condensate drain (on high-efficiency models) can trip the pressure switch and interrupt cycles.
7. **Oversized furnace.** An oversized unit heats the space fast, satisfies the thermostat, and shuts off — then repeats. Common in poorly-sized installs.
8. **Failing control board or gas valve.** Least common, but a board that misreads sensors or a valve that won't stay open will cause erratic cycling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do **not** open the gas valve, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a locked-out unit.

- **Air filter.** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of overheating short cycles.
- **Supply and return vents.** Walk the house and make sure registers aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs. Restricted airflow overheats the furnace.
- **Thermostat.** Replace the batteries. Confirm it's set to Heat and the setpoint is above room temperature. Make sure it isn't mounted near a supply register or in direct sun.
- **Breaker.** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on; a partially tripped breaker can cause odd behavior. Flip it fully off then on once — do not repeat if it keeps tripping.
- **Condensate drain (high-efficiency 90%+ models).** Look for standing water or a clogged drain line/trap near the furnace. A full trap can trip the pressure switch. Clear obvious blockages only.
- **Visible flue/intake terminations** (outside, on condensing furnaces): check for nests, ice, or debris blocking the PVC pipes.

If it still short cycles after a clean filter, open vents, and fresh batteries, call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech should:

1. Read the diagnostic LED blink code at the board and note it against your model's chart.
2. Watch a full ignition sequence to see *when* it fails — at ignition (flame sensor/gas valve) vs. mid-cycle (limit/overheat) vs. random (thermostat/board).
3. **Measure microamp flame current** at the flame sensor — a low reading confirms a dirty or failing sensor.
4. Check **temperature rise** across the supply/return against the nameplate spec to confirm airflow and whether the limit is tripping legitimately.
5. Test the high-limit and rollout switches, and inspect the blower wheel for dirt buildup.
6. On condensing units, check pressure switches, the inducer, and the condensate trap for blockages.
7. Inspect the heat exchanger if overheating has been chronic.

If someone quotes a control board or gas valve without first cleaning the flame sensor and checking airflow, ask why — those are the cheap, common fixes.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burners light then die in ~5–10 sec, repeat | Dirty flame sensor | None safe beyond checking filter | Clean/replace sensor, verify microamps |
| Runs 3–8 min then shuts off, blower keeps running | Overheating / high-limit trip | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, clean blower, test limit |
| Cycles regardless of temperature, erratic | Thermostat wiring/anticipator | Replace batteries, check location | Test thermostat, tighten wiring |
| Short cycles with water near furnace | Clogged condensate trap (90%+) | Clear visible blockage | Clean trap, verify pressure switch |
| Heats fast, shuts off, repeats evenly | Oversized furnace | None | Confirm sizing, adjust staging/airflow |
| Erratic with no clear pattern | Failing control board/gas valve | None | Test/replace board or valve |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Filter replacement:** $0–$30 (DIY).
- **Flame sensor clean:** $0 DIY (fine abrasive pad) to $80–$180 on a service call.
- **Flame sensor replacement:** $150–$300 installed.
- **High-limit or rollout switch:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Blower wheel cleaning:** $150–$400 depending on access.
- **Pressure switch / condensate clearing:** $150–$400.
- **Control board:** $400–$700+ installed.
- **Gas valve:** $300–$600 installed.
- **Cracked heat exchanger (from chronic overheating):** $600–$1,500+, or replacement furnace territory if out of warranty.

Many Goodman furnaces carry a 10-year parts limited warranty (registration required) — confirm your coverage before paying for a board or valve.

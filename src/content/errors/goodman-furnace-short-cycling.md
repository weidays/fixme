## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single Goodman blink code — it's a **behavior**. Your furnace fires up, runs for a short time (often under 3–5 minutes), shuts off, then starts again shortly after. It repeats this pattern instead of running a full, steady heating cycle.

On Goodman and Amana furnaces, short cycling is usually the furnace's **safety systems doing their job** — a high-limit switch tripping on overheating, a flame sensor losing signal, or the pressure switch flickering. The board reacts by cutting the burners, then retrying. Depending on your board revision, you may see a matching diagnostic blink code (commonly a limit-open or flame-loss pattern) on the LED behind the blower door.

Left unaddressed, repeated short cycling almost always ends in a **hard lockout** (see Related codes) once the board hits its retry limit.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow overheats the heat exchanger and trips the high-limit switch, killing the burners early.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers create the same overheating as a dirty filter.
3. **Weak or dirty flame sensor** — a carbon-coated sensor loses flame signal seconds after ignition, so the board shuts the gas valve and retries.
4. **Restricted condensate drain / tripped float switch (96% units)** — a clogged trap or full condensate line trips the safety switch mid-cycle on high-efficiency models.
5. **Faulty or drifting thermostat** — a bad thermostat, loose wire, or a unit located near a heat source can rapidly satisfy and re-call for heat.
6. **Pressure switch / blocked intake or exhaust** — a partially blocked flue, a nesting animal, or a failing pressure switch causes intermittent trips.
7. **Failing high-limit switch** — a limit switch that trips prematurely even with good airflow.
8. **Oversized furnace** — a design-level cause: the unit heats fast, satisfies the thermostat, and cycles off before a full run. No single part is broken.
9. **Control board fault** — less common; the board mis-times or falsely reads inputs.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. Do **not** open the gas valve, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a furnace that has locked out.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to "Heat" and a few degrees above room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Make sure it's not in the sun or near a lamp/vent.
- **Vents and registers:** Walk the house and open any closed supply and return registers. Clear furniture or rugs blocking them.
- **Breaker:** Check the furnace breaker in your panel. A tripped or half-tripped breaker can cause odd cycling. Flip it fully off, then on — once.
- **Condensate (96% units):** Look for water pooling near the furnace or a full drain line. A clogged condensate line trips a float switch. You can clear a visible clog, but if the float switch itself is bad, call a pro.
- **Outdoor intake/exhaust (high-efficiency):** Check the PVC pipes outside for snow, leaves, ice, or a bird/wasp nest blocking them.

If the furnace still short cycles after a fresh filter and open vents, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just swap parts — expect this sequence so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Reads the blink code** on the control board LED and notes the pattern (limit open, flame loss, pressure switch, etc.).
2. **Watches a full cycle** with a meter, timing exactly when and why the burners cut out.
3. **Checks airflow / temperature rise** across the heat exchanger against the nameplate spec — high temperature rise points to airflow restriction or a lazy blower.
4. **Tests the flame sensor microamp signal** — a weak reading means clean or replace the sensor.
5. **Verifies pressure switch operation** and inspects the intake/exhaust and condensate for blockage.
6. **Ohms out the high-limit switch** and blower motor if airflow looks fine.
7. **Inspects the control board** for burnt relays only after ruling out the cheaper causes above.

If the first thing a tech says is "you need a new board" without checking the filter, airflow, and flame sensor, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burners light, run ~3–5 min, then shut off | Overheat / limit trip from dirty filter | Replace filter, open all vents | Check temperature rise, test limit switch, verify blower speed |
| Flame lights then dies within seconds, retries | Dirty/weak flame sensor | Replace filter first | Clean or replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Cycling with water near furnace (96% unit) | Clogged condensate / float switch tripped | Clear visible drain clog | Replace float switch, clear trap, check drain slope |
| Inducer runs but burners never stay on | Pressure switch or blocked flue | Check outdoor PVC for blockage | Test pressure switch, clear/repair venting |
| Runs briefly even with clean filter and open vents | Failing high-limit switch | None — call a pro | Ohm and replace limit switch |
| Heats fast, cycles off, whole house feels uneven | Oversized furnace / thermostat location | Move heat sources away from thermostat | Assess sizing, adjust anticipator/cycle settings |
| Random cycling with no clear pattern | Control board or wiring fault | Reset breaker once | Inspect board relays, wiring, replace board if needed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined (varies by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Flame sensor clean or replace:** $80–$250
- **Condensate float switch / drain clearing:** $100–$300
- **Pressure switch replacement:** $150–$400
- **High-limit switch replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor issue (if airflow-related):** $400–$900
- **Control board replacement:** $350–$600
- **Oversized furnace (no part fix):** thermostat/staging adjustments $0–$200; correcting sizing means a system evaluation and, at worst, replacement

Most short-cycling calls resolve on the cheaper end — a filter, flame sensor, or drain clog. Board and blower replacements are the exceptions, not the rule.

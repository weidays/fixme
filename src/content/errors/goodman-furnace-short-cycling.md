## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a specific blink code on Goodman furnaces — it's a **behavior**. Your furnace ignites, runs for a short time (often under 3–5 minutes), shuts off, then restarts a short while later. On many Goodman boards the diagnostic LED will flash a limit or flame-related code alongside this behavior, but not always.

Short cycling almost always means one of two things: the furnace is **overheating and tripping the high-limit switch**, or the burner is **losing flame signal** and shutting down before the thermostat is satisfied. Both are protective actions — the furnace is doing what it's designed to do. The job is finding *why* it's protecting itself.

Because repeated overheating can damage the heat exchanger over time, and because a failing gas valve or control board can be involved, we rate this **pro-level** once the simple airflow and thermostat checks are ruled out.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts return airflow, heat exchanger overheats, high-limit switch cuts burners early. Most common and free to fix.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — a carbon-coated sensor rod fails to confirm flame, so the board drops the burners within seconds. Classic "lights then dies" pattern.
3. **Blocked or undersized ductwork / closed vents** — same overheating effect as a dirty filter; too many closed registers starves airflow.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — a limit that trips at too low a temperature, or intermittently, shuts the furnace off prematurely.
5. **Thermostat issues** — poor location near a vent, wrong cycle-rate/anticipator setting, dying batteries, or loose wiring causing false satisfaction.
6. **Clogged condensate drain (high-efficiency models)** — a full drain trips the pressure/float switch on condensing GMEC/GMVC/GMES units, killing the cycle.
7. **Restricted flue or intake / faulty pressure switch** — inducer draft problems cause the pressure switch to open mid-cycle.
8. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home satisfies the thermostat too fast; a design flaw, not a broken part.
9. **Failing control board or gas valve** — less common, but a board that mis-reads sensors or a valve that drops out will cut cycles short.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or opening sealed compartments:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat batteries** — replace them if your thermostat uses them, even if the screen looks fine. Weak batteries cause erratic cycling.
- **Thermostat location & settings** — make sure it isn't in direct sun or above a supply vent. If it has a "cycle rate" or "cph" setting for gas heat, confirm it's set correctly per the manual.
- **Supply and return vents** — walk the house and open any closed registers. Confirm return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Breaker** — if the furnace lost power, reset the breaker once. Do **not** repeatedly cycle it.
- **Condensate line (high-efficiency units)** — look for standing water near the furnace or a clogged drain hose. Clearing an obvious clog is safe; if a float switch keeps tripping, call a pro.

Do **not** bypass the high-limit or pressure switches, adjust the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just swap parts. Expect them to:

- **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace and compare it to the rating-plate spec. A high rise confirms an airflow/overheating problem.
- **Inspect and clean or test the flame sensor**, reading the microamp signal to confirm it's within range.
- **Check the high-limit switch** for correct trip temperature and continuity.
- **Verify airflow** — filter, blower wheel cleanliness, blower speed tap, and duct static pressure.
- **Test the pressure switch and inducer draft**, and inspect the flue/intake for blockage on condensing models.
- **Read the control board LED codes** and check wiring, then test the gas valve and board only after ruling out the cheap causes.

If a tech quotes a control board or gas valve *before* checking your filter, temperature rise, and flame sensor, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, restarts | Dirty filter / airflow restriction | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temperature rise, clean blower, check duct static |
| Lights, flame drops in 3–7 seconds, retries | Dirty flame sensor | Replace filter first; sensor is a pro clean | Clean/replace flame sensor, read microamp signal |
| Cycles regardless of airflow | Faulty high-limit switch | None safe | Test and replace limit switch |
| Erratic cycling, thermostat-driven | Bad thermostat location/batteries | Replace batteries, relocate/adjust cph | Verify wiring, calibrate or replace stat |
| Water near furnace, cycles cut short | Clogged condensate drain (HE models) | Clear obvious clog | Clear drain, test float/pressure switch |
| Inducer runs but burners stop | Restricted flue or bad pressure switch | Check outdoor intake/exhaust for blockage | Test pressure switch, inspect flue draft |
| Random shutdowns, board code shown | Control board or gas valve | None | Test and replace board or valve |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined where noted:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $80–$180 on a service call; the part itself is $15–$40 if replaced.
- **High-limit switch replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Pressure switch replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Condensate drain clearing / new trap:** $100–$250.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 depending on model.
- **Inducer motor (if draft-related):** $300–$600.
- **Control board:** $350–$650.
- **Gas valve:** $300–$600.

Diagnostic/service-call fees typically run **$80–$150** and are often credited toward the repair.

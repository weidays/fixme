## What this code means

Short cycling is not a single Goodman error code — it's a behavior. Your furnace starts a heating cycle, runs for only a couple of minutes, shuts down, then fires again a short time later. It may or may not flash a diagnostic LED code on the control board, depending on your model and what's triggering the shutdown.

On Goodman furnaces, the most common cause is **the high-limit switch tripping on overheat**. The board senses the heat exchanger getting too hot (usually from restricted airflow), shuts the burners off to protect the unit, then restarts once things cool. Other causes include a **flame sensor that loses signal mid-cycle**, an **oversized furnace**, or a **thermostat problem**.

Because a few of the causes require reading the control board and testing components under power, this is rated a **pro-level** issue once basic homeowner checks are done.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow overheats the heat exchanger and trips the limit switch.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers starve the system of airflow, same effect as a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty flame sensor** — the furnace lights, then the sensor fails to confirm flame, so the board shuts the gas valve. It may retry and repeat.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — a weak or faulty limit trips too early even with adequate airflow.
5. **Thermostat issues** — dead batteries, poor location (near a supply vent), loose wiring, or a "cycle rate" set too aggressively.
6. **Blower motor problems** — a failing motor or bad capacitor reduces airflow, causing overheating trips.
7. **Dirty flame sensor or grounding faults** on the ignition control.
8. **Clogged condensate drain (96% high-efficiency models)** — a full drain trips the pressure/float switch and interrupts the cycle.
9. **Cracked or dirty secondary heat exchanger / plugged flue** — restricts venting and trips the pressure switch.
10. **Oversized furnace** — heats the space too fast, satisfies the thermostat quickly, then repeats. This is an installation issue, not a broken part.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the furnace power on but **without** removing any wiring or bypassing any switches:

- **Filter:** Pull it out and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat batteries:** If your thermostat takes batteries, swap in fresh ones.
- **Thermostat location & settings:** Make sure it isn't in direct sunlight or above a supply register. Check that it's set to "Heat" and the fan is on "Auto."
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and open any closed registers. Move furniture or rugs off floor vents. Confirm the return grille isn't blocked.
- **Breaker:** If the furnace has tripped its breaker, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Condensate drain (high-efficiency 96% models):** Look for standing water in the drain pan or a full float switch. If you see obvious clogs, clearing the visible drain line and adding a drain tablet is safe. Do not disassemble internal switches.

If the furnace still short cycles after these, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will follow a logical sequence — use this to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the pattern** and read the control board's diagnostic LED flash code.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace and compare it to the rating-plate spec. High rise points to an airflow/overheat problem.
3. **Check static pressure** to find airflow restrictions (ductwork, coil, filter).
4. **Test the high-limit switch** for continuity and correct trip point.
5. **Clean and test the flame sensor** — measure microamp signal to confirm flame is being detected reliably.
6. **Inspect the blower motor and capacitor**, and verify blower speed/wheel condition.
7. **Check pressure switch and venting** (especially on 96% models) and the condensate drain.
8. **Verify furnace sizing** if no component fault is found — an oversized unit is a design problem.

Expect a diagnostic fee of roughly **$80–$180**, often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 2–5 min, shuts off, restarts | Dirty filter / restricted airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise & static pressure, clean blower |
| Lights then shuts off after a few seconds | Dirty flame sensor | None (pro test) | Clean/replace flame sensor, check microamps |
| Shuts off on overheat even with clean filter | Failing high-limit switch | None | Test & replace limit switch |
| Cycles regardless of temperature | Thermostat fault / poor location | Replace batteries, relocate/recheck settings | Test wiring, replace thermostat |
| Weak airflow at registers | Blower motor or capacitor failing | None | Replace capacitor or blower motor |
| Standing water, cycling (96% model) | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear visible drain, add tablet | Clear line, test pressure/float switch |
| Heats fast then repeats all day | Oversized furnace | None | Verify load calc, adjust staging or advise |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Flame sensor clean:** $80–$150 · **replacement:** $150–$300
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900+ (ECM motors cost more)
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$400
- **Condensate drain service:** $75–$250
- **Integrated control board:** $400–$700
- **Correcting an oversized furnace:** varies widely; may involve zoning, duct changes, or replacement

Diagnostic fees ($80–$180) are usually credited toward the repair if you proceed.

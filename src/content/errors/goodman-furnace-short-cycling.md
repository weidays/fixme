## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single Goodman fault code — it's a behavior. Your furnace fires up, runs for a short time (often under 5 minutes), shuts off before the house reaches temperature, then starts again minutes later. On Goodman boards with a diagnostic LED, short cycling frequently pairs with a flash code for **open high-limit / open limit switch** or **flame not sensed**, depending on the root cause.

Whether your furnace shows a code at all depends on the model. Newer GMVC/GMEC boards flash sequences you can read through the sight glass; older single-stage GCES/GMES units may just cycle without a clear code. If yours shows a lockout after several short cycles, see the related Goodman Furnace Lockout page.

Short cycling almost always means one of two things: the furnace is **overheating and self-protecting**, or the **flame signal is dropping out** mid-cycle. Both are worth fixing promptly because they accelerate wear on the igniter, blower motor and heat exchanger.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, heat builds in the cabinet, and the high-limit switch trips to shut off the burners. Most common cause by far.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — a carbon-coated flame rod stops detecting flame, so the board shuts the gas valve after a few seconds, then retries. This causes rapid on-off cycling.
3. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or a collapsed return duct starves airflow and overheats the heat exchanger.
4. **Thermostat problems** — poor location (near a vent or sunlight), an aggressive heat anticipator, loose wiring, or dead batteries can command short cycles.
5. **Failing high-limit switch** — a weak or intermittent limit switch trips early even when airflow is fine.
6. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home satisfies the thermostat too quickly, causing chronic short cycles. Common in older or poorly matched installs.
7. **Flue/venting or pressure switch issues** — a partially blocked flue or marginal inducer/pressure switch can interrupt cycles.
8. **Cracked heat exchanger or control board fault** — less common, but a bad board or safety trip can cut cycles short.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do **not** open the gas valve, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a locked-out furnace.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter. If it's gray, matted or you can't see light through it, replace it. Run the furnace and see if cycles lengthen.
- **Thermostat:** Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Check that it's not mounted in direct sunlight or near a supply vent. Set it to "Heat" and a few degrees above room temperature.
- **Breaker:** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on. A tripped or half-seated breaker can cause odd behavior — flip it fully off, then on.
- **Vents and registers:** Walk the house. Open any closed supply registers and clear furniture or rugs off return grilles. Closed vents are a leading airflow-starvation cause.
- **Furnace switch and door:** Make sure the service switch is on and the blower door is fully seated — a loose door depresses a safety interlock and can cause cycling.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models):** On 90%+ Goodman furnaces, check for a full or clogged condensate trap/line; overflow can trip a float switch and interrupt cycles.

If cycles are still short after these checks, it's time for a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician works root-cause first, not parts-swap first. Expect them to:

- **Read the LED flash code** through the sight glass and note any limit or flame-sense faults.
- **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace and compare it to the rating-plate spec. High rise confirms an airflow problem.
- **Inspect and test the flame sensor** — clean it and measure microamp signal. A weak signal (below spec, often under ~1–2 µA) explains flame dropout.
- **Check the high-limit switch** for continuity and correct trip behavior.
- **Verify blower operation** and static pressure to find duct restrictions or a weak blower motor.
- **Inspect the flue, inducer and pressure switch** for blockage or marginal operation.
- **Examine the heat exchanger** for cracks if overheating has been chronic.

Sanity-check any quote: if they want to replace the control board without measuring airflow or cleaning the flame sensor first, ask why.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fires, runs a few min, shuts off, repeats | Clogged filter / overheating | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, check limit switch |
| Flame lights then dies after ~5–10 sec | Dirty flame sensor | Replace batteries, replace filter | Clean/replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Cycles regardless of temperature | Thermostat location/wiring | New batteries, relocate check | Rewire, adjust anticipator, replace stat |
| Trips even with clean filter | Weak high-limit switch | None | Test and replace limit switch |
| Warm house, very short runs | Oversized furnace | None | Confirm sizing, adjust staging |
| Cycles with gurgling near unit | Condensate/pressure switch | Clear condensate line | Clear trap, test pressure switch |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US pricing including parts and labor. Your area and model affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY, free labor).
- **Flame sensor clean or replace:** $80–$250. The part is cheap ($15–$40); most cost is labor and the service call.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 depending on model.
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$350.
- **Pressure switch / condensate trap service:** $150–$400.
- **Inducer motor:** $300–$600.
- **Control board:** $300–$650.
- **Heat exchanger (or full furnace replacement if cracked):** $1,000+ — often the point where replacing an aging furnace makes more sense.

Many short-cycling calls end up being a $150–$250 flame sensor cleaning or a free filter swap — so start with the cheap causes.

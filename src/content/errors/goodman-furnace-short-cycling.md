## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single flash code on your Goodman furnace — it's a **behavior**: the burners fire and shut off again after only a short run, then repeat, without ever satisfying the thermostat or delivering steady heat. On Goodman boards you may or may not see a diagnostic LED flash code alongside it, depending on *why* it's cycling.

A normal Goodman heat cycle runs several minutes at a time. If yours runs for seconds to a couple of minutes and quits — over and over — something is interrupting the cycle early. The most common triggers are restricted airflow causing the high-limit switch to trip, a weak flame signal, or an oversized/mis-configured system. Because some causes involve overheating and the heat exchanger, we rate this **pro-level** once the easy DIY checks are ruled out.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts return airflow, the heat exchanger overheats, and the high-limit switch cuts the burners. Most common cause by far.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — the board can't reliably prove flame, so it shuts down and retries within seconds. Look for a very short run before shutoff.
3. **Blocked airflow beyond the filter** — closed supply registers, crushed flex duct, dirty blower wheel, or an undersized return also overheat the unit.
4. **Faulty or tripping high-limit switch** — either responding to a real overheat or failing on its own.
5. **Thermostat problems** — bad location (near a supply vent), poor wiring, dying batteries, or a too-tight cycle-rate setting causing rapid on/off.
6. **Flue/venting or pressure-switch issues** — a blocked intake/exhaust or a fluttering pressure switch can interrupt cycles.
7. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats fast, satisfies the thermostat quickly, and short cycles by design. Common in poorly sized installs.
8. **Control board fault** — less common, but a failing integrated ignition control can misread inputs and cut cycles short.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or opening sealed compartments:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This resolves a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat** — replace batteries if it takes them. Check that it's set to **Heat** and the fan is on **Auto**, not On. Make sure it isn't mounted in direct sunlight or above a supply vent.
- **Supply and return vents** — walk the house and open any closed registers. Blocked vents starve airflow and overheat the furnace.
- **Breaker** — if the furnace lost power, confirm the breaker isn't tripped. Reset it **once**. Do not repeatedly cycle power to a locked-out furnace.
- **Return-air path** — make sure furniture, rugs, or storage aren't blocking the return grille.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models)** — on condensing Goodman units, a full or clogged condensate line/trap can trip a safety and interrupt cycles. Check for standing water or an overflowing pan.

If clean airflow and a good thermostat don't fix it, stop here — the remaining causes involve the flame sensor, limit switch, pressure switch, or board.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should:

1. **Read the LED flash code** on the control board and note it (behavior varies by board revision).
2. **Check static pressure and airflow** — measure temperature rise across the furnace and compare it to the rating-plate spec. High rise confirms restricted airflow.
3. **Clean or test the flame sensor** — measure microamp flame signal; a weak or drifting reading explains short runs.
4. **Test the high-limit switch** — verify it opens at the correct temperature and isn't tripping prematurely or failing.
5. **Inspect venting and the pressure switch** — check for blocked intake/exhaust and confirm the pressure switch closes and holds.
6. **Evaluate sizing and thermostat setup** — heat rise, cycle rate, and thermostat location, especially if the furnace was recently installed.
7. **Inspect the heat exchanger** if overheating is suspected — a cracked exchanger is a safety condition requiring shutdown.

If a tech wants to replace the control board without first checking airflow, filter, and flame signal, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, quits, restarts | Dirty filter / restricted airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, clean blower, check limit |
| Fires 30–90 sec then shuts off | Dirty flame sensor | None safe (sealed compartment) | Clean/replace sensor, test flame microamps |
| Rapid on/off, thermostat clicks | Thermostat setting/location/wiring | Replace batteries, check location | Adjust cycle rate, repair wiring |
| Overheats, hot smell | Blower/airflow fault, limit tripping | Check filter and vents | Diagnose blower, limit, heat exchanger |
| Cycles fast even when clean | Oversized furnace or board fault | None | Verify sizing, test control board |
| Cycling with standing water (condensing units) | Clogged condensate/pressure switch | Check for overflow | Clear condensate, test pressure switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 DIY. No labor if you do it.
- **Flame sensor cleaning** — $80–$180 as part of a service call.
- **Flame sensor replacement** — $100–$250.
- **High-limit switch** — $150–$350.
- **Pressure switch** — $150–$350.
- **Thermostat replacement** — $150–$400 depending on model.
- **Blower motor cleaning / capacitor** — $150–$450.
- **Control board** — $300–$600+; verify the diagnosis first, as boards are often misdiagnosed.
- **Duct/airflow correction or resizing** — $500–$2,000+ if the furnace is genuinely oversized or the return is undersized.

A basic diagnostic service call typically runs **$90–$180**, often credited toward the repair.

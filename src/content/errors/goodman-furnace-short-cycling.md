## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single Goodman fault code — it's a behavior. Your furnace fires up, runs for a short time (often less than three minutes), shuts off, then restarts a short while later. On many Goodman boards the diagnostic LED will flash a **open-limit** or **limit switch open** code when overheating is the trigger, but airflow and thermostat problems frequently cause short cycling with **no code shown**.

The key point: short cycling is the furnace protecting itself. A safety device — most often the high-limit switch reacting to overheating — is cutting the burners off before the cycle finishes. It can also be caused by something telling the furnace to stop prematurely (a miscalibrated thermostat) or a flame-sensing failure. Left alone, it accelerates wear on the igniter, heat exchanger, and blower.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, the heat exchanger overheats, and the high-limit switch trips. Most common and cheapest to fix.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns starve airflow, same overheating result as a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty flame sensor** — a fouled sensor loses flame signal seconds after ignition, so the board shuts the gas valve. The furnace re-tries repeatedly, mimicking short cycling.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — an aging or out-of-spec limit switch can trip early even with adequate airflow.
5. **Thermostat problems** — bad location (near a supply vent), wiring issues, low batteries, or a mismatched thermostat causing rapid on/off.
6. **Oversized furnace** — an oversized unit satisfies the thermostat too quickly, then restarts soon after; this is a design issue, not a broken part.
7. **Failing inducer or pressure switch** — intermittent draft proving can interrupt cycles.
8. **Control board fault** — less common; a failing integrated furnace control can misread inputs and cut cycles short.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do **not** bypass safety switches, open the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a locked-out furnace.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the single most common fix.
- **Thermostat:** Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Set it to Heat and a few degrees above room temperature. Make sure it isn't mounted near a supply vent, lamp, or sunny window where drafts fool it.
- **Vents and returns:** Walk the house. Open closed supply registers and clear furniture, rugs, or boxes off return grilles. Aim to keep the large majority of registers open.
- **Breaker:** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on. A tripped or half-tripped breaker can cause odd cycling — reset it once.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models):** On condensing Goodman furnaces, a clogged condensate drain can trip the pressure switch. Check for standing water at the drain trap and pour a cleaning tablet or diluted solution through per your manual.

If short cycling continues after a clean filter, fresh thermostat batteries, and open vents, it's time for a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should:

- **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace (supply minus return) and compare it to the range on the Goodman data plate. High rise points to an airflow restriction.
- **Read the diagnostic LED** and pull any stored fault history from the board.
- **Test the high-limit switch** for continuity and correct trip behavior.
- **Check flame signal (microamps)** to catch a dirty or failing flame sensor.
- **Inspect the inducer and pressure switch** and verify draft, tubing, and condensate flow on condensing models.
- **Verify blower operation and speed tap** — a slow or failing blower motor causes overheating.
- **Confirm furnace sizing and thermostat setup** if hardware tests come back clean.

Watch out for a quote that jumps straight to a control board or heat exchanger without measuring temperature rise or checking the filter and flame sensor first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 1–3 min, blower keeps running after | Dirty filter / airflow restriction | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, check limit switch |
| Igniter glows, flame lights then dies in seconds | Dirty flame sensor | None beyond filter check | Clean or replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Cuts off early, LED shows open limit | Failing/tripping high-limit switch | Verify airflow first | Test and replace limit switch |
| Rapid on/off, no code | Thermostat location/wiring/batteries | Replace batteries, relocate check | Verify thermostat wiring and setup |
| Cycles interrupted, inducer pulses | Pressure switch / condensate blockage | Clear condensate drain | Test pressure switch and inducer |
| Frequent short runs, otherwise healthy | Oversized furnace | None | Confirm sizing, adjust staging if two-stage |
| Erratic cycling, tests all pass | Control board fault | None | Diagnose and replace integrated control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY, no service call).
- **Flame sensor clean or replacement:** $80–$250. The part is cheap; most cost is the diagnostic visit.
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$350.
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$350.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 depending on model.
- **Inducer motor:** $300–$600.
- **Integrated furnace control board:** $350–$600.
- **Blower motor:** $400–$700+.

A basic diagnostic service call usually runs **$90–$180** and is often credited toward the repair. Correcting an oversized-furnace issue may mean staging adjustments or, worst case, replacement — get a second opinion before any sizing-based replacement quote.

## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a specific Goodman fault code — it's a behavior. Your furnace fires up, runs for a short time (often under 3–5 minutes), shuts off, then repeats the cycle over and over without satisfying the thermostat.

On most Goodman furnaces there's no dedicated blink code for short cycling. Instead, the control board LED may flash codes tied to the *underlying* cause — for example an open limit switch, flame loss, or pressure switch fault. What you see depends on your board and model; a GMVC or GMVM variable-speed board reports differently than an older GMS8. Always read the LED flash pattern against the diagnostic label inside the blower door.

The key point: short cycling is a symptom. Your job (and your tech's) is to find what's cutting the cycle short.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets heat build in the heat exchanger, tripping the high-limit switch and shutting the burners off before the thermostat is satisfied.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — A carbon-coated flame rod fails to confirm flame, so the board closes the gas valve seconds after ignition, then retries. This produces very rapid on/off cycling.
3. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — Too many closed registers or blocked returns starve airflow and cause overheat trips, similar to a dirty filter.
4. **Thermostat problems** — Poor placement (near a supply vent or heat source), wiring issues, low batteries, or an anticipator/cycle-rate setting that's too aggressive.
5. **Failing high-limit switch** — A weak or miscalibrated limit switch may trip prematurely even with adequate airflow.
6. **Pressure switch / flue restriction** — Partially blocked intake or exhaust, or a marginal pressure switch, can drop the burners mid-cycle.
7. **Oversized furnace** — A furnace too large for the home heats quickly and satisfies the thermostat fast, then re-fires often. Common in poorly sized replacements; not a repair but a design issue.
8. **Control board or gas valve fault** — Less common, but a failing board or intermittent gas valve can interrupt cycles.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many short-cycling cases.
- **Thermostat:** Replace batteries if it takes them. Confirm it's set to Heat and the temperature is above room temp. Check it isn't mounted in direct sunlight or above a heat source.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and open closed registers. Make sure furniture, rugs, or curtains aren't blocking returns.
- **Breaker:** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on. A tripping breaker points to an electrical fault — call a pro.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models):** On condensing furnaces, a full or clogged condensate drain/trap can trip a float switch and interrupt cycles. Check for standing water and a full drain pan.
- **Air intake/exhaust:** Look outside for blocked PVC vent pipes (leaves, snow, nests).

Do **not** bypass the limit switch, open the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a furnace that keeps shutting down. Repeated overheating can crack a heat exchanger.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should:

1. **Read the LED flash code** on the control board to see what the furnace reports at shutdown.
2. **Check static pressure and airflow** — measuring temperature rise across the heat exchanger to confirm whether overheating is tripping the limit.
3. **Inspect and clean the flame sensor**, then measure microamp signal to confirm reliable flame proving.
4. **Test the high-limit and rollout switches** for correct operation and continuity.
5. **Verify the pressure switch** and inspect the inducer, flue, and intake for restrictions.
6. **Check thermostat wiring and cycle rate.**
7. **Confirm proper furnace sizing** if all components test good — an oversized unit is a design issue, not a broken part.

If a tech quotes a control board or gas valve without first checking the filter, airflow, and flame sensor, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 3–5 min then shuts off, restarts | Dirty filter / restricted airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, test limit switch |
| Fires, then quits after a few seconds | Dirty flame sensor | None (leave to pro) | Clean/replace sensor, verify microamps |
| Rapid on/off, cold spots | Oversized furnace | None | Confirm sizing, recommend staging fixes |
| Cycles with LED limit code | High-limit trip | Check filter & vents | Test/replace limit switch |
| Standing water near furnace | Clogged condensate / float | Clear visible drain, check pan | Clear trap, test float switch |
| Cycles tied to room temp swings | Thermostat placement/wiring | Replace batteries, relocate if possible | Repair wiring, adjust cycle rate |
| Blower runs, burners drop out | Pressure switch / flue block | Clear outdoor vent pipes | Test pressure switch, inspect inducer |

## Repair costs

| Fix | Typical US cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter replacement | $10 – $40 (DIY) |
| Flame sensor cleaning | $80 – $175 with service call |
| Flame sensor replacement | $120 – $250 |
| High-limit switch replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Pressure switch replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Thermostat replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Condensate trap/float service | $100 – $250 |
| Control board replacement | $350 – $600 |
| Gas valve replacement | $300 – $600 |

Prices vary by region, labor rates, and whether parts are under Goodman's warranty. Many Goodman parts carry a 10-year limited warranty if the unit was registered — you'll still pay labor.

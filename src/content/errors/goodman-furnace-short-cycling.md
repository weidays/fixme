## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single Goodman error code — it's a behavior. Your furnace starts a heating cycle, runs briefly, and shuts down before the thermostat is satisfied, then restarts a short time later. On most Goodman gas furnaces the control board may or may not store a diagnostic flash code, depending on the model and board revision. Common flash-code companions include limit-circuit faults or flame-sensing faults, but the same short-cycling symptom can occur with **no stored code at all**.

The furnace is protecting itself. Something is either shutting the burners down early (a safety switch opening) or telling the board to end the cycle prematurely (a thermostat or flame-signal problem). The job is figuring out which.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter.** Restricted airflow lets the heat exchanger overheat, tripping the high-limit switch. By far the most common cause and the first thing to check.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents.** Too many closed registers or blocked returns starve the blower and cause the same overheating trip.
3. **Dirty flame sensor.** A carbon-coated flame rod stops proving flame after a few seconds, so the board shuts off gas, then re-tries — a rapid on/off pattern.
4. **Thermostat problems.** Weak batteries, loose wiring, or a thermostat mounted in a hot/drafty spot can make the furnace cycle erratically.
5. **Failing high-limit switch or rollout switch.** A weak or faulty limit can open at normal temperatures. (Never bypass these — see the flame-rollout page.)
6. **Oversized furnace or duct restriction.** An oversized unit satisfies the thermostat too fast; poor duct design causes chronic overheating.
7. **Blower motor or capacitor issues.** A weak blower moves less air, causing overheat trips similar to a dirty filter.
8. **Faulty pressure switch or inducer.** Intermittent pressure-switch operation can end cycles early.
9. **Failing integrated control board or gas valve.** Least common, diagnosed only after the above are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if you smell gas or see scorching.

- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many short-cycling calls.
- **Check thermostat batteries.** Replace them if your model uses them, even if the screen still lights up.
- **Confirm the thermostat setting.** Set it to Heat and a few degrees above room temperature to force a clean call for heat.
- **Open blocked vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unobstructed by furniture or rugs. Keep the majority of registers open.
- **Check the breaker.** A tripped or partially tripped breaker can cause odd cycling. Reset it once — if it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Look at the condensate drain** (high-efficiency models). A clogged drain can trip a float switch and interrupt cycles. Clearing a visible clog is fine; don't disassemble the trap.

Do **not** reset a furnace repeatedly if it keeps locking out, and never jump out or bypass a limit or rollout switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows the same logic you can sanity-check against their quote:

1. **Reads the control board LED flash code** and any stored history to see what fault triggered the shutdown.
2. **Measures temperature rise** across the heat exchanger and compares it to the Goodman rating-plate spec. A high rise points to airflow restriction; a low rise points to blower or gas-input issues.
3. **Inspects and cleans the flame sensor**, then measures the microamp flame signal against spec (typically a few microamps minimum).
4. **Tests the high-limit and rollout switches** for continuity and correct trip behavior.
5. **Checks blower performance and capacitor**, plus static pressure in the ductwork.
6. **Verifies pressure switch and inducer operation** with a manometer.
7. **Only then** condemns the control board or gas valve — these are last, not first.

If a tech wants to replace a board or gas valve without checking the filter, airflow, and flame sensor first, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 2-5 min, blower keeps running, then restarts | Overheat / high-limit trip from dirty filter | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, check limit and blower |
| Burners light then go out after a few seconds, retries | Dirty flame sensor | Check filter first | Clean/replace sensor, test microamps |
| Erratic on/off, display flickers | Weak thermostat batteries or wiring | Replace batteries, check wire terminals | Test thermostat, verify wiring |
| Frequent cycling, whole rooms too hot/cold | Oversized unit or duct restriction | Open registers | Static pressure test, load calc |
| Cycles with clicking/buzzing at start | Failing capacitor or blower motor | None | Test capacitor, blower amps |
| High-efficiency unit stops, water near furnace | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear trap, verify float switch |
| Cycling with limit fault code, filter clean | Failing high-limit switch | None — safety part | Test and replace limit switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor. Regional and access differences apply.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 DIY.
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 DIY.
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $0 DIY; $80–$180 as a service call.
- **Flame sensor replacement:** $150–$300 installed.
- **High-limit or rollout switch:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Blower capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900 installed depending on ECM vs. PSC.
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Inducer motor:** $350–$650 installed.
- **Integrated control board:** $400–$700 installed.
- **Gas valve:** $400–$700 installed.

Most short-cycling calls end at the filter, flame-sensor, or limit-switch stage — the high-dollar parts are the exception, not the rule.

## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single Goodman fault code — it is a behavior. Your furnace fires the burners, runs for a short time (often under 3–5 minutes), then shuts off, only to restart moments later. On many Goodman boards you will see a diagnostic LED flashing a limit-open code (commonly a slow or fast flash pattern, which **varies by board** — check the label inside your blower door).

Short cycling almost always traces back to one of three things: the furnace is overheating and tripping a safety limit, it is losing flame and dropping out, or it is being told to stop by the thermostat or a safety switch. The goal is to find *why* it stops before the heat call is satisfied.

Because the causes span airflow, ignition, safeties and controls — and some involve the gas valve and heat exchanger — we rate this **pro** for anything beyond the safe checks below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, cabinet overheats, high-limit switch trips and cuts the burners. Most common cause by far.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — same effect as a dirty filter: too little airflow, overheating, limit trip.
3. **Dirty flame sensor** — the sensor stops proving flame, the board drops the gas valve, then retries. Produces a short "fire and die" cycle.
4. **Failing or marginal high-limit switch** — trips too early even with adequate airflow, or is degraded.
5. **Blocked condensate drain (high-efficiency models)** — a full drain trips the pressure switch/float, interrupting the cycle.
6. **Restricted flue or intake / failing inducer or pressure switch** — the pressure switch opens mid-cycle, dropping the burners.
7. **Thermostat problems** — a poorly located thermostat, short-cycle anticipator setting, or wiring/heat-anticipator issue commands rapid on/off.
8. **Oversized furnace** — heats the thermostat's area too quickly and satisfies the call before the cabinet stabilizes. This is a design issue, not a broken part.
9. **Control board or gas valve fault** — less common; intermittent output causes erratic cycling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do **not** open the gas valve, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a furnace that has locked out.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter. If it is gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it and run a full cycle. This resolves a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it is set to Heat and the setpoint is above room temperature. If it is battery-powered, install fresh batteries. Check that the fan is set to Auto, not On.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and make sure vents and return grilles are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs. Restricted airflow overheats the furnace.
- **Breaker:** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on. A tripped or partly-tripped breaker can cause erratic behavior.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency units):** Look for water pooling near the furnace or a full drain pan. If you can clearly see a clogged drain line, clearing it may restore normal operation. If you're unsure, leave it for the tech.

If the furnace still short cycles after a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the actual failure rather than swapping parts. Expect them to:

1. **Read the diagnostic LED** on the control board and note the flash pattern against the door label.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace and compare it to the rating-plate spec — high rise confirms an airflow/overheating problem.
3. **Check static pressure and blower operation** to rule out duct restriction and a weak blower motor.
4. **Test the flame sensor** microamp signal; a low reading means it needs cleaning or replacement.
5. **Test the high-limit switch and pressure switch** for correct operation and continuity.
6. **Inspect the inducer, flue and intake** for blockage on high-efficiency models, and check the condensate path.
7. **Verify the gas valve and control board output** if all mechanical causes are clear.

Sanity check: a quote to replace the board or gas valve *without* first checking filter, airflow, flame sensor and the safeties is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, restarts | Dirty filter / overheating | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, test limit switch |
| Burners light then die within seconds | Dirty flame sensor | None safe beyond filter check | Clean or replace flame sensor, check microamps |
| Cycling with water near furnace | Blocked condensate drain | Clear visible clog; add tablets | Clear drain, test float/pressure switch |
| Inducer runs but burners drop out | Pressure switch / flue restriction | None | Inspect flue/intake, test switch and inducer |
| Short cycles only in cold weather | Marginal limit or oversizing | Confirm clean filter | Evaluate airflow, sizing, thermostat |
| Rapid on/off even before warm | Thermostat / anticipator | Fresh batteries, check location | Correct wiring/anticipator or relocate stat |
| Erratic, no consistent pattern | Control board or gas valve | None | Test board output and gas valve |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US retail including labor; your area and model vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 — DIY.
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $80–$180 (often part of a diagnostic visit); **replacement** $150–$300.
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250.
- **Inducer motor:** $300–$600 installed.
- **Control board:** $350–$600 installed.
- **Gas valve:** $300–$600 installed.
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180, often credited toward the repair.

An oversized-furnace short-cycle problem isn't a "repair" — it's a ductwork or thermostat-strategy discussion, and honest techs will say so rather than sell parts.

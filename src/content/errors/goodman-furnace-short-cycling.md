## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single Goodman fault code — it is a **behavior**: your furnace fires up, runs for only a minute or two, shuts off, and then restarts again shortly after. Instead of one steady heat cycle, you get a rapid on-off pattern that never satisfies the thermostat properly.

On most Goodman gas furnaces, short cycling means one of two things is happening:

- A **safety switch is tripping the burners early** (usually the high-limit switch reacting to overheating, or a flame-sensing / ignition problem), or
- The **thermostat or control signal is telling the furnace to stop and start too often.**

Goodman control boards flash diagnostic LED codes through a small window on the burner-compartment door. The exact code and blink pattern **varies by board generation and model** (GMVC, GMEC, GMS8, GMH8 and modulating units all read differently). Check the wiring-diagram legend printed on the inside of your furnace door — it lists the blink codes for your specific board. If you see a limit-open or flame-loss code alongside the short cycling, that narrows the cause quickly.

Because short cycling frequently involves an overheating heat exchanger or a failing safety component, this is rated a **pro-level** problem once the basic checks below are done.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the heat exchanger overheat and trips the high-limit switch, cutting the burners early.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — closing too many registers or blocked returns starve the blower of airflow, same overheating result as a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty flame sensor** — a carbon-coated flame sensor loses its ability to prove flame, so the board shuts the gas valve seconds after ignition and retries.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — a limit switch that trips at too low a temperature (or is genuinely detecting overheating) ends the cycle prematurely.
5. **Thermostat problems** — dead batteries, poor wiring, a location near a heat source, or a mismatched/oversized "cycle rate" setting can rapid-cycle the call for heat.
6. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats the thermostat area fast, satisfies the call, then re-fires minutes later. Common in poorly matched installs.
7. **Cracked or dirty flue / blocked vent** — a restricted exhaust can trip the pressure switch, causing repeated aborted starts.
8. **Failing control board** — less common; an intermittent board can misread inputs and cycle the unit erratically.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Do not** open the gas valve, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a locked-out unit.

- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted or more than 90 days old, swap it. This resolves a large share of short-cycling calls. Note the size printed on the frame.
- **Open blocked vents and returns.** Make sure supply registers aren't closed and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs or boxes. Aim to keep the majority of registers open.
- **Check the thermostat.** Replace the batteries if it uses them. Make sure it isn't in direct sunlight or near a lamp/vent. Confirm it's set to "Heat" and the setpoint is above room temperature.
- **Reset the breaker once.** If the furnace lost power, flip the breaker fully off and back on a single time. If it trips again or cycles again immediately, stop and call a pro.
- **Look at the condensate drain (high-efficiency models).** A clogged condensate line or full trap can trip a float switch. Check for standing water near the furnace; a blocked drain can be gently cleared, but a full trap replacement is a technician job.
- **Watch the LED code.** Note the blink pattern through the door window and match it to the legend inside the panel — this helps any technician you call.

If new filter and open vents don't fix it, the cause is likely a safety switch, flame sensor or control issue — call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech should work through this sequence — use it to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Read the LED code** and confirm the blink pattern against the board legend.
2. **Check static pressure and airflow** — measure temperature rise across the heat exchanger and confirm it's within the range on the data plate. High rise points to airflow restriction.
3. **Inspect and clean the flame sensor**, then measure microamp flame current with a meter. Low microamps confirm a sensor fault.
4. **Test the high-limit switch** for continuity and correct trip temperature; verify it isn't tripping due to genuine overheating.
5. **Check the pressure switch and inducer** and inspect the flue for blockage.
6. **Verify thermostat wiring and cycle-rate settings**, and evaluate whether the furnace is oversized for the space.
7. **Inspect the control board** for signs of failure only after the above are ruled out.

A tech who wants to replace the board first, without checking airflow and the flame sensor, is skipping the cheap causes.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 1–3 min then shuts off, restarts | Dirty filter / airflow restriction | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, test limit switch |
| Burners light then die after a few seconds | Dirty flame sensor | Note LED code | Clean/replace sensor, test microamps |
| Fires, overheats, limit code flashes | Failing high-limit switch | Check airflow first | Test & replace limit switch |
| Rapid cycling with no overheating | Thermostat / cycle-rate setting | Replace batteries, check location | Reconfigure or replace thermostat |
| Heats fast, satisfies quickly, re-fires often | Oversized furnace | None | Evaluate sizing, adjust staging |
| Inducer runs but burners won't stay lit | Blocked flue / pressure switch | Clear obvious vent blockage outside | Test pressure switch, inspect flue |
| Erratic cycling, multiple codes | Failing control board | Note codes | Replace control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10 – $40 (DIY)
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $0 DIY – $120 with a service call
- **Flame sensor replacement:** $80 – $180
- **High-limit switch:** $120 – $300
- **Thermostat replacement:** $120 – $400 depending on model
- **Pressure switch:** $130 – $350
- **Inducer motor:** $300 – $600
- **Control board:** $350 – $600
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $75 – $175 (often credited toward the repair)

Most short-cycling fixes land cheap — a filter or flame-sensor cleaning. Board and inducer replacements are the top of the range and should only follow a proper diagnosis.

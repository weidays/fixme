## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single Goodman flash code — it's a symptom. Your furnace starts a heating cycle, then shuts down long before the thermostat is satisfied, and repeats this over and over. On Goodman boards you may or may not see a flash code (often a limit-switch or flame-sense related code, which varies by board revision). The pattern itself is the clue.

There are two broad flavors:

- **Very short burns (seconds):** the furnace lights but shuts off almost immediately — usually a flame-sense or ignition problem.
- **Short runs (2-5 minutes):** the furnace heats, then trips off on the high-limit switch — usually an airflow or overheating problem.

Because the underlying causes range from a $5 filter to a failing control board, and because overheating can eventually crack a heat exchanger, we rate this **pro** if simple checks don't fix it.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, heat builds up, the high-limit switch trips and kills the burners. By far the most common cause.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — a coated sensor rod can't "prove" flame, so the board shuts the gas valve seconds after ignition, then retries.
3. **Blocked or undersized return/supply airflow** — closed registers, crushed ductwork, blocked returns, or a failing blower motor all cause overheating trips.
4. **Faulty high-limit or rollout switch** — a weak or failing limit switch trips early even with adequate airflow.
5. **Flue/vent or condensate blockage** — a blocked exhaust or clogged condensate trap (on high-efficiency models) triggers pressure-switch faults and short cycles.
6. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats a thermostat too fast, then cycles frequently. This is a design issue, not a defect.
7. **Thermostat problems** — bad location (near a vent), loose wiring, or a failing thermostat can cause rapid on/off cycling.
8. **Failing control board or gas valve** — less common, but a board that mis-reads inputs or a valve that drops out will cause erratic cycling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Do not** bypass safety switches, open the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a furnace that has locked out.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Set it clearly to Heat and a few degrees above room temp. If it's battery-powered, install fresh batteries. Make sure it isn't mounted near a supply vent or in direct sun.
- **Breaker:** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on. If it keeps tripping, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Walk the house. Open any closed supply registers and clear furniture, rugs, or boxes blocking return grilles. Restricting airflow directly causes overheating cycles.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models):** Check that the condensate drain line and trap aren't clogged or frozen. A backed-up trap trips the pressure switch. Only empty/clear what's clearly accessible.

If short cycling continues after a clean filter and open vents, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will confirm the cause rather than swap parts. Expect them to:

1. **Watch a full cycle** and time it — seconds-long burns point to flame sense/ignition; 2-5 minute burns point to overheating.
2. **Read the flash code** on the control board and check the fault history if the board stores it.
3. **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace (return vs. supply air) and compare it to the Goodman data-plate spec. High rise confirms an airflow/overheating problem.
4. **Inspect and clean the flame sensor**, then measure microamp flame current.
5. **Test the high-limit and rollout switches** for continuity and correct trip behavior.
6. **Check the inducer, pressure switch, and flue** for blockages or weak draft.
7. **Verify blower operation** and static pressure to rule out a duct or motor restriction.

If a tech proposes replacing a control board or gas valve without first checking the filter, airflow, and flame sensor, ask them to justify it.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 2-5 min then shuts off, restarts | Dirty filter / restricted airflow | Replace filter, open all vents & returns | Measure temp rise, check blower & static pressure |
| Lights then dies in 5-10 seconds | Dirty flame sensor | Replace filter first; sensor cleaning is borderline DIY | Clean/replace sensor, measure flame microamps |
| Cycles even with clean filter | Failing high-limit switch | None — call a pro | Test and replace limit switch |
| Short cycles + condensate near unit | Clogged condensate trap (HE models) | Clear accessible drain line | Clear trap, verify pressure switch |
| Rapid on/off, minutes apart | Thermostat fault or bad location | Fresh batteries, confirm settings | Test/replace thermostat, reroute wiring |
| Furnace heats fast, cycles often, house comfortable | Oversized furnace | None | Confirm sizing, adjust staging/fan settings |
| Erratic cycling, other faults | Control board / gas valve | None — call a pro | Diagnose and replace board or valve |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area and model affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $5-$20 (DIY).
- **Flame sensor:** $15-$25 part DIY, or $150-$300 professionally installed with cleaning/diagnosis.
- **High-limit or rollout switch:** $150-$350 installed.
- **Pressure switch / condensate clearing:** $150-$350 installed.
- **Blower motor:** $400-$700 depending on ECM vs. PSC.
- **Thermostat:** $150-$400 installed depending on model.
- **Control board:** $350-$600 installed.
- **Gas valve:** $350-$600 installed.
- **Duct/airflow correction or resizing:** highly variable — get a load calculation.

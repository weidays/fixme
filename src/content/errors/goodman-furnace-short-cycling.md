## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single fault code — it's a behavior. Your Goodman furnace ignites, runs briefly, then shuts down before satisfying the thermostat, and repeats this over and over. On Goodman integrated control boards, short cycling is often accompanied by a diagnostic blink code visible through the sight glass on the blower door (for example, patterns indicating a high-limit/open-limit trip or loss of flame sense).

The exact blink pattern and its meaning **vary by furnace series and board revision**, so always match the LED code to the legend printed on the inside of your unit's blower door rather than assuming. The underlying issue is almost always one of three things: the furnace is **overheating** (airflow restriction), it is **losing flame signal** (dirty flame sensor), or the **thermostat or control** is telling it to stop and start too often.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the most common cause. Restricted airflow overheats the heat exchanger and trips the high-limit switch, cutting the burners early.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — a carbon- or oxide-coated flame rod fails to prove flame, so the board shuts the gas valve seconds after ignition, then retries.
3. **Blocked airflow beyond the filter** — closed/blocked supply registers, crushed or undersized ductwork, or a dirty blower wheel restricts air and causes limit trips.
4. **Thermostat problems** — dead batteries, poor location (near a heat source or in direct sunlight), loose wiring, or a mis-set anticipator/cycle rate.
5. **Faulty high-limit switch** — a failing limit switch can trip prematurely even when temperatures are normal.
6. **Flue or condensate issues (condensing models)** — a blocked flue, plugged condensate drain, or tripped pressure switch on high-efficiency Goodman furnaces can force repeated cycling.
7. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats fast and shuts off quickly; a design/installation issue, not a repair.
8. **Failing control board or gas valve** — less common, but a faulty board or intermittent gas valve can cause erratic cycling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or opening sealed compartments:

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many short-cycling cases.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Heat** and the setpoint is above room temperature. If it uses batteries, replace them with fresh ones.
- **Thermostat location:** Make sure it isn't in direct sun, near a lamp, vent, or drafty spot that gives it false temperature readings.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and open any closed registers and remove furniture/rugs blocking them. Blocked airflow causes overheating trips.
- **Circuit breaker:** If the furnace lost power, reset the breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Condensate drain (high-efficiency models):** Look for standing water or a full drain pan near the furnace. A clogged condensate line can trip a safety and cause cycling — but only clear it if it's an accessible external line; do not disassemble internal components.
- **Read the blink code:** Note the LED flash pattern through the sight glass and match it to the door legend. Have this ready when you call.

Do **not** bypass any safety switch, open the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting down. Repeated resets can hide a real fault.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech should work through causes in a logical order — use this to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint and read the board** — confirm short cycling and record the diagnostic blink code.
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower wheel cleanliness, static pressure, and duct/register restrictions. High static pressure points to airflow-driven overheating.
3. **Measure temperature rise** — compare supply and return temperatures against the rating-plate spec. A rise above spec confirms overheating.
4. **Test the flame sensor** — measure microamp flame signal; a low reading means a dirty or failing sensor. Cleaning or replacing the rod is a common quick fix.
5. **Test safeties** — check the high-limit switch, rollout switches, and (on condensing units) the pressure switch and flue/condensate path.
6. **Verify thermostat and control wiring** — check for loose connections, incorrect cycle rate, and confirm the thermostat isn't giving false calls.
7. **Confirm gas valve and board operation** — only after ruling out the cheaper causes above.

Be wary of a quote that jumps straight to a control board or gas valve without first documenting airflow, temperature rise, and flame signal.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fires, runs 2–4 min, shuts off, repeats | Overheating from dirty filter | Replace filter, open all vents | Check blower, measure temp rise, test limit |
| Burners light then die within seconds | Dirty flame sensor | Replace filter first; note blink code | Clean/replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Cycles with weak or uneven airflow | Dirty blower wheel or duct restriction | Confirm filter clean, vents open | Clean blower, measure static pressure |
| Random starts/stops, no clear pattern | Thermostat batteries/wiring/location | Replace batteries, relocate sensor away from heat | Check wiring, cycle rate, replace stat if faulty |
| Cycling on high-efficiency unit, water near furnace | Clogged condensate/pressure switch | Clear accessible external drain line | Clear condensate, test pressure switch and flue |
| Trips high-limit repeatedly, airflow looks fine | Failing high-limit switch | None — call a pro | Test and replace limit switch |
| Erratic cycling after all above ruled out | Control board or gas valve fault | None — call a pro | Diagnose and replace board or gas valve |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined unless noted:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 or less (DIY).
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** often included in a service call ($90–$180); **replacement** $150–$300.
- **High-limit switch replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Pressure switch (condensing models):** $150–$350.
- **Blower wheel cleaning:** $150–$400 depending on access.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200.
- **Control board replacement:** $350–$600+.
- **Gas valve replacement:** $300–$600+.
- **Oversized/undersized furnace:** no cheap fix; correcting a sizing problem may require duct changes or, in extreme cases, equipment replacement — get a load calculation before spending big.

Diagnostic/service-call fees typically run **$90–$180** and are often credited toward the repair.

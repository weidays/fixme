## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single fault code—it's a behavior. Your Goodman furnace ignites, runs briefly (often under 5 minutes), then shuts down before the thermostat is satisfied, and repeats this pattern over and over. On Goodman integrated furnace control (IFC) boards, you may or may not see a diagnostic LED flash pattern, depending on the cause. If the furnace is tripping the high-limit switch, some boards flash a code indicating an open limit; if it's a flame-sensing dropout, you may see a flame-loss or ignition-related code instead.

Because the underlying trigger varies, short cycling points to a family of problems—airflow restriction, overheating, weak flame sensing, thermostat issues, or an oversized unit—rather than one part. This page walks through the most common ones in order.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the heat exchanger overheat, tripping the high-limit switch and killing the burners early.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — A carbon-coated flame rod stops proving flame, so the board shuts the gas valve within seconds of ignition, then retries. This causes rapid on-off cycling.
3. **Overheating / high-limit trips** — Blocked returns, closed supply registers, a failing blower motor, or undersized ductwork cause the furnace to overheat and cut out.
4. **Thermostat problems** — Weak batteries, a poorly located thermostat (near a supply vent or heat source), or short-cycle anticipator/differential settings that cycle the unit too fast.
5. **Blocked or restricted flue / pressure switch issues** — On 90%+ Goodman furnaces, a partially blocked condensate line or vent trips the pressure switch mid-cycle.
6. **Oversized furnace** — A unit too large for the home reaches the set temperature very quickly, then shuts off—normal operation for the wrong-sized equipment.
7. **Failing control board or wiring** — Intermittent connections or a faulty IFC board can misread flame or limit signals and cycle the unit.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do—no panel removal beyond the blower door, no bypassing safety switches:

- **Replace the air filter.** Pull the filter and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Check the thermostat.** Replace the batteries with fresh ones. Make sure it's set to "Heat," the setpoint is above room temperature, and the fan is on "Auto."
- **Check your breaker.** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on. If it has tripped, reset it once—if it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Open all supply registers and clear returns.** Make sure no vents are closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs. Restricted airflow causes overheating.
- **Check the condensate drain (96% AFUE models).** Look for standing water near the furnace or a clogged drain line/pan. A backed-up condensate line can trip the pressure switch.
- **Verify the thermostat location.** If it's above a supply vent, fireplace, or in a hot spot, it may satisfy too quickly.

Do not remove access panels beyond the blower door, jump out safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. **Read the IFC diagnostic LED** through the sight glass and note any flash codes for limit, pressure switch, or flame faults.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the heat exchanger and compare it to the rating-plate spec—high rise confirms an airflow/overheating problem.
3. **Test airflow and static pressure** to identify blocked ducts, a failing blower motor, or undersized returns.
4. **Check the flame sensor microamp signal** with a meter; a reading below spec (typically under ~1–2 µA) means the sensor needs cleaning or replacing.
5. **Inspect and test safety switches**—high-limit, rollout, and pressure switches—for correct opening/closing and proper resistance.
6. **Verify the flue and condensate path** on high-efficiency units.
7. **Confirm sizing and thermostat settings** if no hardware fault is found.

If a tech quotes a control board or blower motor without first checking the filter, temperature rise, and flame sensor, ask them to walk you through those readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 3–5 min, shuts off, repeats | Dirty filter / overheating | Replace filter, open all vents | Measure temp rise, test blower & limit |
| Fires then dies within seconds | Dirty flame sensor | None safe—call pro | Clean/replace flame sensor, test µA |
| Cycles with LED limit code | High-limit trip | Check filter & registers | Diagnose airflow, replace limit switch |
| Short cycles, water near unit | Clogged condensate (96%) | Clear visible drain blockage | Clear line, test pressure switch |
| Reaches temp fast, shuts off | Oversized furnace | Verify thermostat placement | Confirm sizing/load calc |
| Erratic, no clear pattern | Thermostat or control board | Replace batteries | Test wiring, replace IFC board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $80–$150 (often part of a service call)
- **Flame sensor replacement:** $80–$250
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$400
- **Pressure switch:** $100–$300
- **Condensate line clearing:** $75–$250
- **Blower motor (ECM/PSC):** $300–$800+
- **Integrated furnace control board:** $300–$600+
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180 (often credited toward the repair)

An oversized-furnace fix has no cheap parts answer—options range from airflow tuning to, worst case, replacement, which is a major cost. Get a load calculation before agreeing to that.

## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single Goodman error code — it is a behavior. Your furnace starts a heating cycle, then shuts down after a short period (seconds to a couple of minutes) and restarts, over and over, without satisfying the thermostat.

On most Goodman gas furnaces the integrated ignition control board flashes a diagnostic LED code that points to *why* it dropped out — common ones being a limit switch (open on temperature rise), loss of flame signal, or a pressure/inducer fault. **The exact flash pattern and its meaning vary by board and model year**, so read the legend printed on the blower-door label or in your service manual rather than assuming.

If your furnace has fully **locked out** (no heat, no restart attempts) rather than repeatedly retrying, see the Goodman Furnace Lockout page in Related codes instead.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricted airflow overheats the heat exchanger, tripping the high-limit switch. By far the most common and cheapest cause.
2. **Dirty or failing flame sensor** — the board lights the burners, cannot confirm flame, and shuts the gas valve within a few seconds. Very common on older units.
3. **Blocked or closed supply/return airflow** — closed registers, crushed ductwork, or a stuck blower door switch reduce airflow like a dirty filter would.
4. **Overheating / open high-limit switch** — caused by low airflow, a failing blower motor, or a genuinely bad limit switch.
5. **Pressure switch / inducer / vent issues** — a blocked exhaust or condensate line, or weak inducer, can cause the pressure switch to open mid-cycle.
6. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats fast and cuts out; typically an install-era problem, present since day one.
7. **Thermostat problems** — bad location (near a vent), loose wiring, low batteries, or a short cycle setting.
8. **Failing control board or gas valve** — least common, diagnosed only after the above are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Do not** bypass safety switches, open the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a locked-out unit.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter. If it's gray, matted or you can't see light through it, replace it and run the furnace again. This alone fixes many short-cycling cases.
- **Thermostat:** Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm it's set to Heat and the target temp is above room temp. Make sure it isn't mounted in direct sunlight or above a supply vent.
- **Registers and returns:** Open all supply registers and confirm return-air grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs. Restricted airflow mimics a hardware failure.
- **Breaker:** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on (not tripped or half-set). Toggle it once, firmly, if in doubt.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models):** Check that the condensate drain line and any drain pan aren't clogged or overflowing — a full trap can open the pressure switch and stop the cycle.
- **Vents outside:** Make sure exterior intake and exhaust pipes aren't blocked by snow, leaves, or a bird nest.

If short cycling continues after a clean filter and open airflow, it's a pro job — flame sensors, limit switches, inducers and gas valves involve live gas and electrical work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just swap parts. Expect them to:

1. **Read the LED flash code** on the control board and note it against the Goodman legend for your model.
2. **Watch a full cycle** — timing how long the unit runs before dropping out and at what point (ignition, flame proving, mid-run).
3. **Measure temperature rise** across the heat exchanger and compare it to the rating-plate spec; a high rise confirms low airflow.
4. **Check the flame signal** in microamps with a meter — a weak reading points to a dirty sensor or poor ground.
5. **Test the high-limit and rollout switches** for continuity and correct trip behavior.
6. **Verify the pressure switch and inducer** — checking vacuum, vent for blockage, and condensate drainage.
7. **Inspect the blower motor and capacitor** if airflow is suspect.
8. Only after all that, consider the **control board or gas valve**.

Sanity check: if a quote jumps straight to a control board or gas valve without airflow, flame-signal and limit-switch testing, ask them to show their readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 3–5 min, then shuts off, restarts | Overheating / dirty filter or high-limit trip | Replace filter, open vents/returns | Measure temp rise, test limit switch, check blower |
| Lights burners, cuts off in ~5 sec | Dirty or failing flame sensor / poor ground | Replace filter first | Clean or replace flame sensor, verify ground & microamps |
| Cycles with LED flash code on board | Board-reported fault (limit/pressure/flame) | Read flash legend on door label | Diagnose per code, test the flagged component |
| Inducer runs, unit stops before ignition | Pressure switch open, blocked vent or clogged condensate | Clear exterior vents; check drain line | Test pressure switch, inducer, and venting |
| Fires fine but blower barely moves air | Failing blower motor or capacitor | Confirm filter is clean | Test motor, capacitor, wiring |
| Short cycles since installation | Oversized furnace | None | Evaluate sizing / staging options |
| Random cycling, all else fine | Thermostat wiring/location/batteries | Replace batteries, check location | Test thermostat and control wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Regional rates and model vary.

- **Air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Flame sensor clean:** often folded into a service/diagnostic call, roughly $90–$180
- **Flame sensor replacement:** $150–$300
- **High-limit switch replacement:** $150–$350
- **Pressure switch replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Inducer motor assembly:** $350–$650
- **Blower motor / capacitor:** $150 (capacitor) to $600+ (ECM motor)
- **Control board replacement:** $350–$650
- **Gas valve replacement:** $400–$700
- **Diagnostic / service call (typical):** $90–$200, often credited toward the repair

If a tech recommends a full furnace replacement due to an oversized unit or repeated component failures, get a second opinion and a load calculation first.

## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a specific Goodman blink code — it's a behavior. Your furnace starts a heat call, runs for a short time (often under 3–5 minutes), shuts off before satisfying the thermostat, then restarts a short while later. This on-off-on pattern repeats.

On most Goodman integrated control boards, short cycling often shows up alongside a diagnostic LED flash — commonly a **limit-switch open** or **flame-sense** related code — but the exact flash pattern varies by board revision. Check the label inside your blower door for the LED legend specific to your model.

Short cycling matters because it wears out the igniter, inducer, and blower fast, wastes fuel, and in some cases signals overheating or a compromised heat exchanger.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow makes the furnace overheat and the high-limit switch shuts it down before the thermostat is satisfied.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns starve airflow and trip the limit, same as a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty flame sensor** — a fouled sensor loses its ability to detect flame, so the board shuts off the gas seconds after ignition, then retries.
4. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats the air quickly, satisfies the thermostat fast, and cycles frequently by design. Common in poorly matched installs.
5. **Failing high-limit or rollout switch** — an aging or intermittently faulty limit opens prematurely.
6. **Weak or failing pressure switch / restricted flue or condensate** — a marginal draft signal drops out mid-cycle, tripping the inducer safety.
7. **Thermostat problems** — bad location (near a heat source), loose wiring, or a failing thermostat sending erratic calls.
8. **Failing control board** — less common, but a board with degraded relays or logic can cut cycles short.
9. **Cracked heat exchanger** — least common but most serious; airflow through a crack can affect flame and trip safeties. Requires professional confirmation.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or opening sealed compartments:

- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or older than 90 days, swap it for the correct size. This resolves a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Open closed vents.** Make sure supply registers throughout the house are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Heat, the setpoint is above room temperature, and — for battery models — replace the batteries. Make sure it isn't in direct sunlight or above a lamp.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm the furnace breaker hasn't half-tripped; flip it fully off, then on.
- **Check the condensate drain (high-efficiency models).** A clogged condensate line or full trap can trip a pressure/float switch. Clear standing water if you can see and reach the drain safely.

If short cycling continues after a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a professional. **Do not** bypass or jumper any limit or rollout switch, and do not repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting down.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech should work methodically — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Read the LED flash code** at the control board and note it against the model legend.
2. **Verify airflow** — filter condition, blower amp draw, static pressure across the furnace, and ductwork sizing.
3. **Measure temperature rise** and compare to the rating-plate spec. High rise points to airflow restriction and overheating.
4. **Test the flame sensor** with a microamp meter; clean or replace if signal is weak.
5. **Check the limit and rollout switches** for continuity and correct opening temperature.
6. **Test the pressure switch and inducer draft**, and inspect the flue and condensate path.
7. **Inspect the heat exchanger** with a camera or combustion analysis if overheating or flame disturbance is suspected.

A quote that jumps straight to "replace the board" or "replace the furnace" without checking airflow and the flame sensor first deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, restarts | Dirty filter / blocked vents overheating unit | Replace filter, open vents | Measure static pressure, temp rise |
| Ignites then shuts off in seconds, retries | Dirty or failing flame sensor | Clean sensor if accessible | Test microamps, replace sensor |
| Cycles fast even with clean filter | Oversized furnace or bad thermostat | Reposition/replace thermostat | Verify sizing, calibrate controls |
| Inducer runs, burners cut mid-cycle | Weak pressure switch / flue or condensate blockage | Clear condensate drain | Test pressure switch, inspect flue |
| LED shows limit open, unit hot | High-limit switch tripping or failing | Confirm airflow first | Test/replace limit switch |
| Flame flickers, faint gas smell, cycling | Possible cracked heat exchanger | Stop use, ventilate | Camera/combustion inspection |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts plus labor; regional variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 DIY.
- **Flame sensor:** $80–$250 installed; the part itself is often under $20.
- **High-limit or rollout switch:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 depending on model.
- **Control board:** $350–$700 installed.
- **Blower motor:** $400–$700 installed.
- **Cracked heat exchanger:** $600–$1,500+ for the exchanger alone; on older units, replacing the furnace ($3,000–$6,500) is often the more economical choice. Confirm the diagnosis before authorizing this.

## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single Goodman error code—it's a **behavior**: your furnace fires up, runs for only a minute or two, shuts off, then restarts again shortly after. On Goodman furnaces with an integrated ignition control board, this often shows up alongside a flashing LED fault code visible through the sight glass on the blower door.

Short cycling is the furnace protecting itself. It's usually the control board or a safety switch (like the high-limit or flame sensor) doing exactly what it's designed to do: cutting the burners when something is wrong. The trick is figuring out *what* it's reacting to.

Because the fault codes and board revisions vary across Goodman series (GMVC, GCVC, GMES, GMSS, GC9), **read the LED flash pattern against the legend printed on the inside of your blower door**—the same blink count can mean different things on different boards.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricted return airflow makes the heat exchanger overheat and trips the high-limit switch, shutting burners off early. By far the most common cause.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — The board can't confirm a stable flame, so it drops gas seconds after ignition and retries. Burners light, then die repeatedly.
3. **Overheating from restricted airflow** — Beyond the filter: closed/blocked supply vents, undersized ductwork, a dirty blower wheel, or a failing blower motor.
4. **Faulty high-limit or rollout switch** — A limit switch that trips prematurely (or is genuinely responding to a hot spot) will short cycle the unit.
5. **Flue/vent or condensate blockage** — On high-efficiency models, a blocked exhaust or clogged condensate trap trips the pressure switch mid-cycle.
6. **Thermostat problems** — Bad location (near a supply register), low batteries, loose wiring, or a "cycles per hour" setting that's too aggressive.
7. **Oversized furnace** — An oversized unit satisfies the thermostat too fast, then short cycles all season. Common in replacements sized by rule-of-thumb, not by load calc.
8. **Failing control board or gas valve** — Less common; diagnosed after the above are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here—do not open the gas valve, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a locked-out unit.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm it's set to **Heat** and the temperature is above room temp. Check that it isn't mounted in direct sun or above a supply register.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and open any closed registers. Move furniture, rugs, or boxes off vents. Blocked airflow causes overheating.
- **Breaker:** If the furnace has tripped its breaker, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models):** Look for standing water or a full condensate line/trap. A clogged drain can trip the pressure switch. You can clear a visible external clog, but don't disassemble internal traps.
- **Read the LED code:** Note the blink pattern through the sight glass and match it to the legend on the blower door. Give this to your technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work through this in order so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Read the fault code** off the board's LED and interpret it against the correct board revision.
2. **Check airflow and temperature rise** with a manometer/thermometer—comparing measured rise against the range stamped on the unit's rating plate. Too high = overheating.
3. **Inspect and test the flame sensor** (microamp reading) and clean or replace it.
4. **Test the high-limit and rollout switches** for continuity and proper trip behavior.
5. **Check the pressure switch and flue** on high-efficiency models—verify draft and clear condensate.
6. **Inspect the blower wheel and motor** for buildup or weak performance.
7. **Inspect the heat exchanger** for cracks or hot spots, especially if overheating is confirmed.
8. **Verify sizing** if the unit cycles fast even with everything else healthy—an oversized furnace can't be "repaired," only correctly staged or replaced.

If a tech jumps straight to "replace the board" without measuring airflow and testing switches, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 2–3 min, shuts off, restarts | Dirty filter / overheating | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, clean blower |
| Burners light then die in seconds | Dirty flame sensor | Replace filter first | Clean/replace sensor, check microamps |
| Repeated on/off, LED shows limit fault | High-limit tripping | Check airflow only | Test limit switch, find heat source |
| Cycling with water near furnace | Condensate/pressure switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear trap, test pressure switch |
| Fast cycles even when clean | Oversized unit / thermostat | Check thermostat cph setting | Load calc, adjust staging |
| Cycling + hot/burning smell | Overheat or heat exchanger | Shut off, call pro | Inspect heat exchanger |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your price varies by region and by Goodman model.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries / minor thermostat fix:** $5–$40 (DIY) or $100–$300 for a new thermostat installed.
- **Flame sensor clean/replace:** $80–$250.
- **High-limit or rollout switch:** $150–$400.
- **Pressure switch / condensate clearing:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900 depending on ECM vs. PSC.
- **Control board:** $350–$700.
- **Cracked heat exchanger:** $1,200+ (often makes replacement more economical on older units).
- **Correcting an oversized furnace:** effectively a replacement, $3,500–$7,500+ (rare, but the honest answer when sizing is the root cause).

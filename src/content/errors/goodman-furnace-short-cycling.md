## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single Goodman error code — it's a behavior. Your furnace starts a heating cycle, runs for a short time (often under 3 minutes), shuts the burners off, then restarts a short time later, repeating over and over without properly warming the house.

On Goodman furnaces, this pattern is usually a safety response, not a random glitch. The integrated control board is doing what it's designed to do: it detects a problem — most often overheating or loss of flame signal — and stops the burners to protect the equipment. The board may or may not flash a diagnostic LED code while this happens, and the meaning of those flashes varies by model and board revision (single-stage vs. two-stage/modulating). Check the label inside the burner-compartment door for your specific flash-code chart.

The key point: short cycling is a symptom. The fix depends on *why* the furnace keeps shutting down.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter.** Restricted airflow lets heat build up in the cabinet until the high-limit switch trips. Most common cause by far — and often free to fix.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents, or undersized ductwork.** Same overheating result as a dirty filter: too little air moving across the heat exchanger.
3. **Dirty flame sensor.** A carbon-coated sensor reads a weak or absent flame signal; the board shuts gas off for safety, then retries. Causes rapid on-off cycling within the first minute.
4. **Failing high-limit or rollout limit switch.** A weak or miscalibrated limit trips early, even with adequate airflow.
5. **Weak or dying hot-surface igniter.** Inconsistent ignition causes the board to abort and retry cycles.
6. **Oversized furnace ("too big" for the home).** Heats the thermostat's air fast, satisfies the call, shuts off, then re-fires soon after. Common on replacement units sized incorrectly.
7. **Thermostat problems.** Bad location (near a vent or in direct sun), loose wiring, low batteries, or a short in the thermostat wire causing false calls.
8. **Blower motor or capacitor issues.** If the blower can't move air, the cabinet overheats and the limit trips.
9. **Cracked heat exchanger or draft/pressure-switch faults.** These can interrupt cycles and demand professional attention.
10. **Control board fault.** Least common, but a failing board can misread inputs and abort cycles.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do **not** open the gas valve manually, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a furnace that has locked itself out.

- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or older than 90 days, swap it. This alone resolves a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Check every supply and return vent.** Open closed registers and remove any furniture, rugs, or boxes blocking them. Blocked returns starve the furnace of air.
- **Check the thermostat.** Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm it's set to HEAT and the setpoint is above room temperature. Make sure it isn't mounted near a supply vent or in direct sunlight.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully ON (not tripped or half-tripped). Flip it fully off, then on, once.
- **Look at the condensate drain** (high-efficiency models). A clogged drain or full condensate trap can trip a float switch and interrupt cycles. Clear visible blockages at the drain outlet.
- **Read the diagnostic LED.** Through the sight glass in the burner door, note the flash pattern and match it to the chart on the door label — it helps your technician.

If short cycling continues after a fresh filter and open vents, call a pro. Repeated overheating stresses the heat exchanger.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work in this order — use it to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Read the control-board LED history** and match flash codes to the model's chart.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace (supply minus return temp) and compare it to the rating-plate spec. High rise points to an airflow restriction; low rise to a gas or blower issue.
3. **Check the flame sensor** with a microamp meter for adequate flame signal; clean or replace if low.
4. **Test the high-limit and rollout switches** for correct opening temperature and continuity.
5. **Verify blower operation** — motor amp draw, capacitor value, and wheel condition.
6. **Inspect the igniter** resistance and visually for cracks.
7. **Check the pressure switch and inducer** for proper draft.
8. **Inspect the heat exchanger** for cracks — critical if overheating has been chronic.
9. **Evaluate sizing** if the unit is short-cycling with no fault (oversized equipment).

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fires, runs 2-4 min, shuts off, repeats | Overheating from dirty filter | Replace filter; open vents | Measure temp rise; test limit switch |
| Burners light then die within seconds | Dirty/failing flame sensor | None (component job) | Clean or replace flame sensor; check microamps |
| Igniter glows, no consistent flame | Weak hot-surface igniter | None | Test/replace igniter |
| Runs full cycles but re-fires quickly | Oversized unit or thermostat location | Relocate thermostat away from vents | Verify sizing/staging; adjust setup |
| Blower struggles, cabinet very hot | Blower motor/capacitor fault | None | Test motor amps and capacitor |
| Random shutdowns, LED error flashes | Limit switch or pressure switch | Note flash code | Test switches, inducer draft |
| Repeated aborts, no clear cause | Control board fault | None | Diagnose and replace board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Flame sensor clean or replacement:** $80–$250
- **Hot-surface igniter:** $150–$350
- **High-limit or rollout switch:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (ECM or PSC):** $400–$900
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$400
- **Integrated control board:** $350–$700
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180 (often credited toward the repair)

Chronic short cycling from oversizing isn't a "repair" — correcting it means resizing or adjusting staging, which a good HVAC contractor should flag before selling you parts.

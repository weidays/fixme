## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single Goodman fault code — it's a behavior. Your furnace fires up, runs for a short time (often under a few minutes), then shuts off before your home reaches the thermostat setpoint, only to restart again shortly after. On many Goodman boards this may or may not be accompanied by a diagnostic LED flash code; the flash pattern depends on your control board revision, so check the label inside the blower door for your specific model.

The core problem is almost always one of three things: the furnace is **overheating** and its safety limit is doing its job, the **flame is being lost** and the board is re-trying ignition, or the furnace is **wildly oversized** for the space. Short cycling wastes fuel, wears out components, and can crack a heat exchanger over time — so it's worth diagnosing rather than ignoring.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricted airflow makes the heat exchanger overheat, tripping the high-limit switch and shutting off the burners.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — A carbon-coated sensor fails to detect flame, so the board shuts off gas and re-tries, producing rapid on/off cycling.
3. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — Same effect as a dirty filter: not enough airflow across the heat exchanger.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — A weak or miscalibrated limit trips prematurely even when airflow is fine.
5. **Blower motor or capacitor problems** — A weak blower can't move enough air to keep temperatures in range.
6. **Flue/exhaust restriction or pressure-switch faults** — On 90%+ models, a partly blocked intake/exhaust or condensate drain can trip the pressure switch mid-cycle.
7. **Cracked or corroded heat exchanger** — Can cause flame disruption and limit trips (this is a safety issue — pro only).
8. **Thermostat issues** — Bad location (near a vent), low batteries, or short-cycle/anticipator settings.
9. **Oversized furnace** — Heats the air near the thermostat too fast, satisfying it before the whole house warms. Common in swapped-out equipment.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do:

- **Air filter** — Pull it out and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat** — Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Make sure it's set to "Heat" and the setpoint is above room temperature. If it's mounted near a supply register or in direct sun, that can cause false satisfaction.
- **Supply and return vents** — Walk the house and confirm registers aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs. Closing too many vents starves airflow.
- **Breaker** — Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on. A tripped or partially seated breaker can cause odd power behavior.
- **Condensate drain (96% models)** — If you have a high-efficiency furnace with a condensate line and see standing water, a clogged drain can trip the pressure switch. You can clear a visibly blocked exterior drain, but leave internal work to a pro.

**Do not** bypass the limit switch, open the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a furnace that keeps locking out. Repeated resets on a unit that won't stay lit can be dangerous.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheapest to most expensive:

1. **Verifies airflow** — Checks the filter, blower wheel cleanliness, and measures temperature rise across the heat exchanger against the rating-plate spec. High rise = airflow problem.
2. **Tests the flame sensor** — Measures microamp signal; a weak reading points to a dirty or failing sensor.
3. **Checks the high-limit switch** — Confirms it opens at the correct temperature, not prematurely.
4. **Inspects the blower** — Motor amp draw, capacitor test, and wheel condition.
5. **Reads the board's flash code** — Uses the LED history to see whether the board logs limit trips, flame loss, or pressure-switch faults.
6. **Tests the pressure switch and venting** (90%+ models) — Manometer readings and a look at the intake/exhaust and condensate trap.
7. **Inspects the heat exchanger** — Visual and sometimes combustion analysis to rule out a crack.
8. **Evaluates sizing/thermostat** if everything mechanical checks out.

If a quote jumps straight to "you need a new control board" or "new furnace" without confirming the filter, flame sensor, and limit first, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few min, shuts off, restarts | Dirty filter / airflow restriction | Replace filter, open all vents | Measure temp rise, clean blower |
| Flame lights then dies, retries | Dirty flame sensor | None (leave in access panel) | Clean or replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Frequent shutoffs, filter is clean | Failing high-limit switch | None | Test and replace limit switch |
| Blower weak or noisy | Blower motor / capacitor | None | Test amps, replace capacitor or motor |
| Cycles on 96% model, water present | Clogged condensate / pressure switch | Clear visible exterior drain clog | Clear trap, test pressure switch |
| Heats fast, quits before house warms | Oversized unit or thermostat location | Check thermostat location/batteries | Evaluate sizing, staging, anticipator |
| Rumble/popping before shutoff | Possible heat exchanger issue | Shut down, call pro | Combustion analysis, inspect exchanger |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 DIY. This is often the whole fix.
- **Flame sensor** — $80–$200 installed (part is cheap; you're paying for the service call).
- **High-limit switch** — $150–$300 installed.
- **Blower capacitor** — $150–$300 installed.
- **Blower motor** — $400–$700+ installed depending on ECM vs. PSC.
- **Pressure switch** — $150–$350 installed.
- **Control board** — $400–$600+ installed.
- **Gas valve** — $400–$600+ installed.
- **Heat exchanger** — $1,000–$2,500+ (often makes replacing an older furnace the smarter choice).
- **Correcting oversizing** — Varies widely; a full furnace replacement runs $3,500–$7,000+.

A typical short-cycling service call that turns out to be a flame sensor or limit switch lands around **$150–$300 all-in**.

## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single Goodman flash code — it is a behavior. Your furnace starts a heating cycle, runs briefly, then shuts off well before the thermostat is satisfied, and repeats. Depending on the cause, your Goodman integrated furnace control (IFC) board may or may not store a fault code (commonly a limit-switch open code or a failure-to-prove-flame code).

The furnace is usually protecting itself. Goodman boards constantly monitor the high-limit switch, the flame sensor, and the pressure switch. If any of those signals looks wrong, the board cuts the burners to prevent overheating, unproven combustion, or venting problems. Short cycling is the symptom; the stored code (if any) and the timing of the shutoff tell you why.

Because a persistent short cycle can end in a hard lockout — and because several causes involve the heat exchanger, gas valve, or control board — this is rated **pro**. You can do the safe checks below, but if simple items don't fix it, bring in a licensed HVAC tech.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricted return airflow lets heat build up, tripping the high-limit switch. By far the most common cause and the easiest to rule out.
2. **Dirty or failing flame sensor** — If the board can't prove flame within a few seconds of ignition, it closes the gas valve. You'll see the burners light, then die almost immediately, over and over.
3. **Blocked or closed supply vents / undersized ductwork** — Same effect as a dirty filter: not enough airflow to carry heat away, so the limit trips.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — A weak or miscalibrated limit switch can open prematurely even when airflow is fine.
5. **Restricted flue or pressure-switch issue** — A partially blocked exhaust, disconnected pressure tubing, or weak inducer trips the pressure switch and stops the cycle.
6. **Oversized furnace** — A unit too large for the home satisfies the thermostat too fast, then restarts often. This is a design problem, not a broken part.
7. **Failing control board or gas valve** — Less common, but a board that misreads sensors or a valve that drops out can cause erratic cycling.
8. **Thermostat problems** — Loose wiring, a bad location near a supply register, or dead batteries can command rapid on/off.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should attempt. Do **not** bypass the limit switch, open the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a locked-out furnace.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to Heat and a few degrees above room temperature. If it's battery-powered, install fresh batteries. Confirm the fan setting is on Auto, not On.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and not blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed doors. Restricted airflow is a leading cause.
- **Breaker:** Check that the furnace breaker is fully on. A tripped or half-tripped breaker can cause odd behavior. Flip it fully off, then on, once — not repeatedly.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models):** On condensing Goodman furnaces, a full or clogged condensate line/trap can trip a safety. Check for standing water or a full drain pan, and clear obvious blockages if you can do so safely.

If the furnace still short cycles after these checks, stop and call a professional.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheapest to most expensive, and you can sanity-check their quote against these steps:

1. **Reads the stored flash code** on the IFC board and confirms the cycle timing.
2. **Measures temperature rise** across the furnace and checks static pressure to confirm whether airflow is the problem (filter, ducts, blower).
3. **Tests the flame sensor** — measures microamp signal and cleans or replaces it if the reading is weak.
4. **Checks the high-limit switch and rollout switches** for continuity and correct trip behavior.
5. **Tests the pressure switch and inducer**, inspects the flue for blockages, and verifies condensate drainage on high-efficiency units.
6. **Verifies gas valve operation and manifold pressure**, and inspects the heat exchanger for cracks or hot spots.
7. **Confirms thermostat wiring and anticipator/cycle settings**, and evaluates whether the furnace is oversized for the load.

A legitimate diagnosis should explain *why* it's cycling, not just replace parts by guesswork.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 5–10 min, then shuts off, restarts | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open all vents | Measure temp rise & static pressure |
| Burners light then die in a few seconds | Dirty/failing flame sensor | None beyond filter check | Clean or replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Cycles with limit code on board | Failing high-limit switch | Confirm airflow is clear | Test and replace limit switch |
| Inducer runs, burners never fully stay on | Pressure switch / blocked flue | Check condensate drain | Inspect flue, test pressure switch & inducer |
| Frequent on/off, room near a register | Thermostat location/wiring | Fresh batteries, Auto fan | Verify wiring, cycle settings, thermostat |
| New/large system cycles fast, no fault | Oversized furnace | None | Load calculation, staging or replacement advice |
| Erratic, no consistent pattern | Control board or gas valve | None | Diagnose and replace board or valve |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor unless noted. Actual prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Air filter (DIY):** $10–$40. Often a $0 fix if you already have spares.
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $80–$180 as a service call. **Replacement:** $120–$300.
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$350 replaced.
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$350 replaced.
- **Inducer motor:** $300–$600.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$450 depending on model.
- **Control board (IFC):** $350–$700.
- **Gas valve:** $300–$600.
- **Duct or airflow correction:** highly variable, $200 to several thousand for major duct work.
- **Oversized-furnace correction:** typically only resolved with re-staging or, at the extreme, replacement — get a Manual J load calculation first.

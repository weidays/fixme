## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a specific Goodman fault code — it's a behavior. Your furnace fires up, runs briefly, shuts off, then restarts far too often. On Goodman integrated control boards, this usually shows up as normal ignition followed by an early shutdown, sometimes with the diagnostic LED flashing an **open limit** or **flame loss** pattern between cycles.

There are two general flavors:

- **Safety-driven cycling** — a sensor (usually the high-limit switch) is doing its job and shutting the burners off because something is wrong, most often airflow.
- **Control- or comfort-driven cycling** — the thermostat, wiring, or an oversized furnace is telling the unit to stop and start too frequently.

Because a Goodman furnace flashes different LED codes depending on the board revision (single-stage vs. two-stage/modulating GMVC/GMEC boards differ), **check your specific model's diagnostic legend on the door label** rather than assuming.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, overheats the heat exchanger, trips the high-limit switch. By far the most common cause.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns starve airflow just like a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty flame sensor** — a fouled sensor loses flame signal seconds after ignition, so the board shuts down and retries. Common on older or dusty installs.
4. **Faulty or aging high-limit switch** — a limit that trips early (or an intermittent one) cuts the burners even with adequate airflow.
5. **Thermostat problems** — dead batteries, poor location (near a supply vent or heat source), or short-cycle/anticipator settings that are too aggressive.
6. **Clogged condensate drain (high-efficiency models)** — a full drain trips the pressure/float safety on GMVC/GMEC condensing furnaces.
7. **Blocked flue or intake / pressure switch issues** — restricted venting causes the pressure switch to open mid-cycle.
8. **Oversized furnace** — heats the space so fast it satisfies the thermostat or trips the limit repeatedly. Common with poorly sized replacements.
9. **Failing control board or gas valve** — least common, but a degrading board can misread sensors and shut down early.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no panels involving gas, wiring, or safety switches.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter and hold it to light. If you can't see through it, replace it and see if cycling stops. Do this first.
- **Thermostat:** Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm it's set to **Heat**, not **Auto/Fan**, and that the temperature is above room temp. Make sure it isn't mounted near a vent, lamp, or sunny window.
- **Vents and registers:** Walk the house. Open all supply registers and make sure furniture, rugs, or boxes aren't blocking return grilles. Keep at least 80% of registers open.
- **Breaker:** If the furnace tripped a breaker, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — don't keep resetting.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency units):** Look for water pooling near the furnace or a full drain pan. A clogged drain can trip a safety. You can pour a cup of water through an accessible drain line or use drain tablets, but don't disassemble anything.

Do **not** bypass the limit switch, pressure switch, or flame sensor, and do not repeatedly reset a furnace that keeps locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will follow airflow and safeties in order — here's what to expect so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the LED flash code** on the control board and note whether it's limit, flame loss, or pressure switch.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the heat exchanger and compare it to the rating plate spec (Goodman lists a target rise range). High rise points to airflow restriction.
3. **Check static pressure** and inspect the filter, blower wheel, and coil for obstruction.
4. **Test flame current (microamps)** at the flame sensor — a low reading means a dirty or failing sensor.
5. **Verify the high-limit and pressure switches** with a meter to see if they're opening prematurely.
6. **Inspect flue and intake** for blockage and check inducer/pressure switch operation.
7. **Assess sizing and thermostat setup** if all hardware checks out — an oversized unit or bad anticipator setting is a diagnosis of exclusion.

Expect a diagnostic fee of **$80–$180**, often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 2-4 min then quits, restarts | Dirty filter / airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, clean blower/coil |
| Fires, then shuts off ~5-10 sec later | Dirty flame sensor | None safe | Clean or replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Cycles with limit LED flashing | Overheating / bad limit | Check filter & vents | Test/replace high-limit switch |
| Rapid on/off, thermostat clicks | Thermostat batteries/setting | Replace batteries, relocate check | Rewire, set heat anticipator/cycle rate |
| Cycles + water near furnace (HE unit) | Clogged condensate drain | Clear accessible drain, tablets | Clear drain, test float/pressure switch |
| Inducer runs, burners never stay lit | Pressure switch / flue block | Check outside vent for blockage | Test pressure switch, clear flue/intake |
| Cycles only on coldest days | Oversized furnace / marginal sensor | None | Load calc, staging adjustment, flame test |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Flame sensor clean or replace:** $80–$250
- **High-limit switch replacement:** $150–$350
- **Pressure switch replacement:** $150–$400
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Blower motor cleaning/replacement:** $150–$650 (cleaning cheap; motor replacement is the high end)
- **Control board replacement:** $400–$700
- **Gas valve replacement:** $300–$600
- **Correcting an oversized furnace:** varies widely; often addressed with staging tweaks first, full replacement only as last resort

Most short-cycling calls end up being a filter, flame sensor, or limit switch — the cheaper end of this list.

## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single flash code — it's a behavior. Your Goodman furnace fires up, runs for a minute or two, then shuts off, and repeats that pattern far more often than normal. Depending on your board revision, you may see a limit-related flash code (often related to the open high-limit switch) or no stored fault at all, since the furnace is technically completing ignition each time.

Short cycling matters because it almost always means the furnace is either overheating and protecting itself, losing flame confirmation, or being told to stop and start too often by the thermostat. Left alone, repeated overheating cycles shorten the life of the heat exchanger and control board.

Because the exact LED flash pattern varies by model and board generation, don't rely on the flash code alone — read the pattern of behavior described below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricted airflow lets the heat exchanger overheat, tripping the high-limit switch and cutting the burners. Most common cause by a wide margin.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — A carbon-coated flame rod fails to confirm flame, so the board drops the burners a few seconds after ignition, then retries.
3. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — Too many closed registers or blocked returns starve airflow and mimic a dirty filter.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — A limit switch that trips early (even with adequate airflow) shuts down burners prematurely.
5. **Blocked condensate drain (96% models)** — On high-efficiency Goodman furnaces, a clogged drain trips the pressure switch and interrupts the cycle.
6. **Thermostat issues** — Dead batteries, poor location near a supply vent, loose wiring, or a "cycles per hour" setting that's too aggressive.
7. **Oversized furnace** — An improperly sized unit satisfies the thermostat too fast and cycles frequently. Common in replacements that weren't load-calculated.
8. **Control board or flame-signal fault** — Less common; diagnosed after the simpler causes are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform. Do not bypass any safety switch or open the gas valve.

- **Air filter** — Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Then watch whether the cycling stops.
- **Thermostat** — Replace the batteries if it uses them. Confirm it's set to Heat and the target temp is above room temp. Check that it isn't mounted near a supply vent, lamp, or sunny window.
- **Supply and return vents** — Walk the house and open closed registers. Make sure furniture or rugs aren't blocking returns. A furnace needs open airflow paths.
- **Breaker** — If the furnace has lost power intermittently, check the furnace breaker and the service switch (looks like a light switch near the unit).
- **Condensate drain (96% models)** — Look for standing water or a full drain pan. Clogs can trip the pressure switch. Cleaning tablets can help, but a heavily blocked line may need a pro.

If clean airflow doesn't stop the cycling, stop here and call a technician — the remaining causes involve the flame sensor, limit switch, or board.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will work from simple to complex, and you can sanity-check the quote against this sequence:

1. **Verify airflow** — Inspect the filter, blower wheel, and coil (on combo systems) for restriction.
2. **Measure temperature rise** — Compare supply and return temps against the rating plate range. A high rise confirms an airflow/overheating problem.
3. **Check the flame signal** — Read microamps on the flame sensor. A weak or dropping signal points to a dirty or failing sensor.
4. **Test the high-limit switch** — Confirm it's opening at the correct temperature and not tripping prematurely.
5. **Inspect pressure switches and condensate path** (96% models) — Look for blockage causing early shutdown.
6. **Evaluate sizing and ductwork** — If everything checks out mechanically, they'll assess whether the furnace is oversized for the space.
7. **Board and wiring check** — Last resort after simpler causes are cleared.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, repeats | Dirty filter overheating unit | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, inspect blower/coil |
| Flame lights then drops after ~5 sec | Dirty flame sensor | None safely | Clean or replace sensor, read microamps |
| Cycles even with clean filter | Failing high-limit switch | None | Test and replace limit switch |
| 96% model cycling with water present | Blocked condensate drain | Clear drain, use tablets | Clear line, verify pressure switch |
| Random on/off, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / bad thermostat | Replace batteries | Replace or relocate thermostat |
| Quick full cycles, house comfortable | Oversized furnace | None | Load calc, staging/airflow adjustments |
| No obvious cause, intermittent | Control board fault | None | Test board, wiring, replace if needed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 DIY. The cheapest and most common fix.
- **Flame sensor cleaning** — $80–$180. Replacement runs $150–$300.
- **High-limit switch** — $150–$400 replaced.
- **Condensate drain clearing** — $75–$250 depending on severity.
- **Thermostat replacement** — $100–$350 installed, more for smart models.
- **Pressure switch** — $150–$400.
- **Control board** — $350–$700 installed.
- **Heat exchanger inspection/replacement** — $500–$2,000+; if cracked, replacement or a new furnace is often the better value.
- **Oversizing correction** — Varies widely; may involve airflow tuning or, worst case, resizing the equipment.

Get a written diagnosis before authorizing any part over $300 — many short-cycling cases are solved by a filter and a flame-sensor cleaning.

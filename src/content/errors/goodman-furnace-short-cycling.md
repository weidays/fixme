## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single Goodman fault code — it's a **behavior**: your furnace starts a heat cycle, runs for a short time (often under a few minutes), shuts off, and then restarts a short while later. Sometimes it never fully warms the house; sometimes it clicks on and off endlessly.

On Goodman furnaces this usually points to one of two underlying problems:

1. **The furnace overheats** and its high-limit switch shuts the burners off to protect the heat exchanger, then restarts once it cools.
2. **The flame is lost or never proven** — the flame sensor doesn't detect a stable flame, so the board shuts the gas off, retries, and repeats.

Your control board's **diagnostic LED** (visible through the sight glass on the blower door) flashes a code that tells the technician which path you're on. Codes vary by board generation, so match the flash pattern to the label printed inside your furnace door. If short cycling continues past the board's retry limit, the furnace may go into a **lockout** — see Related codes.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter.** The single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets heat build up until the limit switch trips.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents.** Too many closed registers or blocked returns starves airflow, causing overheating and limit trips.
3. **Dirty flame sensor.** A carbon-coated sensor can't reliably prove flame, so the board cuts gas seconds after ignition and retries.
4. **Oversized furnace or wrong duct sizing.** Too much heat for the ductwork causes rapid limit cycling — common in poorly designed installs.
5. **Faulty or miswired thermostat.** Dead batteries, loose wires, or a thermostat mounted near a heat source can cause rapid on/off signals.
6. **Failing high-limit switch.** A weak or out-of-spec limit switch trips too early even when airflow is fine.
7. **Blocked flue / pressure switch cycling.** A restricted intake or exhaust can make the pressure switch open mid-cycle, stopping the burners.
8. **Cracked heat exchanger or control board failure.** Less common but serious — these require professional diagnosis and repair or replacement.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the gas valve manifold, bypass any safety switch, or reset a locked-out furnace repeatedly.

- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray or you can't see light through it, swap it. Confirm the correct size printed on the frame.
- **Check the thermostat.** Replace batteries if it takes them. Make sure it's set to Heat and the setpoint is above room temperature. Verify it's not mounted in a drafty or sunny spot.
- **Open your vents.** Make sure supply registers aren't closed and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or curtains.
- **Check the breaker.** If the furnace breaker is tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Look for a blocked condensate line (high-efficiency models).** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and interrupt cycles. Clear standing water if the line is obviously plugged.
- **Confirm outdoor intake/exhaust pipes are clear** of snow, leaves, or nests on high-efficiency (two-pipe) units.

If the furnace still short cycles after a fresh filter and open vents, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech should work in this order — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Read the LED flash code** through the sight glass and note it against the door label.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace and compare to the rating plate range. High rise = airflow restriction.
3. **Inspect airflow:** filter, blower wheel, coil, and ductwork static pressure.
4. **Test the flame sensor** microamp signal; clean or replace if below spec.
5. **Check the high-limit switch and rollout switches** for correct operation and resistance.
6. **Verify the pressure switch and inducer** — confirm the flue isn't restricted and the switch closes/holds.
7. **Inspect the heat exchanger** for cracks (visual and sometimes combustion analysis).
8. **Check thermostat wiring and control board** outputs last, since they're less common causes.

Be cautious of any tech who jumps straight to "replace the control board" or "you need a new furnace" without first checking the filter, airflow, flame sensor, and limit switch.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 2–5 min, blower keeps running after burners stop | Overheating / limit trip from dirty filter | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, check blower & ducts |
| Ignites then shuts off in seconds, retries | Dirty or failing flame sensor | None (call pro) | Clean/replace sensor, verify microamps |
| Rapid on/off with erratic thermostat display | Dead batteries or bad thermostat wiring | Replace batteries, check location | Test thermostat & wiring, replace if faulty |
| Never fully heats, cycles all day, new install | Oversized furnace / undersized ducts | None | Airflow & sizing evaluation, duct fixes |
| Cycles with LED flashing pressure/flue code | Blocked flue or weak pressure switch | Clear outdoor pipes of debris | Test pressure switch & inducer, clear flue |
| Trips breaker repeatedly | Electrical fault or failing board | Reset breaker once only | Electrical diagnosis, board test |
| Cycles plus soot or flame irregularity | Possible cracked heat exchanger | Shut down, call pro | Combustion analysis, exchanger inspection |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and diagnostic fees vary):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 DIY.
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $75–$150 (often part of a service call).
- **Flame sensor replacement:** $100–$250.
- **High-limit switch replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Pressure switch replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Inducer motor:** $300–$600.
- **Control (integrated ignition) board:** $350–$650.
- **Gas valve:** $300–$650.
- **Cracked heat exchanger:** $1,000–$2,500+, or often a case for full furnace replacement depending on age and warranty.

Many Goodman furnaces carry a limited parts warranty (commonly 10 years when registered) — ask whether your failed part is covered before paying full price.

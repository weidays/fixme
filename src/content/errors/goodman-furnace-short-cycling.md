## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single Goodman fault code — it's a behavior. Your furnace starts a heating cycle, then shuts down before the space reaches the thermostat setpoint, and repeats this over and over. Goodman's integrated furnace control board may or may not store a matching LED flash code depending on the underlying cause (an open limit switch, weak flame sense, or pressure-switch fault will each flash differently).

The key point: short cycling is a **symptom** of something else. The furnace is protecting itself — usually because it's overheating, losing flame, or getting a stop-start signal from the thermostat. Your job (and your technician's) is to find which one.

Watch the amber diagnostic LED through the sight glass in the blower compartment while it cycles, and note the flash pattern. Match it against the legend printed on the door label. That code narrows the cause dramatically.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricted airflow lets the heat exchanger overheat, tripping the high-limit switch and killing the burners. By far the most common cause.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — Too many closed registers or a collapsed return duct mimics a dirty filter and overheats the unit.
3. **Dirty or failing flame sensor** — The flame rod gets coated with oxide and can no longer prove flame, so the board shuts the gas valve seconds after ignition, then retries.
4. **Overheating / tripping high-limit switch** — Caused by airflow restriction, a weak or failing blower motor, or a dirty blower wheel.
5. **Pressure switch / venting issue** — A partially blocked flue, condensate-clogged trap, or weak inducer motor causes the pressure switch to open mid-cycle.
6. **Thermostat problems** — Bad location (near a supply vent), loose wiring, dead batteries, or a mis-set "cycle rate" causing rapid on/off signals.
7. **Oversized furnace** — An oversized unit satisfies the thermostat too fast and short cycles by design. Common in older installs; not a repair, but a design flaw.
8. **Failing control board** — Less common; the board mis-reads sensors or drops the call for heat erratically.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if you smell gas — leave and call your gas utility's emergency line or 911.

- **Filter:** Pull it out and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Then run the furnace and see if cycling stops.
- **Thermostat:** Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm it's set to HEAT and the setpoint is above room temperature. Make sure it isn't mounted in direct sun or above a supply register.
- **Breaker:** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully ON (flip it fully off, then on). A tripping breaker is a call-a-pro sign.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and open any closed registers. Confirm nothing (furniture, rugs) is blocking returns.
- **Condensate:** On high-efficiency (condensing) Goodman models, check that the condensate drain and float switch aren't clogged or full — a backed-up trap can interrupt cycles.

Do **not** bypass the limit or pressure switches, open the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a furnace that keeps locking out. Those are technician tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will:

1. **Read the LED flash code** and interview you on the cycle timing.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace (return vs. supply) against the rating-plate spec — high rise points to airflow restriction or overheating.
3. **Inspect and clean the flame sensor** and measure microamp flame current (typically well under 1 µA when dirty vs. the ~2–6 µA range a healthy rod produces).
4. **Test the high-limit and pressure switches** with a meter to see which is opening and when.
5. **Check the inducer and blower motor** amp draw and the blower wheel for dirt buildup.
6. **Verify venting and condensate drainage** on condensing units.
7. **Confirm thermostat wiring and cycle rate**, and evaluate whether the furnace is grossly oversized for the home.

If a tech quotes a board or blower motor without first cleaning the filter path, checking flame sense, and measuring temperature rise, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, restarts; filter is dark | Dirty filter overheating heat exchanger | Replace filter | Verify temperature rise, clean blower |
| Ignites then shuts off in ~3–7 seconds, retries | Dirty/failing flame sensor | None (safe DIY-limited) | Clean or replace flame sensor, check microamps |
| Shuts down mid-cycle, LED shows limit code | High-limit tripping from restricted airflow | Open vents, swap filter | Test limit switch, blower motor, wheel |
| Cycles with inducer/pressure-switch flash code | Blocked flue or clogged condensate trap | Check condensate drain | Clear vent, service inducer, replace switch |
| Rapid on/off, no burner problems | Thermostat location/wiring/cycle rate | Replace batteries, relocate check | Repair wiring, adjust cycle rate |
| Short cycles even when clean, house heats fast | Oversized furnace | None | Evaluate sizing, adjust controls |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 DIY. No labor.
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $80–$180 as part of a service call; sensor replacement $150–$300.
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condensate trap/drain clearing:** $100–$250.
- **Inducer motor:** $300–$600 installed.
- **Blower motor:** $400–$700+ installed (ECM motors cost more).
- **Integrated control board:** $350–$600 installed.
- **Oversizing fix:** No parts repair; a two-stage or correctly sized replacement is a larger project — get a Manual J load calculation before replacing.

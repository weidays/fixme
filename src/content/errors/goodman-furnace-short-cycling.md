## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single numbered fault on Goodman furnaces — it's a **behavior**: your furnace fires up, runs for a very short time (seconds to a few minutes), shuts off, then repeats. On many Goodman/Amana integrated control boards, the diagnostic LED may flash a limit-related or flame-sensing code (such as an open high-limit or loss of flame) while this is happening, but the board may also show nothing if the thermostat is simply being satisfied too fast.

Short cycling matters because it points to one of two problem categories: the furnace is **overheating and protecting itself** (a real safety response you should not defeat), or it is **losing flame or ignition** partway through the cycle. Both wear out expensive parts and can, over time, damage the heat exchanger.

Because the exact LED flash pattern varies by board revision, check the label inside your furnace's blower door for your model's specific code chart rather than assuming.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, the heat exchanger overheats, and the high-limit switch cuts the burner. Most common by far.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers create the same overheating as a dirty filter.
3. **Weak or dirty flame sensor** — the board doesn't confirm flame, drops out, and re-tries, producing rapid firing and shutdowns.
4. **Thermostat problems** — dead batteries, poor location (near a supply register or heat source), or a miswired/short-cycle "cycles per hour" setting set too high.
5. **Overheating from a failing blower motor or capacitor** — the blower can't move enough air, tripping the limit.
6. **Faulty high-limit switch** — a limit that opens prematurely even with adequate airflow.
7. **Flue/venting or condensate problems** — a restricted flue or a full condensate trap (on high-efficiency models) can trip the pressure switch mid-cycle.
8. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats fast, satisfies the thermostat, and cycles frequently. This is a design issue, not a broken part.
9. **Control board fault** — less common; the board misreads sensors or drops the call for heat.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if you smell gas or see scorching — that's an emergency; see the flame rollout page.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many short-cycling cases.
- **Thermostat:** Replace the batteries. Confirm it's set to HEAT and the setpoint is above room temperature. If it has a "cycles per hour" or "heat anticipator" setting, leave it at the default for gas furnaces (typically 3–5 CPH).
- **Vents:** Walk the house and open every supply register and confirm return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Breaker:** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on (flip it fully off, then on if it feels loose).
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models):** Check that the condensate drain line and trap aren't clogged or backed up — standing water can trip the pressure switch.

Do **not** repeatedly reset a furnace that keeps locking out, and never bypass or jump out a limit switch, pressure switch, or rollout switch. Those are safety devices.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just swap parts. Expect them to:

- Confirm the symptom and **read the control board's flash code** on your specific model.
- Measure **temperature rise** across the furnace (return vs. supply) and compare it to the rating plate — high rise confirms an airflow/overheating problem.
- Check **static pressure** to find airflow restrictions (ductwork, coil, filter).
- **Clean and test the flame sensor**, measuring microamp signal.
- Test the **blower motor and run capacitor**.
- Verify the **high-limit and pressure switches** are opening/closing correctly.
- Inspect the **flue, inducer, and condensate trap** for restrictions.
- On suspected oversizing, evaluate the load vs. the furnace's BTU rating.

If a tech quotes a control board or heat exchanger without first checking airflow and temperature rise, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, repeats | Dirty filter / overheating | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise & static pressure |
| Fires, then quits within seconds, retries | Weak/dirty flame sensor | Replace batteries, check thermostat | Clean/replace sensor, test microamps |
| Cycles a lot, house feels warm fast | Oversized unit or thermostat CPH too high | Set CPH to default | Load calc, possible staging/thermostat fix |
| Blower runs but heat cuts out | Failing blower motor/capacitor | — | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Shuts off mid-cycle, LED shows limit code | Faulty high-limit or airflow | Replace filter | Test/replace limit switch |
| High-efficiency model cycles, gurgling | Clogged condensate trap | Check drain for backup | Clear trap, test pressure switch |
| No obvious pattern, random shutdowns | Control board fault | — | Diagnose/replace board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor unless noted:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Flame sensor:** $10–$25 part; $120–$250 installed.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $100–$350 depending on model.
- **Blower run capacitor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Blower motor:** $400–$800 (ECM motors cost more).
- **High-limit switch:** $120–$300 installed.
- **Pressure switch / condensate service:** $120–$350.
- **Integrated control board:** $400–$700 installed.
- **Oversizing remedy:** varies widely — a staging thermostat or ductwork correction runs a few hundred dollars; replacing an oversized furnace is a full system cost.

A simple filter fix is free-to-cheap; a control board or motor is the pricey end.

## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single blink code — it's a behavior. Your Goodman furnace starts a heat call, runs briefly, shuts down, and then restarts (or lock out) far too soon. A healthy furnace typically runs several minutes per cycle; short cycling means runs of seconds to a couple of minutes, repeated over and over.

On most Goodman integrated ignition boards, the pattern that leads to short cycling shows up as flame-sense faults, limit-switch (overheat) faults, or pressure-switch faults, each with its own LED blink code. The exact number of blinks varies by board revision, so **read the diagnostic label inside your furnace's blower door** rather than trusting a generic chart.

Short cycling is a symptom, not a root cause. The job is figuring out *why* the furnace keeps interrupting itself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow overheats the heat exchanger, tripping the high-limit switch, which cuts the burners.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — the board can't confirm flame, so it shuts the gas valve within seconds of ignition, then retries.
3. **Overheating from poor airflow** — closed/blocked supply registers, a dirty blower wheel, undersized ductwork, or a failing blower motor all cook the limit switch.
4. **Faulty high-limit switch** — a limit that trips early (or is out of spec) will cut cycles short even with good airflow.
5. **Pressure switch / flue issues** — a blocked flue, condensate blockage, or weak inducer causes the pressure switch to open mid-cycle.
6. **Thermostat problems** — bad location (near a vent), low batteries, loose wiring, or a "short cycle protection" setting misbehaving.
7. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home satisfies the thermostat fast and cycles frequently by design; common in poorly matched installs.
8. **Failing control board** — least common, but a board with bad relays or flame-sense circuitry can produce erratic cycling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or opening sealed compartments:

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Replace the batteries if it takes them. Confirm it's set to Heat and the setpoint is above room temperature. Make sure it isn't mounted in direct sun or above a supply vent.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and confirm registers aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs. Restricting airflow is a top overheat cause.
- **Return air grille:** Vacuum it if it's dusty; a starved return overheats the furnace just like a dirty filter.
- **Breaker:** If the furnace is dead, check the breaker and the furnace switch (looks like a light switch near the unit). Flip once — do **not** repeatedly reset a furnace that keeps locking out.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models):** Look for a full or clogged condensate drain/trap. A backed-up trap can trip the pressure switch. Clear obvious external clogs only.

If none of that resolves it, stop here and call a pro. Do not remove limit switches, bypass safety switches, or jump out the pressure switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should:

1. **Read the LED blink code** at the control board and note the fault (flame, limit, pressure, etc.).
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the heat exchanger and compare it to the rating-plate range — high rise confirms an airflow/overheat problem.
3. **Check static pressure** to catch duct restrictions, a dirty coil, or a clogged filter you already replaced.
4. **Test the flame sensor** microamp signal and clean or replace it if the reading is weak.
5. **Test the high-limit switch** for correct trip point and continuity.
6. **Verify the inducer and pressure switch** — measure draft/pressure and inspect the flue and condensate path.
7. **Inspect the blower motor and wheel** for a dirty wheel, weak capacitor, or failing motor.
8. **Evaluate sizing** if everything checks out — an oversized unit may cycle no matter what.

Ask which fault code was present and what temperature rise/static readings they measured. A quote that jumps straight to "replace the board" without those readings deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes then shuts off, repeats | Overheat / dirty filter | Replace filter, open all vents | Measure temp rise, static pressure |
| Lights then shuts off in seconds | Dirty flame sensor | None (needs access) | Clean/replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Cuts out mid-cycle, hot to touch | Failing high-limit switch | None | Test and replace limit switch |
| Inducer runs but burners stop | Pressure switch / flue block | Clear obvious condensate clog | Inspect flue, test pressure switch, inducer |
| Cycles fast even when clean | Oversized unit or thermostat | Check thermostat location/batteries | Verify sizing, thermostat wiring |
| Erratic starts/stops, odd blink codes | Control board | None | Diagnose and replace board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor unless noted:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries / repositioning:** $0–$15 (DIY).
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $80–$180 as part of a service call.
- **Flame sensor replacement:** $120–$250.
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$350.
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$350.
- **Inducer motor:** $300–$600.
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900 depending on ECM vs. PSC.
- **Control board:** $350–$600+.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180, often credited toward the repair.

If short cycling traces back to an **oversized furnace**, there's no cheap fix — options range from adjusting fan settings to eventual replacement, which a load calculation should confirm.

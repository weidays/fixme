## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a specific Goodman blink code — it's a behavior. Your furnace fires up, runs for a very short time (often under 3–4 minutes), shuts off, then restarts a short while later, over and over. It never reaches the point of a satisfied thermostat and a normal cool-down.

On Goodman furnaces this usually traces back to one of two families of problems: the furnace is **overheating and tripping a safety limit**, or the **flame is being lost or misread** so the board shuts the burners down. Both are the control board doing its job — protecting the equipment — not the board malfunctioning. Your Goodman may flash a limit-open or flame-sense diagnostic code on the LED behind the blower door while this happens; the exact blink pattern varies by board, so check the legend on the door panel.

Short cycling wastes fuel, wears out the ignitor and blower, and stresses the heat exchanger. It's worth fixing quickly.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets heat build up until the high-limit switch trips and cuts the burners.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers, crushed flex duct, or blocked returns cause the same overheating as a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty or failing flame sensor** — a weak flame signal makes the board shut down the burners seconds after ignition, then retry.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — a limit that trips early (or intermittently) even with good airflow will cut cycles short.
5. **Thermostat problems** — bad placement (near a supply vent or heat source), miswiring, a failing anticipator setting, or dead batteries can chop cycles.
6. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats too fast and satisfies the thermostat quickly; common in older or modified homes.
7. **Blower motor or capacitor issues** — poor airflow from a weak blower mimics a clogged filter and causes overheating.
8. **Control board or wiring fault** — least common, but a failing integrated control board can misread inputs and cycle the furnace.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do not remove or bypass any safety switch, and do not repeatedly reset a furnace that keeps locking out.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Replace the batteries if it uses them. Make sure it's set to HEAT, the fan is on AUTO, and it isn't in a draft, in sunlight, or next to a supply vent.
- **Breaker:** Confirm the furnace breaker is fully on. A partially tripped breaker can cause odd behavior.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and open closed registers. Make sure furniture, rugs, or filters aren't blocking return grilles. Restricting airflow directly causes overheating short cycles.
- **Condensate (96%/high-efficiency models):** Check that the condensate drain and trap aren't clogged. A blocked drain can trip a pressure or float switch on some models. Only clear what's visibly accessible.

If short cycling continues after a fresh filter and open vents, it's a pro job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from safe and cheap toward complex:

- Confirms filter and airflow first, then measures **temperature rise** across the furnace to see if it's overheating (a rise above the range on the rating plate points to airflow restriction).
- Reads the **diagnostic LED code** behind the blower door to see whether the board is logging a limit-open or flame-sense fault.
- Tests the **flame sensor** microamp signal; cleans or replaces it if the reading is weak.
- Checks the **high-limit switch** continuity and whether it's opening prematurely.
- Inspects the **blower motor, capacitor, and wheel** for weak airflow.
- Verifies **thermostat wiring and heat anticipator/cycle settings**.
- Inspects **ductwork** for undersized returns or crushed runs if the home chronically overheats the furnace.

Ask to see the temperature-rise reading and the flame-sensor microamp number — those two data points explain most short-cycling diagnoses.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 1–3 min then quits, restarts | Dirty filter / blocked airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, check limit |
| Burners light, die in seconds, retry | Dirty/failing flame sensor | None safe beyond filter check | Clean or replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Cycles even with clean filter | Failing high-limit switch | None | Test and replace limit switch |
| Rapid on/off, thermostat feels off | Thermostat placement/wiring/battery | Replace batteries, relocate if in draft | Verify wiring, cycle settings, replace stat |
| Heats fast, quits, whole house | Oversized furnace | None | Load calc, confirm sizing, adjust staging |
| Weak airflow, warm cabinet | Blower motor/capacitor fault | None | Test capacitor, motor, blower wheel |
| Odd behavior, code doesn't match | Control board fault | None | Diagnose board and wiring, replace if needed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY). The cheapest fix by far.
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $80–$180 as a service call; the part alone is ~$15–$40.
- **Flame sensor replacement:** $120–$250.
- **High-limit switch replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 depending on model.
- **Blower capacitor:** $150–$300.
- **Blower motor:** $400–$700+.
- **Integrated control board:** $400–$650.
- **Duct modification (undersized return):** $500–$2,000+, situational.

Many short-cycling calls end at a filter change or a flame-sensor cleaning, so don't assume the worst before a tech reads the temperature rise and flame signal.

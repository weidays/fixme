## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single Goodman fault code — it's a behavior. Your furnace fires up, runs briefly, shuts off, then restarts a few minutes later, repeating without ever satisfying the thermostat or completing a full heat cycle.

On most Goodman gas furnaces (GMVC, GMEC, GCES, GMS9x, GMSS, and GC9x series), the integrated control board flashes a diagnostic LED code through the sight glass on the burner door. Short cycling often shows up alongside a limit-switch code or a "flame not sensed" code, depending on where in the sequence the furnace quits. **Check your board's LED pattern against the label on the inside of the blower door** — the exact code meaning varies by board revision.

The root issue is almost always one of two things: the furnace is **overheating and tripping a safety limit**, or it's **failing to prove flame** and shutting the gas off as a precaution. Both are the furnace protecting itself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, heat exchanger overheats, high-limit switch trips and cuts the burners. By far the most common cause.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — the sensor rod gets coated and can't detect flame, so the board closes the gas valve within seconds of ignition. Very common on units 3+ years old.
3. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns starve airflow and cause the same overheating as a dirty filter.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — a weak or faulty limit trips prematurely even with adequate airflow.
5. **Blocked condensate drain (high-efficiency models)** — a full condensate trap trips the pressure switch, interrupting the cycle.
6. **Flue/venting or pressure-switch problems** — partial blockage or a marginal inducer trips the pressure switch mid-cycle.
7. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats the thermostat area fast, satisfies the call, and cycles frequently by design. Common in improperly sized installs.
8. **Failing control board or gas valve** — less common, but a marginal board or valve can drop the flame intermittently.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no panels bypassed, no gas work:

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to Heat and the target temp is above room temp. If it's battery-powered, install fresh batteries — a dying thermostat can cause erratic calls.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and open all registers. Make sure furniture, rugs, or boxes aren't blocking return grilles.
- **Breaker:** If the furnace lost power and the board reset, confirm the breaker is fully on (flip it off, then firmly on).
- **Condensate drain (high-efficiency units):** Look for standing water or an overflowing drain pan near the furnace. If the trap or line is clogged, clearing it may restore normal operation.

If these don't resolve it — **stop here.** Do not remove burner panels, bypass limit or pressure switches, or repeatedly reset the furnace. Call a licensed tech.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician should:

1. **Read the LED fault code** through the sight glass and note the flash pattern before touching anything.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace and compare it to the rating-plate spec (typically stamped as a range like 40–70°F). A high rise confirms an airflow/overheating problem.
3. **Check static pressure** and inspect the blower wheel, filter, and coil for restriction.
4. **Test the flame sensor microamps** — a low reading (below roughly 1–2 µA) confirms a dirty or failing sensor.
5. **Verify the high-limit and pressure switches** with a meter across their terminals during a cycle.
6. **Inspect the inducer motor and flue** for airflow, and check the condensate trap on high-efficiency models.
7. **Confirm proper furnace sizing** if everything tests good and the cycling is quick with no fault code.

If a quote jumps straight to "replace the control board" without measuring temperature rise or testing the flame sensor, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs several minutes, then shuts off; restarts later | Overheating / limit trip from dirty filter | Replace filter, open all vents | Measure temp rise, test high-limit switch |
| Fires up, shuts off in 5–10 seconds | Dirty or failing flame sensor | None — call a pro | Clean or replace sensor, test microamps |
| Cycles rapidly, no fault code, house heats fast | Oversized furnace or bad thermostat | Replace thermostat batteries | Evaluate sizing, check thermostat cycle rate |
| Stops mid-cycle, water near furnace | Blocked condensate drain / pressure switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear trap, test pressure switch |
| Won't complete cycle, inducer runs oddly | Flue blockage or weak inducer | Check outdoor vent for blockage | Inspect flue, test inducer & pressure switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter replacement:** $0–$40 (DIY)
- **Flame sensor clean or replace:** $100–$300
- **High-limit switch replacement:** $150–$350
- **Pressure switch replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Inducer motor replacement:** $300–$600
- **Control board replacement:** $350–$650
- **Gas valve replacement:** $400–$700

Most short-cycling calls land at the low end — a filter or flame sensor. Board and gas valve replacements are the exception, not the rule.

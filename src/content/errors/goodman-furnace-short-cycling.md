## What this code means

"Short cycling" isn't a single blink code — it's a behavior. Your Goodman furnace fires up, runs for a short time (often under a few minutes), shuts off, then starts again far more frequently than normal. Depending on your board, you may see the diagnostic LED flash a limit-switch or flame-related code between cycles, but many short-cycle situations throw no stored fault at all until the unit finally locks out.

Short cycling almost always means the furnace is either **overheating and tripping its high-limit switch**, **losing its flame signal**, or being told to stop early by a **thermostat or wiring problem**. On two-stage models (GMVC/GMEC), it can also reflect staging logic reacting to airflow or sensor issues. Because the root cause can range from a $15 filter to a failing control board or heat exchanger concern, we rate this **pro** — safe to check the basics, but persistent cycling needs a technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow overheats the heat exchanger and trips the high-limit switch.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns starve airflow and cause the same overheating.
3. **Dirty flame sensor** — a carbonized flame rod fails to prove flame, so the board shuts the gas valve seconds after ignition, then retries.
4. **Faulty or misplaced high-limit switch** — a limit that trips too early (or a genuinely overheating exchanger) cuts the burners repeatedly.
5. **Thermostat problems** — dead batteries, loose wiring, a location near a supply vent, or short-cycle/anticipator settings causing rapid on-off.
6. **Restricted flue or pressure switch issues** — a partially blocked intake/exhaust or a marginal pressure switch can interrupt the sequence.
7. **Oversized furnace** — an oversized unit heats the space too fast and satisfies the thermostat quickly; more common in older or mismatched installs.
8. **Failing control board** — misreading sensors or mis-timing the sequence; usually diagnosed after other causes are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This resolves a large share of short-cycling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm it's set to **Heat** and the setpoint is above room temperature. Make sure it isn't in direct sun or near a supply vent.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and open closed registers. Confirm return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or filters stacked on top.
- **Breaker:** If the furnace also acts erratically or won't stay on, check that the furnace breaker is fully on (flip fully off, then on).
- **Condensate (90%+ models):** On high-efficiency Goodman furnaces, check that the condensate drain and trap aren't clogged — a backed-up trap can trip a pressure/float switch and interrupt cycles.

Do **not** bypass the limit switch, adjust the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out. Those are technician tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should:

1. **Read the diagnostic LED** and pull any stored fault history from the board.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the heat exchanger and compare it to the rating-plate spec — high rise confirms an airflow/overheating problem.
3. **Check static pressure** to identify duct, filter, or coil restrictions.
4. **Inspect and clean the flame sensor**, then measure the **microamp flame-signal** — low signal points to sensor or grounding issues.
5. **Test the high-limit and pressure switches** for correct operation and continuity.
6. **Verify the blower motor, capacitor, and CFM** are within spec.
7. **Inspect the heat exchanger** for cracks and the flue/intake for blockage.
8. Confirm the thermostat wiring and, on two-stage units, staging behavior.

If a tech wants to replace the control board first without checking airflow, flame signal, and limits, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, restarts | Overheating / dirty filter | Replace filter, open vents | Check temp rise, static pressure, limit switch |
| Burners light then die in seconds | Dirty flame sensor | Replace filter (rule out airflow) | Clean/replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Cycles with LED limit code | High-limit tripping | Verify airflow is clear | Test limit, inspect blower & heat exchanger |
| Rapid on-off, no ignition issue | Thermostat / wiring | Replace batteries, check setpoint | Verify t-stat wiring, anticipator/cycle rate |
| 90% unit cycles, water near furnace | Clogged condensate trap | Clear visible drain blockage | Service trap, test pressure/float switch |
| Cycles worse on cold, windy days | Restricted or affected flue | None | Inspect intake/exhaust, test pressure switch |
| New furnace heats too fast | Oversized unit | None | Evaluate sizing/staging, adjust fan settings |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 — DIY.
- **Flame sensor clean or replace:** $80–$250.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $130–$400 depending on model.
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$400.
- **Pressure switch:** $120–$350.
- **Condensate trap/drain service:** $100–$250.
- **Blower motor / capacitor:** $150–$700 depending on motor type (ECM costs more).
- **Control board:** $350–$600.
- **Heat exchanger inspection/replacement:** inspection often included in a service call; replacement is major (frequently justifies furnace replacement on older units).

A basic diagnostic service call typically runs **$90–$180**, often credited toward the repair.

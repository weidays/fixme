## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single Goodman fault code — it's a behavior: your furnace starts a heat cycle, then shuts down after a short time and repeats, without satisfying the thermostat. Depending on your board, you may see a diagnostic LED flash pattern (for example a limit-open or flame-loss code) behind the blower door, or you may see no code at all while the unit keeps starting and stopping.

Short cycling almost always means the furnace is protecting itself. A safety switch (usually the high-limit) is opening on overheat, the flame sensor can't confirm a flame, or the thermostat is telling it to stop early. It's rarely "just annoying" — the underlying cause frequently involves airflow or overheating that shortens equipment life.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the most common cause. Restricted airflow overheats the heat exchanger and trips the high-limit switch, cutting the burners.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — the flame lights, but the sensor can't detect it, so the board closes the gas valve within seconds. Cleaning or replacing it is a common fix.
3. **Blocked return/supply vents or closed dampers** — same effect as a dirty filter: not enough airflow across the heat exchanger.
4. **Thermostat problems** — dead batteries, poor wiring, or thermostat mounted in a hot spot (near a supply register or sunny wall) causing premature satisfaction.
5. **Tripping high-limit switch** — from restricted airflow, a failing blower motor/capacitor, or a genuinely faulty limit.
6. **Dirty or failing igniter/flame system** — weak ignition causing lockout-and-retry patterns (see the Lockout page for repeated failed starts).
7. **Cracked or leaking flue/pressure switch issues** — a marginal pressure switch can open mid-cycle and shut the furnace down.
8. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home heats the thermostat area fast and shuts off before the whole house is comfortable. Common in replacements, hard to fix cheaply.
9. **Control board fault** — less common; the board misreads inputs or cuts the sequence early.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If it's gray/clogged, replace it — this fixes short cycling more often than any other single item.
- **Check the thermostat.** Replace batteries if it takes them, confirm it's set to Heat and the target temp is above room temp. Make sure it isn't in direct sun or above a register.
- **Check supply and return vents.** Open closed registers and remove furniture, rugs, or boxes blocking returns. Restricted airflow is a top cause of overheating.
- **Check the breaker.** A furnace on a tripping breaker can cycle oddly — if the breaker won't hold, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the condensate drain (high-efficiency models).** A clogged drain or full trap can trip a float switch. Clear visible standing water only; don't disassemble internal plumbing.

Do **not** bypass the high-limit or any safety switch, open the gas valve, or repeatedly reset a furnace that keeps locking out. Repeated resets on a unit that won't stay lit can be dangerous.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will:

1. Read the **diagnostic LED flash code** at the control board and note the pattern.
2. Measure **temperature rise** across the furnace and compare it to the rating-plate range — high rise confirms an airflow/overheat problem.
3. Inspect and clean the **flame sensor**, then check microamp signal.
4. Test the **high-limit switch**, **pressure switch**, and **rollout switches** for correct operation.
5. Check the **blower motor and capacitor** for correct amp draw and airflow.
6. Verify **gas pressure** and inspect the burners and igniter.
7. Confirm the furnace is **correctly sized** for the home if oversizing is suspected.

If a tech quotes a control board or gas valve without first checking the filter, airflow, and flame sensor, ask them to justify it — those cheaper causes should be ruled out first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, restarts | Overheating / dirty filter | Replace filter, open vents | Check temp rise, test high-limit |
| Fires, then dies in 3–7 seconds | Dirty flame sensor | Replace filter first | Clean/replace flame sensor, check microamps |
| Cycles fast, thermostat near register | Thermostat placement/batteries | Replace batteries, confirm settings | Relocate or replace thermostat |
| Blower runs, burners cut early | High-limit tripping | Check airflow/vents | Test limit, blower motor, capacitor |
| Cuts off mid-cycle, clicking | Pressure switch opening | Clear condensate drain | Test pressure switch, check flue/inducer |
| New furnace, always short cycles | Oversized unit | None | Verify sizing, airflow adjustments |
| Random shutdowns, no pattern | Control board fault | None | Diagnose board inputs/outputs |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Flame sensor clean:** $80–$180 · **replacement:** $150–$300
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$400
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $120–$300
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900
- **Control board:** $350–$650
- **Gas valve:** $300–$600+
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180 (often credited toward the repair)

Oversizing is the exception — there's no cheap fix; a tech may optimize airflow, but the real remedy is correct replacement sizing at end of life.

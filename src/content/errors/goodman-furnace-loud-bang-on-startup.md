## What this code means

"Loud bang on startup" is not a blink-code Goodman furnaces flash on the control board — it is a symptom you hear when the furnace begins a heating cycle. There are two very different sources, and telling them apart matters:

- **A sharp bang or "whoomp" right when the burners light** is almost always **delayed ignition**. Gas flows into the burners but does not ignite immediately, so a small pocket of unburned gas builds up and then lights all at once — a mini-explosion inside the burner box. This is a combustion problem and should be treated as urgent.
- **A softer boom or metallic pop a second or two after the blower starts or stops** is usually **duct "oil-canning"** — the large flat sheet-metal duct panels flexing under pressure change. This is a noise nuisance, not a safety issue.

Because delayed ignition can crack a heat exchanger, trip the rollout switch, or push the furnace into a lockout over time, this page is rated **pro**: diagnose the source, but leave burner and gas work to a licensed technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or corroded burners / clogged burner ports** — carbon, rust, scale, or spider webs narrow the flame path so flame carryover from burner to burner is slow, delaying full ignition. In field practice this is the classic delayed-ignition finding.
2. **Weak, dirty, or misaligned hot-surface igniter** — the igniter glows but is slow to reach light-off temperature, or it sits too far from the burner it is supposed to light, so gas accumulates before the burners catch.
3. **Incorrect manifold gas pressure** (too high or too low) — set by a technician's manometer; either extreme causes rough, delayed light-off. Common after an altitude or fuel conversion done incorrectly.
4. **Weak flame sense or a poor burner/control ground** — this does not make the furnace release extra gas. The trial-for-ignition period is fixed by the control, and if flame is not proved the control closes the gas valve. What weak flame sense or bad grounding *can* do is cause the control to drop flame mid-cycle and then try again, producing pops or a bang on the re-light.
5. **Restricted airflow** (severely clogged filter or blocked returns) — disrupts clean, stable combustion. Worth ruling out first because it is DIY-checkable.
6. **Ductwork oil-canning** — not a combustion issue at all; the bang tracks blower start/stop rather than burner light-off. Fixed with duct reinforcement or a slower blower ramp.

There is no auto-resetting fault code tied to this noise. If delayed ignition eventually trips a safety, the furnace will lock out and the LED on the control board will flash a fault code. **Do not assume a flash count means what you read online:** Goodman and Amana have used several different integrated ignition controls over the years, and the same number of flashes can mean completely different things from one board to the next. Read the flash-code legend printed on the label inside the blower door of *your* furnace, match the flashes you actually see to that legend, and give the technician both the flash count and the furnace model number.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do — none involve opening the burner compartment:

- **Replace a dirty air filter.** A clogged filter restricts airflow and can worsen an ignition problem. Swap in a clean one of the correct size.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it is set to Heat and replace the batteries if it uses them. If you want to confirm the noise, **keep all panels and the blower door closed, stand well clear of the furnace cabinet, and listen from outside it** — never with the burner compartment open. If it bangs again, **shut the furnace off immediately at the service switch (or the breaker) and leave it off** until a technician has seen it.
- **Confirm all supply and return vents/registers are open and unblocked** by furniture or rugs.
- **Verify the furnace switch and breaker are on.** If the furnace has locked out, you may cycle power **once** (breaker off/on or the service switch) to reset it — but do **not** repeatedly reset a unit that keeps banging or locking out.
- **Check the condensate drain** on condensing models (GMEC/GMVC/GMVM) isn't backed up or overflowing, which can trip safeties.

If the bang returns after a clean filter and one reset, stop and call a technician. Do not keep running a furnace that bangs at ignition.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A quote should reflect these steps — if it doesn't, ask why:

1. **Watch a cold start** and listen for the timing of the bang relative to igniter glow, gas valve opening, and blower start — this instantly separates delayed ignition from duct oil-canning.
2. **Pull and inspect the burners** for carbon, rust, spider webs, and clogged ports; clean or replace as needed and verify flame carryover across all burners.
3. **Inspect and test the hot-surface igniter** for resistance, glow time, and position relative to the burner.
4. **Measure manifold gas pressure** with a manometer and set it to the furnace nameplate spec for the fuel and altitude.
5. **Check the flame sense signal, burner ground, and line-voltage wiring** so flame is proved cleanly and held for the whole cycle.
6. **Read the control board's stored/flashing fault code against the legend on the blower-door label** for that specific board before acting on it.
7. **Inspect the heat exchanger** for cracks caused by repeated bangs — a critical safety check.
8. **For duct noise,** confirm the bang tracks blower operation and recommend duct stiffening or a blower-ramp adjustment.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sharp bang exactly when burners light | Delayed ignition from dirty burners or a weak igniter | Replace filter, cycle power once, then stop | Clean burners, test/replace igniter, check ground |
| Bang plus soot or singed smell | Rich flame / manifold pressure too high | Shut down at the service switch, call for service | Measure and set manifold gas pressure |
| Bang, then furnace locks out and the board flashes a code | A safety has tripped — read the flash code against the blower-door legend | One reset only; do not re-run if it recurs | Identify the tripped safety, correct root cause, inspect heat exchanger & rollout |
| Bang worsens with dirty filter | Restricted return airflow | Replace filter, open all vents | Check duct static pressure, blower speed |
| Soft boom when blower starts/stops | Duct oil-canning (not combustion) | None needed for safety | Add duct stiffeners, cross-break panels, adjust ramp |
| Bang with slow, lazy flame | Clogged burner ports | Replace filter | Remove and clean burners, verify carryover |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor:

- **Air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Burner cleaning / removal & reinstall:** $120–$300
- **Hot-surface igniter replacement:** $150–$350
- **Manifold gas pressure adjustment / flame sensor service:** $90–$250
- **Duct reinforcement to stop oil-canning:** $150–$500 depending on access
- **Heat exchanger inspection:** often included in a service call. **Replacement** (if cracked by repeated bangs) runs **$1,000–$3,000+** — even when the exchanger itself is covered under warranty, the labor to pull and rebuild the furnace typically starts around $1,000, which is why full furnace replacement is usually worth pricing on older units.

Most delayed-ignition calls land in the **$150–$400** range once a technician cleans the burners and replaces the igniter.

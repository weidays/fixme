## What this code means

"Keeps shutting off" is a symptom, not a single Carrier fault code. Your furnace is either short cycling (starting and stopping within a few minutes) or shutting down and locking out after one or more failed attempts. Carrier furnaces are designed to stop the burners the moment a safety device — like the high-limit switch, flame sensor, or pressure switch — sees something outside safe limits.

On most Carrier boards, the furnace will flash a numbered status code on the control board LED (visible through the sight glass on the lower door) that tells you *why* it shut off. If the furnace stopped after several failed ignition attempts, it may enter a **hard lockout** that holds until power is cycled or auto-resets after a set time — typically 3 hours on most boards. Because the exact code and reset behavior vary by board generation, read the LED and match it to the codes below.

If you ever smell gas, do **not** troubleshoot. Leave the house, avoid switches, and call your gas utility's emergency line or 911 from outside.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted return airflow overheats the heat exchanger and trips the high-limit switch, shutting the burners off.
2. **Dirty flame sensor** — a carbon-coated sensor can't confirm flame, so the board shuts the gas valve within seconds. This causes repeated ignition-then-shutoff cycles (often Code 34 territory).
3. **Restricted airflow from closed vents, blocked returns, or a dirty blower** — same overheating effect as a dirty filter.
4. **Tripping high-limit switch** — from overheating, or a failing limit switch itself.
5. **Pressure switch / venting issues** — a blocked flue, condensate blockage, or weak inducer opens the pressure switch mid-cycle, which typically shows as Code 31 or 32 (and 43 on some low-heat boards). A different fault, Code 23, means the pressure switch *did not open* — a switch stuck or welded closed, or a miswired condition — so it points the opposite direction. Code numbering varies by board generation, so confirm against the label inside your furnace door.
6. **Flame-proving / ignition faults** — a failing igniter, gas valve, or flame signal issue (see Codes 14, 22, 34).
7. **Control board or wiring faults** — intermittent connections or a failing board that drops the call for heat.
8. **Oversized furnace or thermostat placement** — genuine short cycling from too-fast heat rise or a thermostat in a hot draft.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do:

- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, dusty, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of shut-off complaints.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Heat and above room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Look at the breaker and furnace switch.** Confirm the breaker is on and the switch on or near the furnace is on. If the unit is locked out, cut power **one time only** — either at the breaker or at the furnace switch, not both — wait about a minute, then restore it. That is one reset, and it's the only one you should attempt.
- **Open supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't choking airflow. Keep most vents open.
- **Check the condensate line and drain** (high-efficiency furnaces). A clogged drain or full trap can trip a safety switch. Clear a visible external blockage only.
- **Read the LED code** through the door sight glass and note the flash pattern for the technician.

If the furnace locks out again after that single reset, stop. Don't keep resetting it.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A tech should work the actual fault code, not just swap parts. Expect them to:

- Read the stored/flashing fault code and check the board's fault history.
- Measure temperature rise across the heat exchanger and compare it to the rating-plate range to confirm airflow adequacy.
- Test the high-limit switch and rollout switches for correct operation and continuity.
- Inspect and clean or replace the flame sensor, then measure the microamp flame signal.
- Check the pressure switch, inducer draft, and flue/vent for blockages or condensate backup.
- Verify gas pressure, igniter operation, and gas valve function.
- Inspect blower operation, wheel cleanliness, and capacitor.
- Check control board voltages and wiring connections for intermittent faults.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, restarts | High-limit trip from dirty filter/airflow | Replace filter; open vents | Test limit switch, measure temp rise, clean blower |
| Ignites then quits in seconds, repeats | Dirty or failing flame sensor | None (one reset) | Clean/replace flame sensor, check microamps |
| Shuts down after several failed lights | Ignition/flame-proving lockout | One reset only | Test igniter, gas valve, gas pressure |
| Inducer runs but burners won't stay on | Pressure switch / blocked vent | Clear condensate line externally | Test pressure switch, inspect flue & inducer |
| Blower won't keep up, overheats | Dirty blower or failing motor/cap | Replace filter | Clean wheel, test motor and capacitor |
| Random shutoffs, erratic codes | Control board or loose wiring | None | Inspect wiring, test board, replace if faulty |
| Cycles very fast, all seems clean | Oversized unit or thermostat location | Move heat sources from thermostat | Evaluate sizing, thermostat placement |

## Repair costs

- **Air filter:** $8–$25 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$10 (DIY)
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90–$160
- **Flame sensor clean or replace:** $80–$250
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$350
- **High-limit or rollout switch:** $150–$350
- **Inducer motor:** $400–$1,200 installed
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900 for a standard PSC motor; $600–$1,800 installed for an ECM or variable-speed motor
- **Gas valve:** $300–$650
- **Control board:** $400–$1,200 installed — Infinity and variable-speed boards land at the top of that range

Prices vary by region, model, and whether parts are under warranty. Many Carrier furnaces carry a 10-year parts warranty if registered, which can cut part costs significantly.

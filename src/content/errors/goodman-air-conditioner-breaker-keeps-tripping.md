## What this code means

"Breaker keeps tripping" is not a stored fault code on most Goodman air conditioners. It is a symptom. The circuit breaker feeding the outdoor condenser, or sometimes the indoor air handler or furnace, is sensing too much current and shutting the power off. The causes are:

- **Overcurrent:** a hard-starting or locked compressor, or a seized fan motor.
- **A short circuit:** damaged wiring, or a component shorted to ground.

Single-stage Goodman condensers (GSX, GSXN, GSXH) have no control board that logs this. The unit simply goes dead until you reset the breaker.

Two-stage and variable-speed models (GSXC, GSXV) have electronic controls that may show an LED or communicating-thermostat fault before the breaker trips. If yours has one, note the code for the technician.

A tripped breaker does not reset on its own. It holds until someone resets it manually.

This page covers air conditioners specifically. For heat pumps, which add a reversing valve and defrost controls, see the separate heat pump page.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failing run capacitor.** This is the most common cause on Goodman condensers. A weak or bulging dual capacitor makes the compressor and fan motor draw high current at startup. Trips usually happen a few seconds after the unit tries to start.
2. **Worn or pitted contactor.** Burned contacts can arc, chatter, or weld shut. This causes high current or erratic power to the compressor.
3. **Damaged or loose wiring.** Chafed, rodent-chewed, or corroded wires in the condenser or the line-voltage whip can short to the cabinet. Loose lugs at the disconnect or breaker are also common. Trips may be instant, even with the thermostat off.
4. **Outdoor fan motor failing or seized.** A shorted or locked fan motor draws excess current. If the fan isn't spinning, the compressor also overheats and pulls more amps.
5. **Compressor electrical fault.** A grounded or shorted winding typically trips the breaker instantly. A mechanically locked compressor (locked rotor) trips it within seconds. This is the most expensive cause.
6. **Very dirty condenser coil or restricted airflow.** High head pressure raises compressor amp draw. This usually causes trips after a long run on hot days rather than at startup.
7. **Undersized, worn, or incorrect breaker.** The breaker should match the "Max Fuse/Breaker" rating on the unit's nameplate. Old breakers can also trip below their rating.
8. **Refrigerant system problems.** Overcharge or a restriction raises pressures and amperage. Only a technician can diagnose and correct this.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Note when it trips.** Does it trip instantly when the breaker is reset, a few seconds after the AC tries to start, or after running a while? This timing is very useful for the technician.
- **Reset the breaker once.** Turn the thermostat to OFF first. Then flip the breaker fully off and back on. Wait about 5 minutes, then set the thermostat to cool.
  - If the breaker trips again, leave it off and call a pro.
  - Do not reset it repeatedly.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it is set to Cool with a setpoint below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is weak or blank.
- **Replace a dirty air filter.** Severe restriction can contribute to high-load trips.
- **Make sure supply and return vents are open** and not blocked by furniture.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from the outside.** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around it, and keep 2 feet of clearance. Do not open the electrical panel or the unit.
- **Check that the exterior service panel is seated and secured.**
- **Check the condensate drain.** Make sure the line is draining and any float or safety switch hasn't shut the system off. That issue usually shuts the system down rather than tripping a breaker, but it is worth ruling out.

Stop and call a technician right away if you see any of these:
- Burn marks, a melted smell, or buzzing at the unit or panel.
- A breaker that is hot to the touch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will usually:

1. **Verify power and the breaker.** They check that breaker size matches the nameplate's maximum overcurrent rating. They look for loose or burned lugs at the panel and disconnect, and measure supply voltage.
2. **Inspect the condenser electrical compartment.** They look for chafed wires, rodent damage, burned terminals, and a bulging or leaking capacitor.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a capacitance meter against its rated µF. A reading outside about ±6% means replacement.
4. **Inspect and test the contactor.** They look for pitting and check the voltage drop across the contacts.
5. **Test the compressor windings.**
   - They measure resistance across common, run, and start.
   - They megohm or ohm each terminal to ground.
   - A grounded compressor trips the breaker instantly and generally means replacement.
6. **Test the fan motor** for shorts to ground and free rotation.
7. **Measure amp draw on startup and while running.** They compare it to the nameplate RLA and LRA. Sustained locked-rotor amps point to a stuck compressor. A hard-start kit may be tried if the compressor is electrically sound.
8. **Check the condenser coil and refrigerant pressures** if high running amps suggest a load problem.
9. **Read any fault codes** on two-stage or variable-speed controls.

If a quote says "replace compressor," ask to see the ground or winding readings. Replacing a capacitor or contactor should come first unless those tests show a clear compressor fault.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Breaker trips instantly when reset, thermostat off | Shorted wiring or a component grounded | Leave breaker off | Locate the short; repair wiring or replace the grounded part |
| Trips a few seconds after AC starts, humming first | Weak capacitor or locked compressor | Reset once only | Test and replace the capacitor; check LRA; possibly add a hard-start kit |
| Clicking or chattering at outdoor unit, then trip | Failing contactor | None beyond one reset | Replace the contactor |
| Fan not spinning, unit hums, then trips | Failed fan motor or capacitor | Clear debris around the unit (from outside) | Test and replace the fan motor or capacitor |
| Trips after long runs on hot afternoons | Dirty condenser coil, high head pressure | Clear debris; replace the filter | Clean the coil chemically; check charge and amps |
| Trips randomly; breaker warm, panel smells hot | Loose lug or worn breaker | Stop resetting; call a pro | Electrician or tech tightens connections or replaces the breaker |
| Indoor breaker trips instead of outdoor | Air handler or furnace blower motor, or heat strips | Check the filter | Test the blower motor, capacitor, and heat kit |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic or service call | $80 – $200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120 – $350 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Hard-start kit | $150 – $400 |
| Wiring repair (whip, burned terminals, rodent damage) | $150 – $500 |
| Breaker replacement or panel connection repair | $150 – $400 |
| Outdoor fan motor replacement | $300 – $750 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100 – $250 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500 – $3,500+ (often less if the parts warranty applies; labor and refrigerant still cost money) |
| Full condenser replacement | $3,500 – $7,500+ |

If a Goodman condenser is more than 12–15 years old and the compressor is grounded, replacing the whole condenser is often the better value.

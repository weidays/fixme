## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is a condition, not a numbered fault code. It means ice has formed on the indoor coil, which sits above your furnace or inside your air handler. Ice can also form on the copper suction line that runs to the outdoor unit.

The evaporator coil normally runs a little above 32°F. If too little warm air moves across it, or the refrigerant pressure drops too low, the coil falls below freezing. Moisture from the air then turns to ice.

Common signs include:

- Weak or warm airflow from the vents
- Visible frost on the refrigerant lines
- Water around the indoor unit while the ice melts
- An outdoor unit that runs constantly without cooling the house

Most American Standard systems do not display a code for this. On communicating Link or AccuLink systems, the thermostat or outdoor control may log related airflow or pressure alerts. What gets reported varies by model, so check your installation guide for specifics.

Because the problem has a physical cause, it does not "reset." Once the coil thaws, it will refreeze unless that cause is corrected.

If the main complaint is poor cooling without ice, see the separate "American Standard AC Not Cooling" page.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter.** This is the most common cause. A loaded filter starves the coil of airflow.
2. **Closed or blocked supply vents and return grilles.** Furniture, rugs, or many closed registers restrict total airflow.
3. **Running the AC in low outdoor temperatures.** Running the system below about 60°F outdoors, or with a very low thermostat setpoint, can drop coil temperature below freezing.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil.** Dust and biological growth on the coil fins block airflow even when the filter is clean.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Low charge drops coil pressure and temperature. Evaporator coil leaks are a known issue across the industry, including some American Standard and Trane coils.
6. **Blower motor or control problems.** Possible faults include:
   - A failing blower motor
   - A failed capacitor on PSC motors
   - An incorrect blower speed or airflow setting on ECM or variable-speed units
   - A slipping blower wheel
7. **Ductwork restrictions.** Collapsed flex duct, undersized returns, or disconnected duct sections reduce airflow.
8. **Metering device restriction.** A sticking TXV or a clogged filter-drier causes a starved coil. This is less common.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Stop cooling and thaw the coil.** Set the thermostat to Off and the fan to On. Wait until no frost remains on the indoor coil or the outdoor copper line. This usually takes several hours. Do not scrape ice off the coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If you cannot see light through the filter, replace it. Use the size printed on the old filter's frame. Very restrictive filters (MERV 13 or higher) can starve some systems unless the ducts were designed for them.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it is set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint, around 72–78°F rather than 60°F. If the display is blank or faint, replace the batteries.
- **Open vents and clear return grilles.** Make sure most supply registers are open and nothing blocks the return.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Melting ice produces a lot of water. Make sure the drain line is flowing and the drain pan is not overflowing. If your system has a float or condensate safety switch, it may have shut the system off. Clear the drain at the outside termination or the access tee, using a wet/dry vacuum if needed.
- **Check breakers and panels.** Confirm the breakers and disconnects for both the indoor and outdoor units are on. Make sure the blower compartment door is firmly seated, because a loose panel can keep the blower from running.
- **Restart and watch.** After thawing, run Cool and check the copper line at the outdoor unit after 15–20 minutes. It should feel cold and sweaty, not frosty. If frost returns within a day after these steps, stop the system and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A technician should confirm the coil has fully thawed before measuring anything, because readings taken on a frozen coil are meaningless. Expect most of the following steps:

- **Measure airflow.** They will check total external static pressure and the temperature drop across the coil. A typical drop is about 16–22°F.
- **Verify blower settings.** They will compare blower speed or airflow settings with the system tonnage, especially on variable-speed air handlers.
- **Inspect the blower.** This covers the motor, wheel, and capacitor (on PSC motors), plus the amp draw.
- **Inspect the coil.** They will check the evaporator coil for dirt and the duct system for restrictions.
- **Check the refrigerant charge.** With gauges or digital probes, they will measure superheat and subcooling according to the metering device. They will also compare readings against the charging chart on the outdoor unit.
- **Search for leaks.** If the charge is low, they should use an electronic detector, bubble solution, or a nitrogen pressure test. A good technician finds the leak rather than just adding refrigerant.
- **Check the metering device and filter-drier.** Restrictions here show up as high superheat combined with normal or high subcooling.

**Red flag:** Be wary of a quote that adds refrigerant without testing airflow or looking for a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, filter visibly dirty | Restricted airflow | Thaw the coil, replace the filter | Only needed if the coil refreezes |
| Ice after running on a cool night | Low outdoor or indoor temperature | Raise the setpoint, avoid cooling below about 60°F outdoors | Check for low-ambient controls if needed |
| Ice returns with a clean filter and open vents | Low refrigerant or leak | Shut the system off and thaw it | Leak search, repair, evacuate, recharge |
| Weak airflow, blower loud or not running | Blower motor, capacitor, or settings | Check the breaker and blower door | Test the motor and capacitor, correct the airflow settings |
| Frost on the line set, high energy bills | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw the coil, replace the filter | Clean the coil |
| Water overflowing while thawing | Clogged condensate drain | Clear the drain line | Clean the pan, check the float switch |
| Ice on only part of the coil | Metering device restriction or low charge | Thaw the coil | Diagnose and replace the TXV or filter-drier |

## Repair costs

These are typical US price ranges. Your quote will vary by region, system size, and warranty coverage.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter replacement | $10–$40 (DIY) |
| Diagnostic visit | $80–$200 |
| Evaporator coil cleaning | $150–$500 |
| Blower capacitor replacement | $120–$350 |
| Blower motor replacement (PSC) | $350–$800 |
| Blower motor replacement (ECM or variable-speed) | $600–$1,500 |
| Leak search | $150–$500 |
| Small leak repair plus recharge | $300–$1,200 |
| TXV or filter-drier replacement | $400–$1,200 |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$3,000+ |
| Duct repairs | $200–$2,000 |

**Refrigerant cost:** Recharging with R-410A or R-454B depends on how much is needed, often $100–$150 per pound plus labor. Older systems that use R-22 cost much more to recharge.

**Warranty:** American Standard parts warranties often cover the coil itself if the unit is registered, but labor is usually not covered. Check your registration before approving a coil replacement.

## What this code means

"Leaking water" is a symptom, not a fault code. Bryant central AC systems do not display an error for it. Evolution systems with a connected float switch may show a condensate or overflow alert on the thermostat, depending on how the switch is wired.

Here is how drainage normally works. During cooling, the indoor evaporator coil condenses humidity into water. The water drips into a drain pan and flows out a primary condensate line, either by gravity or through a condensate pump. Some installations also have a secondary drain pan or line.

A leak means water is escaping somewhere along that path. The most common reason is that the water cannot get out fast enough, so the pan overflows.

If you see water dripping from supply registers, or your coil is iced over, read "Bryant AC Frozen Evaporator Coil" first. Melting ice is a separate root cause.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged primary condensate drain line.** Algae, sludge, dust or insects block the PVC line or its trap. The pan then overflows. This is by far the most common cause.
2. **Failed or clogged condensate pump.** This applies to basement, attic or closet units that can't drain by gravity. The pump motor, float or check valve fails, or the reservoir fills with sludge.
3. **Dirty air filter causing coil icing.** Restricted airflow freezes the coil. When it thaws, more water comes off than the pan can handle, and it may drip outside the pan. Low refrigerant can also freeze the coil, and that requires a technician.
4. **Disconnected, cracked or poorly sloped drain piping.** Joints separate, glue fails, or the line lacks pitch or a proper trap. Negative pressure in the cabinet can hold water in the pan when the trap is missing.
5. **Cracked or rusted drain pan.** This is common on older coils with metal pans or aged plastic pans. On many coils the primary pan is integral to the coil assembly.
6. **Clogged secondary pan or drain (attic units).** The overflow pan under an attic air handler fills and spills through the ceiling.
7. **Condensation on uninsulated ducts or refrigerant lines.** Sweating suction lines or ducts drip in humid spaces. This can be mistaken for a pan leak.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling off** at the thermostat. Leave the fan on Auto. Mop up standing water and protect anything below.
- **Check the air filter.** If it is dirty, replace it. If the coil looks iced, leave cooling off and run the fan only until it thaws.
- **Find the condensate line outlet.** It is usually a PVC pipe ending outdoors near the foundation, at a floor drain, or at a pump. Note whether water is dripping out while the system runs. No flow plus a leak strongly suggests a clog.
- **Clear the drain at the access point.** Many lines have a capped vent or a T near the indoor unit. Remove the cap and slowly pour about a cup of plain distilled white vinegar or warm water in. A wet/dry shop vac on the outdoor end can pull out a clog. Use only plain vinegar or water. Don't use bleach mixed with other cleaners, and don't use mechanical snakes that could crack fittings.
- **Check the condensate pump, if you have one.** Make sure it is plugged in and its outlet tubing isn't kinked. If the reservoir is full and the pump isn't running, unplug it and call a technician.
- **Look at the secondary pan** under an attic unit. If it holds water, the primary drain is blocked.
- **Restore power once.** If a float switch shut the system off, it should reset by itself after water drains. Try cooling once. If it shuts off or leaks again, stop and call a pro.
- **Make sure the exterior and access panels are seated.** Don't open the coil cabinet or the furnace burner compartment.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Confirms where the water is coming from.** The tech will distinguish between pan overflow, a piping leak, condensation on ducts or lines, and coil icing.
- **Inspects and flushes the drain.** They check the primary drain and trap, often blowing it out with nitrogen or CO2 or vacuuming it. They also verify slope and that the trap is correct for the cabinet pressure.
- **Tests the condensate pump.** They check the float, motor and check valve, and replace the pump if needed.
- **Checks the float or overflow switch.** They test the switch and confirm it is wired to interrupt cooling.
- **Inspects the drain pan for cracks or rust.** If the pan is integral to the coil, they will quote a pan or coil replacement.
- **Checks for icing.** They check airflow, blower operation and coil cleanliness. If icing is present, they measure the refrigerant charge with gauges. Any leak search and repair is licensed work.
- **Checks insulation.** They verify insulation on the suction line and on ducts in unconditioned spaces.

A fair quote names the specific cause. Be cautious if you are told you need a new coil before the drain has been cleared and tested.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water around the furnace or air handler, none at the drain outlet | Clogged condensate line | Turn cooling off. Flush with vinegar or water and shop-vac the outlet | Power-flush the line, fix the trap and slope |
| AC stopped cooling, pan full of water | Float switch tripped by a clog | Clear the drain, then restore power once | Test the float switch and clear the drain |
| Pump reservoir full, pump silent | Failed condensate pump | Check that it is plugged in and the tubing isn't kinked | Replace the pump or check valve |
| Ice on lines or coil, water after it thaws | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace the filter, then run the fan only | Check airflow and refrigerant charge, repair leaks |
| Water through an attic ceiling | Primary clog plus secondary pan overflow | Turn cooling off, check the secondary pan | Clear both drains and add a float switch |
| Drip at a pipe joint | Separated or cracked PVC | Note the location, turn cooling off | Re-glue or replace the fitting |
| Constant leak with a clear drain | Cracked or rusted pan | Turn cooling off | Replace the pan or coil |
| Drips along ducts or the copper line | Missing or damaged insulation | None beyond noting it | Re-insulate lines and ducts |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges. Your price will vary by region and by how easy the equipment is to reach.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| DIY drain clearing (vinegar, shop vac) | $0–$20 |
| Professional drain line flush or clear | $75–$250 |
| Drain piping or trap repair | $100–$350 |
| Float or overflow switch install or replacement | $100–$250 |
| Condensate pump replacement | $150–$450 |
| Secondary drain pan replacement | $150–$500 |
| Primary pan replacement (where separately serviceable) | $250–$700 |
| Evaporator coil replacement (integral pan, includes refrigerant work) | $1,200–$2,500+ |

| Line or duct insulation | $100–$400 |

The cost of a coil replacement depends on whether the coil is still under warranty. Bryant parts warranties typically require registration; labor is usually not covered.

## What this code means

This is not a numbered fault code. It's a visible symptom: frost or ice on the copper refrigerant line, usually the larger insulated suction line running between the indoor coil and the outdoor unit, sometimes right at the outdoor service valve.

The suction line normally runs cool and sweaty, around 40–50°F. Ice means refrigerant inside it is colder than 32°F. That happens when too little heat reaches the indoor coil (an airflow problem) or when refrigerant pressure is too low (a charge or metering problem). Ice on the line nearly always goes with ice on the indoor coil, which is covered in more depth in the Carrier AC Frozen Evaporator Coil guide. This page focuses on what the line ice tells you and how to check it.

The symptom does not auto-clear. Most Carrier single-stage and two-stage units have no freeze lockout and keep running until the ice blocks airflow, at which point cooling drops off. Some Infinity systems may show low-pressure or airflow alerts on the wall control. The behavior varies by model.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Restricted indoor airflow.** The most common cause is a dirty filter. Closed or blocked supply registers, blocked return grilles, or collapsed ductwork also cause it. Low airflow lets the coil and suction line drop below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Low charge drops suction pressure and temperature. Frost may start at the outdoor service valve or partway along the line. A frost line that keeps creeping back toward the outdoor unit is typical.
3. **Dirty indoor evaporator coil.** Dust buildup on the coil, often after years of poor filtration, blocks heat transfer even when the filter is clean.
4. **Blower problems.** A failing blower motor or capacitor, a wrong speed tap, or an incorrect airflow setting on ECM or variable-speed blowers can all cut airflow. The airflow setting is a common culprit after an install or board replacement.
5. **Metering device restriction.** A sticking TXV or a partially plugged piston or filter-drier can starve the coil. Ice then often appears at or right after the restriction.
6. **Running in low outdoor temperatures.** Cooling below roughly 60–65°F outdoors without low-ambient controls can drop head pressure enough to freeze the coil and line.
7. **Thermostat fan or setpoint misuse.** Very low setpoints with long continuous runs in humid shoulder seasons can contribute, especially combined with marginal airflow.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Stop the freeze.** Set the thermostat to **Off** and the fan to **On**. Let the ice melt completely, which usually takes 2–6 hours. Don't chip ice or use a heat gun.
2. **Check the air filter.** If it's dirty, replace it, or wash it if it's a reusable type. Make sure the airflow arrow points toward the blower.
3. **Open the vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or curtains.
4. **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's in Cool mode with a reasonable setpoint (72–78°F). Replace the batteries if the display is weak or blank.
5. **Watch the condensate.** As the ice melts, a lot of water will drain. Make sure the condensate line and pan are draining and not overflowing. Clear the line from its accessible cleanout if it's clogged.
6. **Make sure panels are seated.** The blower and filter access doors must be fully closed. Loose panels can cause odd airflow, and many units won't run the blower if a panel is ajar.
7. **Test once.** After everything is fully thawed and dry, run cooling and check the suction line after 15–20 minutes. It should feel cold and sweaty but not frosted. If frost returns, shut it off and call a technician. Don't keep cycling it.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Airflow first.** The tech measures temperature drop across the coil (typically 16–22°F) and may check static pressure. They inspect the blower, the capacitor, and the speed or airflow configuration (dip switches or Infinity setup) against the outdoor unit's tonnage.
- **Coil inspection.** They look at the indoor coil face for dirt and matted debris.
- **Refrigerant pressures.** Gauges or probes go on the service ports to read suction and liquid pressures. The tech calculates **superheat** for piston systems or **subcooling** for TXV systems, then compares the results to the Carrier charging chart on the unit.
- **Leak search.** If the charge is low, the tech should look for the leak with an electronic detector, bubble solution, UV dye, or a nitrogen pressure test before adding refrigerant. Common leak spots are the indoor coil, flare or brazed joints, and service valves.
- **Metering check.** High superheat with normal or high subcooling points to a restriction, such as a TXV, piston, or filter-drier problem.
- **Low-ambient check.** If the freezing only happens on cool days, the tech checks outdoor conditions and the controls.

A credible quote should state the measured superheat or subcooling and, if refrigerant is needed, where the leak is. Be wary of "it just needs a top-off" with no leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on suction line and indoor coil, filter very dirty | Restricted airflow | Thaw, replace filter, open vents | Check static pressure if it recurs |
| Frost starts at outdoor service valve, cooling weak | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off cooling, thaw | Leak search, repair, evacuate, recharge |
| Ice returns with a clean filter, weak airflow at registers | Dirty coil or blower issue | Confirm returns are clear | Clean coil; test blower motor and capacitor; verify airflow setting |
| Frost at one point on the line, warm beyond it | Metering device or filter-drier restriction | Turn off cooling | Diagnose restriction; replace TXV or drier |
| Icing only on cool nights or mild days | Low ambient operation | Raise setpoint or use fan only on cool days | Verify charge; add low-ambient control if needed |
| Water overflowing during thaw | Melt overwhelming drain or clogged line | Clear condensate line, empty pan | Repair drain or float switch |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter (DIY) | $5–$40 |
| Service call / diagnosis | $90–$200 |
| Indoor coil cleaning | $150–$450 |
| Blower capacitor | $120–$300 |
| Blower motor (PSC) | $350–$700 |
| Blower motor (ECM / variable-speed) | $600–$1,500+ |
| Leak search | $150–$500 |
| Minor leak repair (joint, valve core, flare) | $150–$600 plus refrigerant |
| Refrigerant recharge (R-410A or R-454B) | $200–$800+, depending on pounds and local pricing |
| TXV replacement | $500–$1,200 |
| Filter-drier replacement | $200–$500 |
| Indoor evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$3,000+ |

Older Carrier units on R-22 cost much more to recharge. A large leak in an old system is often the point where replacement is worth discussing.

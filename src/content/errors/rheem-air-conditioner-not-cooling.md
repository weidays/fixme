## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Rheem or Ruud air conditioner is running (or trying to) but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. Some newer EcoNet-enabled Rheem systems will display a fault or communication code on the thermostat or app, but the classic RA-series condensers have no display, so you diagnose by behavior.

There are two broad scenarios: the **outdoor unit isn't running at all** (electrical/control problem), or the **outdoor unit runs but the system won't cool** (airflow, refrigerant, or capacity problem). Sorting which one you have is the first step, and it's something you can safely observe before calling anyone.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops capacity and can freeze the indoor coil into a block of ice.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust choke heat rejection, so the system runs constantly without cooling.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "ON" vs. "AUTO"), or a miswired/failing thermostat.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a cheap part that stops the compressor and/or fan motor from starting. You may hear a hum or clicking outside.
5. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit; burned or pitted contacts stop the condenser from energizing.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, long run times, and often a frozen indoor coil or ice on the outdoor lines.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — a downstream result of low airflow or low refrigerant; the unit blows warm because the coil is iced over.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor runs but the top fan doesn't spin, causing an overheat shutdown.
9. **Compressor failure or hard-start issues** — the most expensive; the compressor won't start, trips the breaker, or is seized.
10. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — often a symptom of one of the problems above, not the root cause.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything smells hot, is buzzing loudly, or the breaker trips.

- **Filter:** Check and replace if dirty. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling. Run the fan a while after swapping.
- **Thermostat:** Set to **COOL** and the fan to **AUTO**. Lower the setpoint 5°F below room temp. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once only**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris. Ensure at least 2 feet of clearance. Gently rinse the outside of the coil fins with a garden hose (water only, no pressure washer) with the power **off**.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or clogged condensate line — some units have a float switch that shuts off cooling when the pan fills. A shop-vac on the drain line outside often clears it.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or outdoor copper lines, turn the AC **off** and set the fan to **ON** to thaw it (can take a few hours). Cooling while frozen won't work and can damage the compressor.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or add refrigerant.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech works from cheap to expensive, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Airflow and filter check** — confirms the basics, inspects the evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
2. **Electrical checks at the condenser** — measures voltage at the contactor, tests the run capacitor with a meter (a good $15 capacitor test rules out a $1,500 misdiagnosis), and inspects the contactor for pitting.
3. **Amp draw** — clamps the compressor and fan motor to see if they're pulling correct current, locked-rotor amps, or nothing at all.
4. **Refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to read suction and liquid pressures and takes superheat/subcooling measurements. This tells them if the charge is correct and whether a leak exists.
5. **Leak search** — if low, they use a leak detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test to locate the leak (coil, line set, Schrader valves).
6. **EcoNet diagnostics** — on communicating systems, they'll read fault history from the thermostat/board.

Ask for the measured capacitor reading, refrigerant pressures, and amp draws. Vague answers like "it just needs a recharge" without finding the leak are a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked return | Replace filter, open vents | Inspect ductwork, blower |
| Ice on indoor coil or copper lines | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Find leak, recharge, verify airflow |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor fan runs | Failed capacitor, contactor, or breaker | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Humming outside, fan/compressor won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor, test hard-start |
| Compressor runs, top fan not spinning | Failed condenser fan motor | Turn off to prevent overheat | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Long run times, house never cools | Low refrigerant, dirty condenser coil | Rinse outdoor coil, check filter | Pressure check, leak repair |
| AC shuts off shortly after starting | Full condensate pan / float switch, overheating | Clear condensate line | Diagnose safety trip, amp draw |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor or wiring fault | Do NOT keep resetting | Diagnose compressor/electrical |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor (regional and unit-specific variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$200 professionally; ~$15 for spray if DIY.
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700 installed.
- **Condensate line clearing:** $75–$250, or DIY with a shop-vac.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type. Older R-22 units cost much more.
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,900 (often covered under Rheem's parts warranty if the unit is registered and in-warranty).
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+. On a unit 12+ years old, weigh this against full system replacement.

Rheem residential ACs typically carry a **10-year parts warranty** if registered within 60 days of installation — you pay labor, not the part. Have your model/serial ready to check.

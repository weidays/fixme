## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on your Rheem air conditioner — it's the symptom you're experiencing. Rheem/Ruud central ACs don't have a self-diagnosing display on the condenser the way some furnaces do, so "not cooling" covers everything from the system running with weak, warm airflow to the outdoor unit not starting at all.

Because the cause could be as simple as a dirty filter or as serious as a failed compressor or refrigerant leak, this page walks you through the safe checks you can do yourself, then explains how a technician narrows it down so you can sanity-check any quote.

If you have a Rheem **heat pump** and see a blinking light on the control board, that's a different diagnostic path — see "Rheem Heat Pump Flashing Light" in Related codes.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — Restricts airflow across the indoor coil, causing weak cooling and, eventually, a frozen coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — Dirt, grass, and cottonwood block heat rejection; the unit runs but can't cool effectively.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — Extremely common on aging units. The compressor or fan hums but won't start, or the outdoor fan won't spin.
4. **Thermostat problem** — Dead batteries, wrong mode, or a miswired/failed thermostat that never calls for cooling.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse** — The outdoor unit gets no power and won't turn on.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Caused by low airflow (filter) or low refrigerant; ice blocks airflow entirely.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Weak cooling, long run times, and possible icing. Always indicates a leak, not "normal loss."
8. **Failed contactor** — The relay that powers the compressor doesn't close, so the outdoor unit stays silent.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — The compressor may run but overheat and shut down without airflow over the coil.
10. **Failed compressor** — The most expensive outcome; the unit won't cool at all despite calling for it.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop at anything requiring you to open the electrical panel of the unit, touch capacitors, or handle refrigerant.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the single most common fix.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel. AC systems often have two breakers (indoor air handler + outdoor condenser). Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs, and that return grilles aren't dusty.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or leaves against the coil. Gently clear anything blocking airflow. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the copper lines or coil, **turn the system to OFF and the fan to ON** to thaw it (can take several hours). Running a frozen unit can damage the compressor.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged condensate line can trigger a float switch that shuts the system off. If you have accessible drain cleaning tablets or a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain outlet, clearing a clog is safe DIY.

Do **not** attempt to add refrigerant, replace a capacitor, or bypass any float or safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work systematically. This is roughly what to expect so you can gauge whether a quote is fair:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — Confirms the thermostat is actually sending 24V to the outdoor unit (checks the Y terminal).
2. **Check power and the contactor** — Measures voltage at the disconnect and tests whether the contactor is pulling in.
3. **Test the capacitor** — Uses a meter to check microfarad rating against the label. A weak cap is a fast, cheap find.
4. **Measure temperature split** — Compares return vs. supply air temperature (typically ~14–20°F on a healthy system).
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — Reads suction and head pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to judge charge. Low readings point to a leak.
6. **Leak search** — If charge is low, uses electronic detector or dye to locate the leak before adding refrigerant.
7. **Inspect motors and coils** — Checks condenser fan operation, coil cleanliness, and evaporator airflow.
8. **Compressor test** — If everything else is good, tests compressor windings and amp draw.

Be cautious of any tech who tops off refrigerant without looking for a leak — that's a temporary and non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, dirty filter | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Check for resulting coil freeze |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit completely dead | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, verify charge |
| Long run times, never reaches setpoint | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Clear debris outside | Clean coil, check refrigerant |
| Compressor runs, air still warm | Low refrigerant / failed compressor | None — call pro | Leak search, charge or replace |
| System shuts off shortly after starting | Float switch (full drain pan) | Clear condensate drain | Confirm drain, inspect coil |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries / wiring | Replace batteries | Test thermostat and 24V supply |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US pricing including parts and labor. Your area, unit age, and refrigerant type (older R-410A units cost more to service) affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,000+ depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often worth weighing against a full system replacement on older units)

For units over 12–15 years old with a failed compressor or major leak, ask your tech whether replacement makes more financial sense than repair.

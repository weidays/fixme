## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a numeric fault code — it's the most common complaint Rheem homeowners report, covering anything from air that's slightly warm to a system that runs constantly without lowering the house temperature. Unlike a heat pump flashing a diagnostic pattern, a standard Rheem AC that simply won't cool may show **no error at all**, or your thermostat may report the system is "cooling" while warm air comes out.

The underlying problem is almost always one of: reduced airflow, a refrigerant issue, a failed electrical component (capacitor or contactor), or a compressor that isn't running. Some Rheem systems paired with EcoNet or Prestige communicating thermostats **will** log a fault; a basic single-stage RA13/RA14 with a conventional thermostat usually won't. Behavior varies by model, so check your thermostat's alerts.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
3. **Failed run/dual capacitor** — the compressor or fan won't start; you may hear humming or clicking.
4. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — caked with dust, grass, and cottonwood, it can't reject heat.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cooling weakens over weeks; ice or hissing may appear. Always a leak, never "used up."
6. **Failed contactor** — the 24V relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't pull in, so nothing outside runs.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor disconnect or the 5A control fuse on the air handler board.
8. **Bad compressor** — the least common but most expensive; often preceded by hard-starting or breaker trips.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and 5°F below room temp. Confirm the fan is on AUTO, not just ON (ON blows air even when not cooling). Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Confirm the pull-out disconnect box next to the condenser is fully seated.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF and set the fan to ON. Let it thaw fully (a few hours) before restarting.
- **Condensate:** Check that the drain pan isn't full and the float switch hasn't tripped — a full pan can shut the system down. Clear a visibly clogged drain line if accessible.
- **Outdoor coil:** Gently rinse the outdoor unit's fins with a garden hose (never a pressure washer) after shutting off power.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable process — use this to sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirms airflow** — checks filter, blower operation, and any icing before touching refrigerant.
2. **Checks electrical** — measures capacitor microfarads against the rated value, tests the contactor, and verifies 24V control voltage.
3. **Measures superheat and subcooling** — connects gauges to determine if refrigerant charge is correct, low (leak), or overcharged.
4. **Leak search if low** — dye, electronic sniffer, or nitrogen pressure test to find the leak *before* recharging.
5. **Tests compressor** — checks amp draw, windings resistance, and start behavior.
6. **Reads any EcoNet/Prestige fault log** on communicating systems.

A legitimate diagnosis should explain *why* it's not cooling, not just "you need refrigerant." Topping off without finding a leak is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked return | Replace filter, clear returns | Inspect blower and ductwork |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, fan ON, let thaw | Find restriction or leak, recharge |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — shut it off | Test and replace dual capacitor |
| Nothing outside runs, indoor fan works | Contactor or control fuse | Check breaker/disconnect | Replace contactor or 5A fuse |
| Runs constantly, house won't cool | Low refrigerant or dirty condenser | Rinse outdoor coil | Leak repair, recharge |
| Breaker trips on cooling call | Shorted compressor or wiring | Reset once only | Test compressor and wiring |
| Gradually weaker cooling over weeks | Slow refrigerant leak | None | Leak search and repair |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Rheem parts fall in the typical range for major brands.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Coil cleaning (indoor or outdoor):** $100–$300
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Control board fuse:** $75–$200
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

If your Rheem is under 10 years old, check warranty — Rheem's standard parts warranty is often 10 years with registration, though **labor is rarely covered**.

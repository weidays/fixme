## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on a Rheem air conditioner — it's a symptom. Your Rheem split system (like the RA14, RA16, or Prestige series) may be running, blowing air, and drawing power, yet the air coming from your vents is warm or barely cool.

Because most Rheem residential AC condensers don't have a digital display, there's no numeric code to read. Some Rheem communicating systems paired with an EcoNet thermostat will log system faults, and two-stage or variable-speed condensers may store diagnostic data on the control board — but a basic single-stage unit gives you no code at all. **This means diagnosis relies on symptoms and measurements, which varies by how sophisticated your specific model and thermostat are.**

The root cause is almost always one of three things: airflow is restricted, the refrigerant charge is low, or an electrical component (capacitor, contactor, or compressor) has failed.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricted airflow across the indoor coil is the number-one reason a unit runs without cooling. Severe restriction can freeze the coil solid.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — The outdoor unit can't reject heat when the coil fins are packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — If the system is undercharged, it can't absorb heat. This is never a "top-off" fix; refrigerant leaks must be located and repaired.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — A common failure. The compressor or fan motor hums, struggles, or won't start.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
6. **Faulty contactor** — The relay that sends power to the condenser can pit or stick, preventing the compressor from engaging.
7. **Thermostat or wiring fault** — A miscalibrated, dead-battery, or miswired thermostat may not be calling for cooling correctly.
8. **Failing compressor** — The most expensive cause. The unit won't cool despite everything else checking out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should attempt. Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser or touch refrigerant lines.

- **Filter:** Pull your air filter. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. Then run the system for 30 minutes.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. If one has tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit airflow:** With the unit off, look for grass clippings, leaves, or debris smothering the condenser. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (low pressure, top-down).
- **Condensate drain:** If your air handler has a float switch and the drain is clogged, the system may shut down cooling. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear a visibly clogged drain line.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully — several hours — before restarting. Persistent freezing means a pro is needed.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work through a repeatable sequence so you can sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling** — verify 24V control signal from the thermostat reaches the contactor.
2. **Check the contactor and capacitor** — measure capacitor microfarads against the rated value stamped on it; inspect the contactor for pitting.
3. **Verify compressor and fan operation** — confirm the compressor is drawing correct amperage (compared to the rated LRA/RLA on the data plate).
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — read suction and liquid pressures, measure superheat and subcooling to determine if the charge is correct. Low readings point to a leak.
5. **Leak search** — if undercharged, use electronic detectors or dye to locate the leak before adding refrigerant.
6. **Inspect coils and airflow** — measure temperature split across the indoor coil (a healthy system typically shows a 16–22°F drop).

If a tech offers to "just add a pound of Freon" without searching for a leak, get a second opinion — that's a band-aid, not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter, run 30 min | Check ductwork, blower motor |
| Outdoor unit hot, poor cooling | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse fins with hose (unit off) | Deep coil cleaning, fin comb |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Charge check, leak search |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan runs, compressor won't engage | Bad contactor | None — call pro | Replace contactor |
| Cools poorly, gradually worse | Refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Leak repair & recharge |
| Breaker trips on startup | Electrical / compressor fault | Reset once only | Amp draw test, diagnose short |
| No cooling call at all | Thermostat / wiring | Replace batteries, set to COOL | Test control wiring, thermostat |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher for R-410A/newer refrigerants and hard-to-reach leaks)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500 installed
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ — often the point where replacing the whole condenser makes more financial sense, especially on units over 10 years old

Diagnostic/service call fees typically run $80–$180 and are often credited toward the repair.

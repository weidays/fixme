## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you'll notice at the thermostat: the Rheem air conditioner is running (or trying to) but the house never reaches the setpoint, or the air from the vents is warm. Rheem central AC units generally don't display an alphanumeric error code the way a furnace board does; instead you diagnose by airflow, temperature split, and the outdoor unit's behavior.

There are really three failure patterns hiding under "not cooling":

1. **The system runs but blows warm air** — usually airflow or refrigerant related.
2. **The outdoor unit won't start** at all — power, capacitor, contactor, or control problem.
3. **The system short-cycles or freezes up** — airflow restriction or low charge.

Because the exact controls, staging, and any communicating-thermostat diagnostics vary by model (single-stage RA13/RA14 vs. two-stage RA16/RA17 vs. variable-speed RA20 and Endeavor line), treat the steps below as general guidance and confirm against your unit's manual.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops cooling capacity and can freeze the coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a setpoint/schedule issue.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser or air handler has lost power.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; the unit runs but nothing cold reaches the vents.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bulging or dead capacitor stops the compressor or condenser fan.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharge means weak or no cooling and often ice on the lines.
7. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so it runs constantly without cooling.
8. **Contactor or control board fault** — the outdoor unit doesn't get the signal to start.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive; the unit hums, trips, or won't start.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything smells burnt, looks scorched, or is beyond these steps.

- **Check the filter.** A gray, matted filter kills cooling. Replace it and see if performance recovers over the next hour.
- **Confirm the thermostat.** Set it to **Cool**, drop the setpoint 5°F below room temperature, and confirm the fan is on Auto. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breakers.** Look for a tripped breaker for the air handler and a separate one for the outdoor condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Look at the vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Check for ice.** If the indoor coil or the copper lines are frosted, set the system to **Fan Only** for 2–4 hours to thaw, then replace the filter before restarting in Cool.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A clogged drain can trigger a float switch that shuts cooling off. Clear standing water in the pan and confirm the drain line is flowing.
- **Rinse the outdoor unit gently.** With power off at the disconnect, hose loose grass and debris off the condenser fins from the outside — no pressure washer.

If cooling still doesn't return after the filter, thermostat, and breaker checks, it's a pro job — especially anything involving refrigerant, capacitors, or the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician follows a repeatable path, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verifies the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirms 24V reaching the outdoor unit.
2. **Measures the temperature split** across the indoor coil (supply vs. return). A healthy split is roughly 15–20°F; low or high split points toward airflow or charge issues.
3. **Inspects airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil cleanliness.
4. **Tests the capacitor** with a meter (microfarad reading vs. rating on the label).
5. **Checks the contactor and control voltage** to confirm the compressor and fan are being commanded on.
6. **Connects gauges** to read suction/liquid pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to judge refrigerant charge. Low readings prompt a **leak search** (dye, electronic detector, or bubble test).
7. **Evaluates the compressor** — amp draw, windings, and start behavior — if power and controls check out but it won't run.

Ask for the temperature split numbers and refrigerant readings — a legitimate diagnosis includes them.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs but blows warm air | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Measure split, check blower |
| Ice on coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Fan-only to thaw, new filter | Find/repair leak, recharge |
| Outdoor unit silent, no cooling | Tripped breaker or dead capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor, contactor |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Weak or leaking refrigerant charge | None — call a pro | Leak search, seal, recharge |
| Unit hums then trips breaker | Failing compressor or hard start | Stop resetting the breaker | Test compressor, amp draw |
| Cooling stops, water near air handler | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, check switch |
| Runs constantly, weak cooling | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse fins (power off) | Deep coil clean, verify charge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor), varying by region and model:

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0–$15 (DIY)
- **Breaker reset:** $0 (DIY) — but a repeatedly tripping breaker means a pro diagnosis
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher if the leak is in the coil)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,600+ (often prompts a full-unit replacement conversation on older systems)

If your Rheem AC is more than 12–15 years old and facing a compressor or coil, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison.

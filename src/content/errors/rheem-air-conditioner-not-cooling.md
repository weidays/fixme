## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you're seeing when your Rheem or Ruud air conditioner runs but the house won't get cold, or the air from the vents feels warm. On Rheem systems with a communicating control board (Prestige/EcoNet models), a genuine hard fault will usually show a status code on the thermostat or a flashing LED on the control board. On basic single-stage systems (RA13/RA14/RA16), there's often no code at all — just weak or warm airflow.

The root cause almost always falls into one of three buckets: **restricted airflow** (filter, coils, blower), **a refrigerant problem** (low charge from a leak), or an **electrical/mechanical failure** (capacitor, contactor, compressor, or outdoor fan motor). This page helps you narrow it down before you pay for a service call.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the indoor coil of airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the coil solid.
2. **Frozen indoor evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. Ice on the coil means little or no cold air until it thaws.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection so the system can't cool efficiently.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — if the outdoor unit has no power, the indoor blower runs and blows warm air.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a common failure that stops the compressor or outdoor fan motor from starting. Often shows as a humming outdoor unit.
6. **Bad contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit; pitted or stuck contacts prevent the compressor from engaging.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, warm air, and often a frozen coil or hissing. Requires a certified tech.
8. **Failed condenser fan or blower motor** — no airflow across the coil means no heat transfer.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit runs but produces no cooling.
10. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a loose control wire.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do not open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto** (not On, which blows room-temp air between cycles). Replace batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for debris, tall grass, or leaves against the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (never a pressure washer) with power off at the disconnect.
- **Condensate:** Check for standing water in the drain pan or a full float switch — a clogged drain can shut the system down. Cleaning tablets and a wet/dry vac on the drain line often clear it.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper line at the outdoor unit, turn the system to **Off** and run the fan-only mode for a few hours to thaw it, then replace the filter and try again.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat mode, setpoint, and call for cooling.
2. **Measure airflow** — inspect filter, blower wheel, and coils for restriction or icing.
3. **Read the control board** — on EcoNet/Prestige systems they'll pull status codes; on basic units they'll check for a 24V call reaching the outdoor unit.
4. **Check electrical components** — test the run capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and verify voltage to the compressor and fan motor.
5. **Take refrigerant readings** — connect gauges to measure suction/liquid pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to confirm charge and detect a leak.
6. **Leak search** if charge is low — with electronic detector or dye; adding refrigerant without finding the leak is not a repair.
7. **Test the compressor** — amp draw, windings, and start behavior if all else checks out.

Expect a diagnostic fee of **$75–$200**, often credited toward the repair. Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace compressor" without leak testing or electrical checks.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air from vents, unit running | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter, thaw coil in fan mode | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant, restore charge |
| Indoor coil or line iced up | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw, swap filter | Find airflow restriction or leak; recharge |
| Outdoor unit humming, not spinning | Failed run capacitor | None — electrical | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor blows warm | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Weak cooling, house never reaches setpoint | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Rinse outdoor coil, clear debris | Clean coil, check charge and leak-test |
| Nothing runs at all | Thermostat, control power, or float switch | Check batteries, mode, clear drain | Trace 24V power, board diagnosis |
| Runs constantly but no cooling | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test and replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 professional
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900 (ECM/variable-speed motors on Prestige units cost more)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location; coil leaks are the high end
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+; on an older unit, replacing the whole condenser or system is often the better value

Prices vary by region, model, and whether your unit is still under Rheem's parts warranty (typically 10 years with registration — you still pay labor).

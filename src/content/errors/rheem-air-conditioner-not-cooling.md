## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Rheem air conditioner is powered on, the thermostat is calling for cool, but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or not getting the house to setpoint.

Rheem split systems don't usually show a lettered error for this the way a furnace board does. Instead, you diagnose by the pattern: Is the outdoor unit running? Is the indoor blower running? Is the air cold at the vents but not reaching the house, or warm everywhere? Each answer points to a different cause.

Because "not cooling" can range from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, this is rated **pro** — most permanent fixes involve refrigerant or sealed-system work you legally and safely cannot do yourself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — leaves, grass, and dust choke heat rejection, so the system can't dump heat outside.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks cooling entirely.
4. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "ON" vs "AUTO"), or a miswired/failed thermostat.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice, and long run times. Requires a pro to find and repair.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — the outdoor fan or compressor hums but won't start, or trips the breaker.
7. **Clogged condensate drain / float switch tripped** — a full drain pan can shut the system off on many setups.
8. **Contactor or control board fault** — outdoor unit won't energize even with a cooling call.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or the electrical panel beyond the main breaker.

- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray or clogged, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Check the thermostat.** Set it to COOL, fan to AUTO, and drop the setpoint a few degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Check the breakers.** Both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser have breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Look for ice.** If the indoor coil or the copper lines are frosted, set the system to OFF (or fan-only) and let it thaw fully for a few hours before running again. Then replace the filter.
- **Check supply and return vents.** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Closing too many vents restricts airflow.
- **Check the condensate drain.** If your unit has a float switch and the drain pan is full, the system may refuse to cool. Clearing the drain line is DIY-safe; see parts below.
- **Clear the outdoor unit.** Turn it off, then rinse debris and gently spray the condenser fins with a garden hose (top to bottom). Keep the coil cleaner for a pro or careful DIY.

If the system still won't cool after these checks, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the steps helps you sanity-check a quote:

- **Confirm the call and airflow.** Verifies the thermostat is calling for cool, the blower runs, and airflow is adequate.
- **Measure temperature split.** A healthy system drops supply air about 15–22°F below return air. A low split points to refrigerant or airflow issues.
- **Check refrigerant charge with gauges.** Reads suction and liquid pressures plus superheat/subcooling. Low readings indicate a leak, not just a need to "add gas."
- **Leak search if charge is low.** Uses electronic detector or dye to find the leak before any refrigerant is added.
- **Test electrical components.** Checks the run capacitor (microfarad reading), contactor, and compressor amp draw.
- **Inspect coils and drain.** Confirms the condenser and evaporator are clean and the condensate path is clear.

Be wary of any tech who "tops off refrigerant" without leak-testing — that's a temporary patch on a leak you'll pay for again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, both units running | Dirty coil, low refrigerant | Replace filter, rinse condenser | Check charge, find/repair leak |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Fan-only to thaw, new filter | Verify charge and blower airflow |
| Outdoor unit silent | Contactor, capacitor, breaker | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor or capacitor |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — pro job | Replace capacitor |
| Weak airflow at vents | Clogged filter, blower issue | Replace filter, open vents | Inspect blower motor/wheel |
| System shuts off randomly | Full drain / float switch | Clear condensate drain | Repair drain, check float switch |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Undercharge or bad compressor | None — pro job | Charge test; compressor diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, including parts and labor:

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $0 DIY rinse – $150–$300 professional cleaning
- **Thermostat batteries or replacement:** $5 batteries – $150–$350 for a new thermostat installed
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $75–$200 pro
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often worth checking warranty first)

Many Rheem systems carry a 10-year parts warranty if registered — the part may be free, but you'll still pay labor and refrigerant.

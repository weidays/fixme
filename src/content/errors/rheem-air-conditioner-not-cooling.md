## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a stored fault code — Rheem/Ruud central air conditioners don't display a numeric error for this. It means your system is powered and calling for cooling, but the air coming from the vents isn't cold enough to bring the house to the thermostat setpoint.

Because it's a symptom, the cause can sit anywhere in the chain: airflow (filter, coil, blower), the refrigerant circuit (charge, compressor, metering device), or the electrical controls (capacitor, contactor, thermostat). This page walks through the likely culprits in order and shows you which ones you can safely rule out yourself.

If your Rheem is a **heat pump** and you're seeing a blinking indicator on the control board, see the related page on flashing lights instead — that behavior is model-specific and separate from a straight "no cool" complaint.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — restricts airflow enough to drop capacity and, in bad cases, freeze the coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — a coil packed with grass, cottonwood, or dust can't reject heat, so cooling collapses on hot days.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; you'll get warm air and possibly water around the indoor unit.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces capacity and freezes coils. Always a leak, never "used up."
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the outdoor fan may spin but the compressor won't start, or hums and shuts off.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the condenser is pitted or stuck, so the outdoor unit never energizes.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/config fault sending no cool call.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit or air handler has no power.
9. **Failing compressor** — hums, trips the breaker, or won't start even with a good capacitor.
10. **Clogged condensate drain with a safety float switch** — a full drain pan can shut the system off to prevent overflow.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or opening sealed/electrical components:

- **Filter:** Pull the air filter. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the fan is on **Auto**, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler/furnace **and** the outdoor condenser. Reset one that's clearly tripped **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed doors throughout the house.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** Gently clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser fins. Don't remove panels or bend fins.
- **Condensate drain / float switch:** If there's standing water in the drain pan or the system shut off after storms/high humidity, a clogged drain may be tripping a safety float. You can pour water through the drain to check flow, but leave a stubborn clog to a tech.

Do **not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical order — use this to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the cooling call** at the thermostat and confirm 24V reaching the condenser.
2. **Measure temperature split** across the indoor coil (typical target is roughly 16–22°F between return and supply air).
3. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower operation, and the evaporator coil for dirt or ice.
4. **Check outdoor electrical** — test the capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and confirm the fan and compressor energize.
5. **Attach gauges and measure refrigerant** — superheat/subcooling readings tell them whether the charge is correct or there's a leak or restriction.
6. **Leak search** if the charge is low — electronic detector, dye, or soap bubbles to find where refrigerant is escaping before recharging.
7. **Compressor test** — amp draw and windings if the compressor won't start or trips the breaker.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or "replace the compressor" without electrical testing first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, house won't cool | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower, check static pressure |
| Warm air, ice on refrigerant lines | Frozen evaporator coil | Turn AC off, fan on, let it thaw | Find cause: low airflow or low charge |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Bad capacitor or contactor | None — electrical | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Find why it tripped; replace fuse |
| Cooling drops only on hot days | Dirty condenser coil / low charge | Clear debris around unit | Clean coil, check charge, leak search |
| System shut off, water in pan | Clogged drain / float switch | Clear visible blockage | Clear drain line, test float switch |
| Compressor hums, then stops | Weak capacitor or failing compressor | None — electrical | Test capacitor, then compressor |
| No cool call at all | Thermostat settings/batteries | Set to Cool, new batteries | Check thermostat wiring/config |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area, model, and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer blends) affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$450
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Blower motor:** $400–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (higher if the coil must be replaced)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often worth comparing to full system replacement on older units)

If the unit is out of warranty and over ~12–15 years old, weigh a major repair against replacement — a tech can quote both.

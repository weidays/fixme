## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a symptom. Your Rheem air conditioner may run (the outdoor fan spins, the indoor blower moves air) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint on a hot day.

Rheem split systems don't display a lettered/numbered code for this the way a furnace board does. Instead, you diagnose by working through the airflow and refrigeration chain: air filter → indoor coil → refrigerant charge → compressor and condenser. On Prestige and higher-tier units with communicating controls, a companion fault (like a flashing light or a comfort-control alert) may appear — see Related codes.

Because most root causes of "not cooling" involve refrigerant, high-voltage components, or the compressor, this is rated **pro**: a handful of checks are safe for homeowners, but the actual repair usually needs a licensed HVAC tech.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass clippings, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection so the system can't cool.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the single most common "runs but blows warm" cause; refrigerant does not get "used up," so low charge means a leak.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a cheap part that keeps the compressor and/or fan motor running; a weak one causes hard starts, warm air, or short cycling.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; ice blocks heat exchange and airflow stops being cold.
6. **Contactor failure** — the outdoor unit's high-voltage relay pits or sticks, so the compressor won't engage.
7. **Condenser fan motor failure** — outdoor fan not spinning causes the compressor to overheat and trip out.
8. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — bad calibration, dead batteries, or a broken wire that never calls for cooling.
9. **Failing compressor** — the worst case; grounded windings, no start, or loss of capacity. Usually the last thing confirmed.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening a sealed system or touching high-voltage parts.

- **Air filter:** Pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Cool**, temperature several degrees below room temp, and fan to **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim.
- **Breaker:** Check the panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler and to the outdoor condenser (often two separate breakers). Reset **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — don't keep resetting.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Closing too many vents starves airflow.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for a fan that isn't spinning, or coils packed with leaves/grass. Turn off power at the breaker before gently hosing debris off the outside of the coil fins (never pressure-wash).
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float safety switch and shut down cooling. Check for standing water in the drain pan; a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain line can clear a simple clog.
- **Frozen coil / ice on the copper line:** If you see ice on the refrigerant line or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** and run the **fan only** for 1–3 hours to thaw it, then replace the filter and see if it cools normally. If it re-freezes, you need a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Rheem tech should work methodically — use this to sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify airflow first** — filter, blower operation, static pressure, and whether the evaporator coil is clean and ice-free.
2. **Check electrical** — measure the run capacitor's microfarads against the label spec, inspect the contactor for pitting, and confirm 24V control voltage from the thermostat call.
3. **Confirm outdoor operation** — condenser fan spinning, compressor drawing correct amperage (compared to nameplate LRA/RLA).
4. **Gauge the refrigerant** — connect manifold gauges, read suction/head pressures, and check superheat/subcooling against Rheem's charging chart for that model. Low readings point to a leak.
5. **Leak search** — if charge is low, they should locate the leak (electronic detector or dye) before adding refrigerant, not just top it off.
6. **Compressor test** — if pressures and electrical are fine but there's no cooling, they'll test compressor windings and start behavior.

Red flag: a tech who adds refrigerant without looking for the leak, or condemns the compressor without checking the capacitor and contactor first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airflow weak, coil iced over | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, thaw with fan-only | Check blower, static pressure, coil |
| Runs but blows warm, ice on line | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, thaw, then call | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Failed run capacitor | None (high voltage) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker / contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Outdoor fan not spinning, hot unit | Condenser fan motor | Power off, clear debris | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Never calls for cooling | Thermostat / wiring | Batteries, set to Cool | Trace wiring, replace stat |
| Short cycles then warm | Weak compressor/capacitor | None | Amp test, diagnose compressor |
| Dirty outdoor coil, poor cooling | Blocked condenser | Rinse coil gently | Deep coil clean, check charge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Prices vary by region and unit size:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries / basic thermostat:** $5–$250.
- **Condenser coil cleaning (professional):** $100–$250.
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 (depends on leak location; evaporator coil leaks are the pricey end).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often near the cost of a new outdoor unit; if out of warranty, weigh replacing the system).

Note: Rheem parts may be covered under a **5- or 10-year limited warranty** if registered — you often still pay labor and refrigerant. Ask the tech to check your unit's warranty status by serial number before approving a big repair.

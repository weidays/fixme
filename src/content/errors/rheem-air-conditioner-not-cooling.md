## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice at the thermostat: the Rheem unit is powered and may even be running, but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or the house never reaches setpoint. On EcoNet-equipped Rheem systems you may also see a fault message or alert in the EcoNet app, but many standard 24V units give no code at all and simply run without cooling.

The root cause falls into a few buckets: airflow problems (filter, coil, blower), electrical problems (capacitor, contactor, fan motor), or the refrigerant circuit (low charge from a leak, failing compressor). A few are safe DIY checks. Most require a technician with gauges and a meter.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — ice on the indoor coil blocks airflow entirely. Caused by dirty filters, blocked returns, or low refrigerant.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — a weak or bulged dual capacitor stops the compressor and/or condenser fan from starting. Very common on units 7+ years old.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system can't absorb heat properly. Always indicates a leak; never just "low from age."
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with dirt/grass can't reject heat, so cooling drops on hot days.
6. **Failed condenser fan motor** — if the outdoor fan isn't spinning, the compressor overheats and cuts out.
7. **Bad contactor** — pitted or stuck contacts prevent the outdoor unit from energizing.
8. **Thermostat or control fault** — wrong settings, dead batteries, or a wiring/EcoNet communication issue.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit hums or trips the breaker but won't build pressure.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here if the problem isn't obvious — do not open the electrical panel of the condenser or touch refrigerant lines.

- **Air filter** — pull it out. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker** — check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. If one is tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking airflow. Keep return grilles clear.
- **Condensate drain** — a full drain pan or clogged line can trigger a safety float switch that shuts the system down. Check for standing water near the indoor unit.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. If you see ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, turn the system OFF (fan ON) for a few hours to let it thaw, then replace the filter.

Do **not** repeatedly cycle a unit that keeps shutting off, and do not attempt to add refrigerant.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work in this order, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply/return air temperature split (a healthy system runs roughly an 15–20°F drop).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Electrical test** — meter the capacitor (microfarads against rating), check the contactor, and confirm the condenser fan and compressor draw proper amps.
4. **Refrigerant check** — connect gauges to read suction and liquid pressures, then calculate superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak, which they should locate with an electronic detector or dye — not just top off.
5. **EcoNet units** — pull any stored alerts through the app or control board.
6. **Compressor test** — if pressures and electrical are wrong, they'll test the compressor windings.

Be cautious of any tech who adds refrigerant without looking for the leak, or quotes a compressor without testing the capacitor first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, dirty filter | Clogged filter | Replace filter | — |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Frozen evaporator | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Indoor blower runs, outdoor unit silent | Bad capacitor or contactor | Check breaker once | Test & replace capacitor/contactor |
| Outdoor fan not spinning, unit humming | Failed fan motor or capacitor | Shut off to prevent overheat | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Cools poorly on hot days | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris, rinse gently | Deep coil cleaning |
| Never reaches setpoint, hissing | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call a pro | Locate leak, repair, recharge |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor or electrical fault | Stop resetting | Diagnose compressor/wiring |
| No display / wrong settings | Thermostat fault | Replace batteries, check mode | Repair wiring/EcoNet comms |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Regional rates and EcoNet parts can push these higher.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate line clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$300
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$1,800+ (often worth comparing against full unit replacement on older systems)

If your Rheem is under 5–10 years old, check warranty status — many parts (and compressors) carry a limited warranty that covers the part cost, leaving you paying labor only.

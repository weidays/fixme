## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single blink code — it's a symptom. Your Rheem air conditioner may run continuously without lowering the temperature, blow warm or room-temperature air, or short-cycle on and off. Depending on your model and installed control board, you may see a steady or flashing status light at the outdoor unit, but many Rheem condensers give no display at all for a general cooling failure.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, the goal here is to help you separate the cheap, safe DIY checks from the problems that require an EPA-certified technician. Refrigerant and sealed-system work is always a pro job.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — set to "on" instead of "auto," wrong mode, dead batteries, or a miswired/failing stat.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil (indoor)** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll often feel weak, warm airflow while ice builds on the coil.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure. The compressor or condenser fan won't start, or hums and stops.
5. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — blocked by dirt, grass clippings, or debris, so heat can't be rejected.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — poor cooling, longer run times, possible coil icing. Requires leak repair, not just a top-off.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse / bad contactor** — the outdoor unit gets no power or won't energize.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor unit runs but the fan doesn't spin, causing overheating and high-pressure shutdown.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; unit runs but produces no cooling, or trips on overload.
10. **Clogged condensate line triggering a safety float switch** — some installs cut cooling when the drain backs up.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or handle refrigerant.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter alone causes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto** (not "On"). Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure vents aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs. Closing too many registers strains the system.
- **Outdoor unit:** Check that the condenser isn't buried in leaves, grass, or debris. Gently clear anything blocking airflow. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance around it.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water pooling near the indoor unit or the system stopped cooling, a clogged drain may have tripped a float switch. Some systems clear once the line is unclogged.
- **Ice on the lines or coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, **turn the system off** and run the fan on "On" to thaw it (this can take several hours). Running a frozen system can damage the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable sequence. Knowing it helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and indoor coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Electrical test** — checks incoming voltage, contactor, and tests the **capacitor** with a meter (a cheap, common find).
4. **Motor checks** — verifies the condenser fan and compressor draw amps within spec.
5. **Gauge readings** — connects manifold gauges to read suction/head pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to judge refrigerant charge. Low readings point to a **leak**, which they'll locate with a detector or dye.
6. **Compressor test** — checks windings and start behavior if it won't run.

Ask for the measured temperature split, capacitor readings (microfarads), and refrigerant pressures. A legitimate refrigerant diagnosis includes finding the leak — not just adding refrigerant and leaving.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air, ice on coil | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter, turn off to thaw, run fan | Restore airflow, check charge, clear ice |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, transformer, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit runs, no fan spinning | Failed condenser fan motor | Clear debris, then call pro | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Long run times, poor cooling, some icing | Low refrigerant from leak | None | Locate leak, repair, evacuate & recharge |
| Runs constantly, no cool at all | Failed compressor | None | Test windings, replace compressor |
| Cooling stopped, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain line, add tablets | Clear drain, test/replace float switch |
| Set to Cool but nothing happens | Thermostat set wrong / dead batteries | Fix settings, replace batteries | Diagnose/replace thermostat, check wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included unless noted. Prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY, $0 labor)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $75–$250
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Blower motor (indoor):** $400–$800
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher if the leak is in the coil)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200, frequently credited toward the repair

If your unit is 12–15+ years old and facing a compressor or coil failure, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison before committing.

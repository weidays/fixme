## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on your Rheem — it's a symptom. Your air conditioner may be running but pushing warm or weak air, or the outdoor unit may not be starting at all. Because this covers several possible failures, there's no single flashing light or number tied to it on most Rheem/Ruud residential units.

Some Rheem systems paired with a communicating thermostat (like the EcoNet control) will display equipment status or fault messages that point you toward the cause. Standard 24V thermostats give you nothing but the symptom. Where your setup shows a code, note it — it will speed diagnosis.

This is rated **pro** because the most common non-trivial causes (refrigerant, electrical components, compressor) require a licensed HVAC technician and, in the case of refrigerant, EPA certification.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the coil. The single most common reason for weak or no cold air.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — ice blocks airflow. Caused by dirty filter, dirty coil, low refrigerant, or a weak blower.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "heat" or "fan"), or a miswired/failing thermostat.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler has lost power.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor won't start; you may hear a hum or clicking outside.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit won't close, so the condenser never starts.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces or eliminates cooling. Refrigerant does not get "used up"; low charge means a leak.
8. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil is caked with dirt/grass, so heat can't escape.
9. **Clogged condensate drain** — a full pan can trip a float safety switch and shut cooling down.
10. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the outdoor unit runs but doesn't cool, or won't run at all. The most expensive outcome.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all things you can do without tools or risk:

- **Air filter** — Pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Do this first, every time.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to **Cool** and the temperature is set below room temperature. Try lowering it 3–5°F. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Breaker** — Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker for the AC or air handler. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal an electrical fault.
- **Vents and registers** — Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Ice on the unit** — If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper line outside, switch the system to **Fan Only** for a few hours to thaw it, then run cooling with a clean filter.
- **Condensate drain** — If there's standing water in the drain pan or near the air handler, the float switch may have shut the system off. Clearing a visible clog can restore cooling. Pouring drain tablets into the access port helps prevent recurrence.
- **Outdoor unit** — Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.

Do **not** open the electrical compartment, top off refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical order, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verifies the symptom** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, coil, and blower operation before touching refrigerant.
3. **Tests electrical** — measures the capacitor's microfarads against its rating, checks the contactor, and confirms 24V control voltage.
4. **Reads refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to check whether the system is properly charged; low pressure with a temperature split confirms a leak or restriction.
5. **Leak search** — if the charge is low, a proper repair means finding and fixing the leak (electronic detector or dye), **not** just adding refrigerant.
6. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw and windings if the outdoor unit runs but won't cool.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add a couple pounds of refrigerant" without discussing a leak. That's a temporary patch, not a fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air from vents, blower runs | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter, thaw coil on fan-only | Clean coil, check charge and airflow |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor blower runs | Tripped breaker, contactor, or capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor or capacitor |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call a pro | Replace capacitor, verify compressor amps |
| Ice on coil or copper line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Filter change, thaw on fan-only | Find restriction or repair refrigerant leak |
| Weak cooling, long run times | Low refrigerant or dirty condenser | Clean around outdoor unit | Leak search, recharge, coil cleaning |
| Water near air handler, system off | Clogged condensate drain, float trip | Clear visible clog, add drain tablets | Flush drain, test float switch |
| No cooling, thermostat blank | Dead batteries or control power | Replace batteries | Diagnose transformer/wiring/thermostat |
| Unit runs constantly, never cools | Failed compressor | None — call a pro | Compressor test and replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included unless noted:

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries** — $5 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing** — $0 DIY with tablets; $100–$250 for a pro flush.
- **Run capacitor** — $150–$350 installed.
- **Contactor** — $150–$400 installed.
- **Condenser coil cleaning** — $150–$300.
- **Thermostat replacement** — $150–$450 depending on model.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge** — $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type.
- **Blower or condenser fan motor** — $400–$700 installed.
- **Compressor replacement** — $1,200–$1,800+ (often near the cost of a new outdoor unit; weigh replacement).

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far pricier), and whether your unit is still under Rheem's parts warranty — check your registration, as it may cover the part while you pay labor.

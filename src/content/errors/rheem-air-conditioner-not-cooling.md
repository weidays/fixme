## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Rheem air conditioner is running (or trying to run) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold enough to bring the house to the thermostat setpoint. On Rheem units with a diagnostic LED or a communicating thermostat, you may or may not see a stored fault; many cooling problems (like a slow refrigerant leak) never trip a code at all.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a $20 filter to a failed compressor, the goal of this page is to help you rule out the cheap, safe stuff yourself and understand what a tech should be checking before you approve an expensive repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection so the system can't dump heat outside.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks airflow and you get little to no cold air.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; always indicates a leak, never "topping off" as routine maintenance.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan won't start; you may hear humming outside.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit fails to close, so the compressor never runs.
7. **Thermostat problem or wrong setting** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/calibration fault.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit or air handler has no power.
9. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the unit runs but produces no meaningful cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no covers off, no electrical or refrigerant work.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak or no cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler or condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply vents are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance on all sides. Don't remove any panels.
- **Ice on the lines:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, **turn the system off at the thermostat** and let it thaw fully (several hours) before running again. Running an iced unit can damage the compressor.
- **Condensate:** If the unit shut off and there's a full drain pan or a float switch, a clogged condensate drain may be the cause. Clearing a visible clog or emptying the pan is fine; anything deeper needs a tech.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Rheem tech should work through this order — use it to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply and return air temperatures. A healthy system typically shows a 16–22°F drop across the coil.
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and evaporator coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Check electrical** — measure voltage at the contactor, test the capacitor's microfarad rating against its rating plate, and inspect the contactor contacts.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — read suction and head pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to confirm proper refrigerant charge.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, use electronic detector or dye to find the leak; a legitimate shop won't just recharge and leave.
6. **Compressor test** — check amp draw and windings if the compressor won't start or runs but doesn't pump.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "add a couple pounds of refrigerant" without doing a leak search or measuring subcooling.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower/coil if it recurs |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, let thaw, replace filter | Find restriction or leak, recharge |
| Outdoor unit humming, not starting | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor fan/compressor never runs | Bad contactor or no power | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, check wiring |
| Runs constantly, house won't cool | Low refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Leak search and repair, recharge |
| Outdoor coil caked with dirt | Dirty condenser | Gently rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil cleaning |
| No response from system | Thermostat or fuse | Check settings, batteries, breaker | Test low-voltage fuse and wiring |
| Runs but zero cooling | Failed compressor | None — call pro | Compressor diagnosis/replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and unit size.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Low-voltage fuse/wiring repair:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 (higher for R-410A refill or hard-to-reach leaks)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

If your unit uses older R-22 refrigerant, recharge costs are much higher and replacement is often the smarter long-term choice.

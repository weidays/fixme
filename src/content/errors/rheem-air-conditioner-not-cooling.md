## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom homeowners report when a Rheem air conditioner runs but the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint, or the air coming from the vents isn't cold. On basic Rheem/Ruud single-stage units (RA13–RA16) there is no diagnostic display, so you're troubleshooting by behavior. On variable-speed **Prestige** communicating systems (RA17/RA20 with an EcoNet control), a specific fault may appear on the thermostat or control board instead.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a $20 filter to a failed compressor, this page ranks the likely causes and shows you what a technician should check before quoting a big repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops output and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty condenser coil / blocked outdoor unit** — the outdoor coil can't reject heat, so cooling collapses on hot days.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan can't start; you may hear humming or see the fan not spinning.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — weak cooling, ice on the copper line, longer run times. This always means a leak, never "used up" refrigerant.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; airflow drops to a trickle and vents feel damp/cool at best.
6. **Failed contactor** — the outdoor unit won't energize; often shows pitted or stuck contacts.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit dead while the fan/thermostat still act normally.
8. **Thermostat or wiring fault** — bad batteries, wrong mode, or a broken 24V wire preventing a cooling call.
9. **Failed compressor** — least common but most expensive; unit hums, trips breaker, or won't start even with a good capacitor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or refrigerant handling:

- **Air filter:** Replace it if it's gray or clogged. A dirty filter alone can stop cooling and freeze the coil.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor AC breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or drapes.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (power off) if it's visibly dirty.
- **Condensate drain:** If the unit has a float switch and it's tripped from a clogged drain, cooling may shut off. Clear a visibly clogged drain line and check for standing water in the pan.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** and the fan to **On** for a few hours to thaw before calling. Never chip ice off.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass the float switch or door interlocks, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap to expensive, and you can sanity-check the visit against this order:

1. **Confirms the complaint** — checks setpoint vs. actual temperature and measures the temperature split across the indoor coil (typically ~14–20°F on a healthy system).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Reads the outdoor unit** — verifies 24V call at the contactor, tests the capacitor with a meter, and checks the contactor for pitting.
4. **Takes refrigerant readings** — connects gauges to measure pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to judge charge. Low readings point to a leak.
5. **Leak search if charge is low** — uses an electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test. A responsible tech finds the leak before adding refrigerant.
6. **Compressor test** — checks windings and amp draw if the compressor won't start with a proven-good capacitor.
7. **On Prestige/EcoNet units** — pulls stored fault codes from the communicating control to confirm the story.

Be cautious of any quote to "just add refrigerant" without a leak search — that's a temporary fix on a sealed system.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm vents | Dirty filter / blocked return | Replace filter, open vents | Clean blower & coil |
| Ice on copper line or coil | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — leave off | Replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker / bad contactor / blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor/fuse, find cause |
| Unit short-cycles, breaker trips | Failed compressor or hard-start issue | Stop resetting | Test compressor, quote replacement |
| No cooling, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / control fault | Replace batteries | Test 24V, repair wiring |
| Warm air on hottest days only | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse outside of coil | Deep-clean coil, check charge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Low-voltage fuse / minor wiring:** $100–$250
- **Condenser or evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,800 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A units cost more; older R-22 is very expensive)
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 — often the point where replacing the whole outdoor unit makes more sense, especially on older systems

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type, and whether your unit is still under Rheem's parts warranty (labor is typically not covered).

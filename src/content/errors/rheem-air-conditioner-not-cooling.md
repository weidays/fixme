## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a fault code — most Rheem split-system air conditioners don't display a specific code for it. Instead you'll notice the thermostat calling for cooling while air from the vents stays warm, weak, or nonexistent. On systems paired with a Rheem furnace or air handler with a diagnostic board, you may see a flashing status LED, but the AC condenser itself typically has no readout.

The problem falls into three broad buckets: **airflow problems** (the system can't move air across the coils), **electrical/component failures** (a capacitor, contactor, or compressor won't run), and **refrigerant problems** (a leak or restriction has left the system unable to absorb heat). Figuring out which bucket you're in is the first step, and some of that you can do safely yourself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged or dirty air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. Ice on the coil or the copper line at the air handler stops cooling entirely.
3. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — set to "fan on" instead of "auto/cool," dead batteries, or a miscalibrated stat.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — the outdoor fan and/or compressor hum but won't start. Very common on units 5+ years old.
5. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — dirt, grass, or cottonwood blocks heat rejection, so the system runs but barely cools.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — gradual loss of cooling over weeks, possibly with ice on the outdoor line. Always indicates a leak.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit is dead while the indoor fan runs.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit hums, trips the breaker, or does nothing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no panels off the electrical or refrigerant side.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the target is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets can destroy a compressor.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow. Open all vents.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut the system down. If you have accessible cleaning tablets and a visible drain line, keep it clear.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for ice on the copper lines, heavy debris in the coil fins, or a fan that isn't spinning. **Turn the system off if you see ice** and let it thaw — running iced is a pro-only diagnosis.

If none of these solve it, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable process — you can use this to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the call** — verifies the thermostat is calling for cool and 24V is reaching the outdoor contactor.
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects the filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Reads electrical values** — tests the capacitor's microfarad rating, checks the contactor for pitting, and measures compressor amp draw.
4. **Connects refrigerant gauges** — measures suction and liquid pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct.
5. **Leak search if low** — uses electronic detectors or dye to find the leak rather than just topping off refrigerant (topping off without a leak search is a red flag).
6. **Compressor test** — if the unit hums and trips, checks windings and start components before condemning the compressor.

Expect a diagnostic fee of **$75–$200**, often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vents blow warm air, unit runs | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Ice on indoor coil or copper line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, let thaw, swap filter | Diagnose airflow/charge, leak search |
| Outdoor fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit completely dead | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace fuse/contactor, find cause |
| Weak cooling, hot outdoor unit | Dirty condenser coil | Gently rinse fins with hose | Deep coil clean, verify charge |
| Unit hums then trips breaker | Failing compressor or start components | Stop resetting, call pro | Test windings, replace parts/compressor |
| No cold air, thermostat blank | Dead thermostat batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Repair thermostat/wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / recalibration:** $0–$150
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$300
- **Low-voltage fuse / minor wiring:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often better to replace the condenser or system if out of warranty)

Rheem AC compressors typically carry a **10-year parts warranty** if the unit was registered within 60 days of install — always ask the tech to check your serial number before condemning the compressor, as it can cut the part cost dramatically.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Rheem air conditioner runs but the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents feels warm. On Rheem systems with an **EcoNet communicating control**, you may also see a related fault or alert on the thermostat or in the EcoNet app; on standard **RA-series single- and two-stage units**, there's no display, so you're diagnosing by behavior.

Because "not cooling" has many possible root causes — from a clogged filter to a dead compressor — this page walks from the cheapest, most likely issues to the ones that require a licensed HVAC technician. Anything involving refrigerant, sealed-system components, or high-voltage compressor circuits is **pro-only**.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," wrong schedule, or dead batteries.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer, so vents blow warm.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** (outdoor unit) — a caked coil can't reject heat, killing cooling capacity.
5. **Failed run/dual capacitor** — the outdoor fan or compressor won't start; the unit hums or does nothing.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit or air handler has no power.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — long run times, warm air, and ice; always indicates a leak, not "topping off."
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
9. **Clogged condensate drain / float switch tripped** — a safety switch can cut cooling to prevent overflow.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive; outdoor unit hums, trips breaker, or is dead.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Filter:** Pull the air filter. If it's gray and clogged, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls and prevents coil freeze-ups.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler **and** the outdoor condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** but leave the fan running to thaw it (can take a few hours). Replace the filter before restarting.
- **Condensate drain:** If your unit has a float switch and cooling stopped, a full drain pan may have tripped it. Clearing a visible clog and emptying the pan is safe; anything deeper is a service call.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work systematically so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the complaint** — measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Verify power and controls** — test the contactor, capacitor, and low-voltage (24V) fuse with a meter.
4. **Inspect the condenser** — clean the coil, check the fan motor and compressor start-up.
5. **Read the EcoNet board** (if equipped) — pull any stored alerts or fault history from the communicating control.
6. **Connect gauges** — measure suction/head pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge refrigerant charge.
7. **Leak search** (if charge is low) — with electronic detector or dye, because a low charge always means a leak.

Ask for the measured temperature split, capacitor microfarad readings, and pressure numbers. A good tech will show these rather than jump straight to "you need a new compressor."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm vents | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Clean blower/coil, verify airflow |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, run fan to thaw, replace filter | Find airflow/charge root cause |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None (high voltage) | Test and replace dual run capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker/contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, check wiring |
| Long runtimes, warm air | Low refrigerant leak | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Cooling stops, water near unit | Clogged drain/float switch | Clear visible clog, empty pan | Clear line, test float switch |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor/electrical fault | Stop resetting | Diagnose compressor and circuit |
| Dirty outdoor coil, poor cooling | Caked condenser | Gently rinse fins (power off) | Deep coil clean, capacity check |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included where noted:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries / setting fix:** $0–$15 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 service call.
- **Dual run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$300.
- **Low-voltage fuse / minor wiring:** $100–$300.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700 installed.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800–$2,500+ installed — often near the cost of a new condenser, so weigh replacement if the unit is aging or out of warranty.

Rheem covers many parts under a **10-year limited warranty** if the unit was registered; confirm coverage with your installer before approving major parts.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific Rheem fault code — it's a symptom. Your Rheem or Ruud air conditioner may be running, blowing air, and showing no error light, yet the air coming from your vents isn't cold. On systems with a communicating comfort-control board (like some Prestige models), you may see a fault indication, but many Rheem single-stage units have no display at all.

The job of your AC is to move heat from inside your home to the outdoors. "Not cooling" means that heat-transfer process has broken down somewhere: airflow, refrigerant, electrical, or the compressor itself. This page walks through the likely causes in order, the checks you can safely do, and what a technician will look at — so you can tell a fair quote from an inflated one.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops capacity and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — matted with grass, dust, or cottonwood, the unit can't reject heat outdoors.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common electrical failure. The fan or compressor may hum, struggle to start, or not run at all.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — you'll get weak cooling, ice on the copper lines, or warm air. Requires a certified tech.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low charge; blocks cooling until it thaws.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown contactor** — the outdoor unit gets no power, so only the indoor fan runs (warm air).
7. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — wrong setting, dead batteries, or a bad C-wire.
8. **Failed compressor** — the worst case. Expensive, and often a repair-vs-replace decision.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening a sealed system or touching wiring.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak or no cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (there are often two — indoor air handler and outdoor condenser). Reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets can damage equipment.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (power off) if it's visibly dirty.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, switch the system to FAN ONLY for 2–4 hours to thaw it, then run cooling again with a clean filter.
- **Condensate:** Check that the drain pan isn't overflowing and the drain line isn't clogged; a full pan can trip a float switch and shut off cooling.

Do **not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** and checks thermostat calls, filter, and airflow.
2. **Measures the temperature split** (return vs. supply air) — a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F. A low split points to charge or airflow issues.
3. **Checks capacitor and contactor** with a meter — a weak run capacitor is a cheap, common find.
4. **Inspects the condenser coil and fan motor** for cleanliness and operation.
5. **Connects refrigerant gauges** and reads pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low readings mean a leak — they should then perform a leak search, not just top off.
6. **Tests the compressor** windings and amp draw if it isn't starting or running properly.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to add refrigerant without first finding a leak, or who quotes a compressor without measuring pressures and electrical values.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents | Check blower, ductwork |
| Ice on copper lines or coil | Low airflow or low charge | Fan-only to thaw, new filter | Measure charge, find leak |
| Outdoor unit not running | Tripped breaker or bad capacitor/contactor | Reset breaker once | Test & replace capacitor/contactor |
| Humming, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor |
| Cooling fades over weeks | Refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor coil packed with debris | Dirty condenser | Rinse exterior with hose (power off) | Deep coil cleaning |
| Runs constantly, never cold | Low charge or failing compressor | None — call pro | Full pressure/electrical diagnosis |
| Thermostat blank or wrong mode | Dead batteries / setting | Replace batteries, set to COOL | Check C-wire, control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and unit age):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 — DIY.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250 professional; near $0 for a light DIY rinse.
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500+ depending on leak location (a leaking evaporator coil is at the high end).
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500 — often the point where replacing the whole system is worth considering, especially on units over 10–12 years old or using R-22.

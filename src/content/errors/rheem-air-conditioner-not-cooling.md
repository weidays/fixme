## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on Rheem air conditioners — it's a symptom. Your Rheem or Ruud system may be running (the outdoor fan spins, the indoor blower moves air) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On some Rheem models with EcoNet controls or a diagnostic board, you may also see a fault light or an app alert, but many standard units give no code at all — just warm air.

Cooling depends on four things working together: adequate airflow, a running compressor, the correct refrigerant charge, and a clean coil at both ends. A failure in any one of these produces the same complaint. Because refrigerant and compressor issues dominate the serious cases, we've marked this **pro** severity — but several likely causes are safe for you to check first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow freezes the indoor coil, which then blocks airflow entirely.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bulged or dead dual capacitor stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting. Very common on units 5+ years old.
4. **Iced-over evaporator coil** — a result of low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil turns to a block of ice and no cold air passes.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit dead while the indoor blower runs.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck and won't pass power to the compressor.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; a slow decline over weeks. Always indicates a leak, never "used up" refrigerant.
8. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a bad wire.
9. **Failed compressor** — the least common but most expensive; the outdoor unit hums or trips but won't run.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. If none of them fix it, stop and call a licensed HVAC tech.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and look through it toward a light. If it's gray and clogged, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 cause of "not cooling."
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the fan is on **AUTO**, and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (there are often two — one for the air handler, one for the condenser). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, leave it off and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the condenser. Make sure nothing is packed against the fins. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (never a pressure washer) with the unit **off**.
- **Ice check:** Look at the refrigerant lines and indoor coil. If you see ice, turn the system to **OFF** but leave the fan set to **ON** for a few hours to thaw it, then check the filter. Persistent icing needs a technician.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or a tripped float switch can shut down cooling. If your pan is full, that's a drain clog — worth mentioning to your tech.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, remove the electrical access panel to poke at the capacitor, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and coil for icing or blockage.
3. **Electrical checks** — tests the capacitor (µF reading vs. rating), contactor, and low-voltage fuse with a meter.
4. **Amp draw** — measures compressor and fan motor current against the nameplate to spot a failing motor or compressor.
5. **Refrigerant gauges** — connects manifold gauges to read suction and head pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling. This tells them if the charge is correct.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, they'll use an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before adding refrigerant. A reputable tech will not just "top it off" without addressing the leak.

A good diagnosis is $90–$200 and should end with a written explanation, not just a parts list.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vents blow warm, unit runs | Dirty filter / airflow block | Replace filter, open vents | Check blower, coil, charge |
| Outdoor unit dead, blower runs | Tripped breaker / blown fuse / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace fuse/contactor, find cause |
| Outdoor fan runs, compressor hums | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, fan on, thaw, swap filter | Diagnose airflow/charge, fix leak |
| Cooling slowly declined over weeks | Refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Poor cooling on hot days only | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil (unit off) | Professional coil cleaning |
| Nothing cools, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / thermostat fault | Replace batteries | Test thermostat and wiring |
| Unit hums then trips breaker | Failing compressor | Do not repeat resets | Compressor test / replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 replaced
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 replaced
- **Low-voltage fuse:** $75–$150
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A costs more than older systems)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $2,000–$3,000+ — often within the range where replacing the whole condenser or system makes more sense, especially if out of warranty

Rheem covers many parts under a 10-year limited registered warranty, which can dramatically cut the cost of a compressor or coil — labor is usually not included. Ask your tech to check your model and serial for coverage.

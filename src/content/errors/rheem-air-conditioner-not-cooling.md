## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Your Rheem air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, thermostat calling) but the air coming from your vents is warm or only mildly cool, or the system won't reach the set temperature. On most Rheem split systems there's no error display in the condenser; you diagnose by observation and measurement.

If your **indoor blower and outdoor unit are both running** but there's no cold air, the problem is usually airflow, refrigerant, or the compressor circuit. If the **outdoor unit won't start at all**, suspect power, the contactor, or the capacitor. Inverter-driven Rheem models (Endeavor, some Prestige) do log fault codes on the control board — if yours has a diagnostic light or app fault, note it, because that changes the diagnosis.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil. The single most common "not cooling" cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — a coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so the system runs but barely cools.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bulged or dead capacitor stops the compressor or fan motor from starting; you may hear humming.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — causes weak cooling, warm air, ice on the small copper line, or hissing.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the condenser won't close, so the outdoor unit stays dead.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; blocks cooling until it thaws.
8. **Bad thermostat, dead batteries, or wiring fault** — the system never gets a proper cooling call.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; unit hums or trips the breaker and produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening a sealed system or bypassing a switch.

- **Filter:** Pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Run the system 30 minutes and recheck airflow.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and at least 5°F below room temp. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm it isn't in "fan only" mode.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (no pressure washer) with the power off.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch — a clogged drain can shut down cooling on many systems.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or coil, turn the system to OFF (fan can stay ON) and let it thaw fully before running again.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. Those are pro or code-restricted tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheapest to most expensive, and their steps should match the causes above:

1. **Confirms the call** at the thermostat and verifies 24V reaches the condenser contactor.
2. **Checks power** at the disconnect and measures the **run capacitor** with a meter (a common $150–$300 fix).
3. **Measures airflow and temperature split** — return vs. supply air should drop roughly 15–20°F. A weak split points to airflow or charge problems.
4. **Reads refrigerant pressures** with gauges and checks superheat/subcooling. Low pressure indicates undercharge — and they should then **leak-search**, not just top it off.
5. **Inspects the coils** for dirt, damage, and freezing.
6. **Tests the compressor** windings and amp draw if the unit hums or trips.
7. On **inverter Rheem models**, pulls board fault codes to pinpoint drive or sensor faults.

If a tech recommends adding refrigerant without finding the leak, or quotes a compressor without testing capacitor and windings first, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Dirty filter | Replace filter, run 30 min | Verify airflow, check blower |
| Runs but barely cools | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil (power off) | Deep coil cleaning |
| Outdoor unit dead, blower runs | Tripped breaker | Reset breaker once | Find cause of trip, test circuit |
| Humming, won't start | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Warm air, ice on lines, hissing | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off, thaw coil | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit silent, no hum | Failed contactor | Verify power/breaker | Test 24V, replace contactor |
| Ice on indoor coil | Low airflow or low charge | Filter, thaw, then call | Diagnose airflow vs. charge |
| Never calls for cooling | Thermostat/batteries | Replace batteries, reset | Test thermostat wiring |
| Hums then trips breaker | Failing compressor | Stop resetting | Amp/winding test, replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Warranty may cover parts on newer Rheem units (typically 10 years registered), but labor is usually separate.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $100–$350
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. newer blends)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often makes replacing an older unit worth considering)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400

If your unit uses R-22 (older Rheem models), refrigerant is expensive and increasingly scarce — factor that into repair-vs-replace decisions.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on a Rheem air conditioner — it's a symptom. Most Rheem/Ruud split systems and packaged units don't display an alphanumeric error for poor cooling the way a furnace flashes a code. Instead, you notice the air handler is running, the thermostat is calling for cool, but the house won't come down to setpoint.

The underlying cause falls into a few buckets: **restricted airflow** (dirty filter or coil), an **electrical failure** in the outdoor unit (capacitor, contactor), a **refrigerant problem** (a leak has lowered the charge), or a **compressor** that has stopped pumping. Some higher-end Rheem units with communicating controls (Prestige/EcoNet) *will* log a diagnostic message in the app or thermostat — check there first if you have one.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, ices the indoor coil, and kills cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so cooling drops off.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common outdoor-unit failure; the compressor and/or fan won't start or struggles.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces capacity; often accompanied by ice on the copper lines or hissing.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown contactor** — the outdoor unit gets no power, so the indoor fan just circulates warm air.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; the system "runs" but blows warm or weak air.
7. **Thermostat fault or dead batteries** — not calling for cool correctly, or set to the wrong mode.
8. **Failed compressor** — least common but most expensive; the unit hums or trips the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker** — check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Rheem systems typically have two separate breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — don't keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers** — make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser and gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (power OFF). Give at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate** — check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch (a full pan can shut the system down on some setups). Empty standing water if you can reach it safely.
- **Ice** — if you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** for a few hours to thaw. Running an iced unit can damage the compressor.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, remove electrical panels on the condenser, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheapest to most expensive, and their steps should match your quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirm the thermostat is signaling and 24V is reaching the outdoor contactor.
2. **Check the contactor and capacitor** — with power removed, they test the run capacitor with a meter and inspect the contactor for pitting.
3. **Measure amperage** — an amp clamp on the compressor and fan tells them if the compressor is drawing correctly, locked, or not starting.
4. **Take temperature split** — measuring return vs. supply air temperature (should be roughly 15–20°F difference) reveals whether the system is actually cooling.
5. **Gauge the refrigerant** — connecting gauges to check pressures and superheat/subcooling shows if the charge is low, indicating a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if low, they use electronic detectors or dye to find the leak before recharging.
7. **Inspect coils and airflow** — confirm the evaporator and condenser are clean and the blower is moving air.

Ask for the temperature split reading and the capacitor test result — those two numbers quickly justify or challenge a repair recommendation.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor fan runs, weak/warm air | Dirty filter or iced coil | Replace filter; thaw coil (fan ON, cooling OFF) | Correct airflow, check charge |
| Outdoor unit silent, no power | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, find cause of trip |
| Outdoor fan runs, compressor won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Thaw unit, replace filter | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| House warm, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / control fault | Replace batteries | Diagnose wiring/control board |
| Condenser coil caked with debris | Poor heat rejection | Rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil cleaning |
| Compressor hums, breaker trips | Failed/locked compressor | Stop resetting; call pro | Replace compressor or unit |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,000+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older systems)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500 — often the point where replacing the whole condenser or system makes more financial sense, especially on units over 10 years old.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200, often credited toward the repair.

If the compressor has failed on a unit past 10–12 years, get a quote for a full system replacement to compare — repair costs on aging equipment frequently don't pay off.

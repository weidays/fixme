## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom rather than a specific Rheem fault code — most Rheem air conditioners do not display a numeric code for this. Instead, homeowners notice the system runs but the house stays warm, or the outdoor unit isn't turning on at all.

On Rheem systems with a **communicating control board** (some Prestige and Endeavor models), the indoor board or a compatible thermostat may show a fault or status message. On **standard single-stage units** with a basic 24V contactor, there is no display — you diagnose by behavior and measurement.

Because cooling depends on the thermostat, indoor blower, outdoor condenser, and a sealed refrigerant circuit all working together, "not cooling" has many possible causes ranging from a $0 filter swap to a failed compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, freezes the indoor coil, and kills cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, leaves, and dust block heat rejection, so the system runs but can't cool.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "on" vs "auto"), or a miscalibrated/failed thermostat.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit has its own breaker; if it trips, the indoor fan may still blow warm air.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure that prevents the compressor or fan motor from starting; you may hear a hum or clicking.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the compressor burns or sticks, so the condenser won't engage.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the lines, or a frozen coil. Requires a licensed tech.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks all cooling.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor runs hot and shuts down on high pressure.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; often the end result of ignored problems above.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform. Do not open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

- **Filter:** Pull the air filter. If it's gray or clogged, replace it and give the system 30–60 minutes to recover.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the temperature is below room temp. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Set the fan to AUTO, not ON.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full condensate pan or a tripped float switch — a clogged drain can shut the system down. Empty standing water and clear a visible clog.
- **Outdoor unit:** Turn the system off and gently clear leaves, grass, and debris from around and on the condenser fins. Confirm the outdoor fan is spinning when cooling is called for.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to FAN ONLY for a few hours to thaw it, then run cooling again. If it re-freezes, call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech should work through a logical sequence — this helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirm 24V is reaching the contactor.
2. **Measure airflow and filter/coil condition** to rule out the cheap causes first.
3. **Check the run capacitor** with a meter and inspect the contactor for pitting or sticking.
4. **Read superheat and subcooling** using refrigerant gauges to determine if the charge is correct — this is the key diagnostic for sealed-system problems.
5. **Leak-search** with electronic detectors or dye if refrigerant is low. A reputable tech will not "just top it off" without finding the leak.
6. **Test the compressor and condenser fan motor** for proper amp draw and windings.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace compressor" without documenting superheat/subcooling readings or a leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Clean coils, check blower |
| Outdoor unit off, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, test circuit |
| Humming, fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — power hazard | Replace capacitor, verify motor |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Fan-only to thaw, replace filter | Find leak, check charge |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Clean debris outdoors | Coil clean, superheat/subcooling test |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Failed compressor or capacitor | None | Test compressor, capacitor |
| Clicks and shuts off | Contactor or capacitor fault | None | Diagnose control circuit |
| System shuts down, water present | Full condensate pan / float switch | Clear drain, empty pan | Clear line, check pump/switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Actual prices vary by region and model.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$3,000+ (often worth comparing to full system replacement if out of warranty)

Many Rheem units carry a 10-year parts limited warranty if registered — confirm your model and serial before paying for parts.

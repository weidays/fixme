## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on Rheem air conditioners — it's a symptom. Your Rheem or Ruud unit may be running, blowing air, and showing no obvious error, yet the air coming from the vents isn't cold or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

Because central ACs are a system, "not cooling" can originate at the thermostat, the indoor air handler/furnace, the refrigerant circuit, or the outdoor condenser. Some newer Rheem systems with communicating controls (e.g. EcoNet-enabled) will display a fault or flash a status light at the control board when a specific component fails. Most single-stage Classic-series units give no code at all — you diagnose by observation.

This page covers the whole-system "warm air" problem. If your unit is flashing a light instead, see the related page below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, causing weak cooling and eventually a frozen evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode/setting, dead batteries, or a wiring/calibration fault so the system doesn't call for cooling correctly.
3. **Dirty condenser coil or blocked outdoor unit** — dirt, grass, and debris prevent the outdoor unit from rejecting heat.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start, so no cooling.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharged systems can't absorb heat. This always means a leak, not "AC that ran low."
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; ice blocks the coil so no cold air reaches the vents.
7. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the condenser never energizes.
8. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive cause; the compressor is electrically or mechanically dead.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Set the fan to AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak cooling and iced coils.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers/disconnect. If one has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets are unsafe.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** With power off at the disconnect, clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser and gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (low pressure, top to bottom).
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and shut cooling off. Check the drain line and pan for standing water; drain tablets help keep it clear.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or coil, turn the system to OFF and set the fan to ON to thaw it (can take a few hours). Running a frozen unit damages the compressor.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, remove electrical panels on the condenser, or attempt to test/replace a capacitor unless you're trained — capacitors hold a dangerous charge even with power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech typically works the system end to end:

1. **Verifies the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirms low-voltage (24V) signal reaching the outdoor unit.
2. **Checks airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition — because low airflow mimics and causes many "not cooling" complaints.
3. **Measures electrical** — tests the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor for pitting, and confirms the compressor and fan motor draw correct amperage.
4. **Connects refrigerant gauges** and takes superheat/subcooling readings to determine if the charge is correct, low, or overcharged.
5. **Leak-searches** (electronic detector, dye, or bubbles) if the charge is low, since adding refrigerant without fixing the leak is a temporary and non-compliant fix.
6. **Inspects the compressor** — resistance/continuity tests to confirm whether it's the actual failure.

A trustworthy quote should identify the specific failed part and, if refrigerant is low, should include finding the leak — not just a "top-off."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Check blower and duct static |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, correct charge |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Failed run capacitor | None | Test and replace capacitor |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Bad start/run capacitor | None | Replace capacitor, check windings |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| House never reaches setpoint | Low refrigerant (leak) | None | Leak search and repair, recharge |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Short in compressor or wiring | Stop, leave off | Diagnose compressor/electrical |
| No cooling, water near air handler | Clogged condensate/float switch | Clear drain, tablets | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area, model, and refrigerant type affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (higher if the leak is in the coil)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,000+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

Note: R-410A systems and older R-22 systems differ significantly in refrigerant cost; a tech should tell you which you have and why it matters.

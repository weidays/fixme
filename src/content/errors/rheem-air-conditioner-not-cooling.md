## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single blink code on Rheem equipment — it's the symptom you're seeing: the system runs (or tries to) but the air coming out of your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches setpoint. Unlike a hard fault, most Rheem thermostats and control boards won't flash a specific number for this. Instead, you have to work backward from what the unit is actually doing.

There are two broad buckets:

- **Airflow / cold-side problems** — dirty filter, iced-over indoor coil, blocked returns. Air moves but can't absorb heat.
- **Heat-rejection / mechanical problems** — the outdoor unit isn't running, the fan or compressor won't start (bad capacitor or contactor), the condenser coil is caked in dirt, or the system is low on refrigerant from a leak.

If your Rheem is a heat pump and the *outdoor* unit is the concern, also see the related flashing-light guide below — heat pumps use fault indicators that a straight AC does not.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and often leads to a frozen evaporator. The single most common "no cool" call.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant. You'll see ice on the copper line or indoor coil; airflow drops to almost nothing.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor condenser has its own breaker; if it's off, only the indoor fan runs.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — the outdoor fan or compressor hums, buzzes, or won't start. Very common on units 6+ years old.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't dump heat, so cooling weakens and pressures climb.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit gets pitted and won't pull in; outdoor unit stays dead.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — weak cooling, long run times, sometimes ice. Refrigerant doesn't "run out" — a leak caused it.
8. **Thermostat fault or wiring** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a loose C-wire on communicating Rheem thermostats.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit runs the fan but never actually cools.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the checks any homeowner can safely do:

- **Filter** — pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Then wait — a frozen coil needs the system off (fan-only mode) for a few hours to thaw before it'll cool again.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to **Cool**, temperature is below room temp, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's a battery model and the screen is dim or dead.
- **Breakers** — check the main panel for the **indoor air handler/furnace** and the separate **outdoor condenser** breaker. If one is tripped, flip it fully off, then on. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns** — make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser so it can breathe. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (power off) if they're dirty.
- **Condensate drain** — a full drain pan or clogged line can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water; a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain outlet often clears it.

Do **not** open the electrical compartments, discharge or replace capacitors without proper knowledge, add refrigerant, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting down.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path — you can sanity-check their quote against this:

1. **Confirms the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects the filter, coil, and blower for the frozen-coil or restriction pattern.
3. **Tests the outdoor unit** — verifies the contactor pulls in, and tests the run capacitor with a meter (a weak or bulged cap is a fast, cheap find).
4. **Reads refrigerant pressures** — hooks up gauges to see if the charge is low; low pressure points to a leak, and they'll use a leak detector or dye to locate it.
5. **Checks electrical** — measures amp draw on the compressor and fan to spot a failing motor or a locked compressor.
6. **Inspects the board/thermostat** — confirms the call for cooling is reaching the outdoor unit through the low-voltage wiring.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without a capacitor test and a leak search first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, ice on lines | Frozen coil / dirty filter | Replace filter, run fan-only to thaw | Find airflow or charge cause |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Bad compressor or low charge | Check filter, then call | Test amps, pressures, compressor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call | Test and replace capacitor |
| Warm air, long run times | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call | Locate leak, repair, recharge |
| Nothing runs at all | Dead thermostat or fuse | Replace batteries, check mode | Replace low-voltage fuse, wiring |
| Cools poorly on hot days only | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse fins, clear debris | Deep coil cleaning, check charge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and unit access):

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 DIY.
- **Condensate drain clearing** — $0 DIY to $75–$200 for a service call.
- **Run/dual capacitor** — $150–$400 replaced.
- **Contactor** — $150–$350 replaced.
- **Condenser coil cleaning** — $150–$400.
- **Low-voltage fuse / thermostat wiring** — $100–$300.
- **Thermostat replacement** — $150–$500 depending on model.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge** — $200–$1,500+ depending on leak location; evaporator coil leaks are the pricey end.
- **Compressor replacement** — $1,200–$1,800+; on older units, this is often the "repair vs. replace" tipping point.
- **Diagnostic/service call** — typically $75–$180, often credited toward the repair.

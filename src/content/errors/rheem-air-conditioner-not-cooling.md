## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a numeric fault code — it's the most common complaint homeowners have about a Rheem or Ruud air conditioner. The system may be running (compressor and fans on) but blowing warm or room-temperature air, or it may not turn on at all when the thermostat calls for cooling.

Because there's no single blink code for this, diagnosis is about narrowing down the cause: airflow, electrical, refrigerant, or control. On EcoNet-equipped Rheem systems, the thermostat or app may show a specific fault or communication message — if it does, look that code up directly, as it will point you faster than the general "not cooling" path.

This is rated **pro severity** because the most common non-airflow causes (capacitors, contactors, refrigerant, compressors) involve high-voltage components or EPA-regulated refrigerant that homeowners should not service.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — Restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil. The single most common and most fixable cause.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — Grass, cottonwood, and dust block heat rejection, so the system runs but can't cool well.
3. **Thermostat problem** — Dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "on" vs "auto"), or a miswired/failed thermostat.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — Outdoor disconnect or panel breaker tripped; the fan may run indoors while the condenser is dead outside.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — A very common failure. The compressor or fan hums, struggles to start, or won't start at all.
6. **Failed contactor** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit; pitted or stuck contacts stop the compressor from energizing.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Causes weak cooling, ice on the lines, and long run times. Always indicates a leak, never "normal use."
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Result of #1 or #7; blocks airflow completely.
9. **Failed compressor** — The most expensive cause; the outdoor unit hums or trips the breaker but won't cool.
10. **Blower motor or control board fault** — Less common; the indoor unit doesn't move enough (or any) air.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If none of them fixes the problem, stop and call a licensed HVAC technician.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Set mode to **Cool** and temperature several degrees below room temp. Set the fan to **Auto**, not On. Replace batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop — do not keep resetting it, as this signals an electrical fault.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside coil fins with a garden hose (low pressure, top to bottom) with power off at the disconnect.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch (some systems shut off cooling when the pan fills). Clear standing water and a clogged drain line if accessible.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the thermostat to **Off**, set fan to **On** to thaw it, and replace the filter. If it refreezes, call a pro — that points to low refrigerant.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician follows a logical sequence so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — Measures supply and return air temperatures (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — Inspects filter, blower, and coils for restriction or ice.
3. **Electrical checks** — Tests the capacitor with a meter (not by eye), checks the contactor, and confirms voltage at the disconnect and to the compressor.
4. **Refrigerant** — Connects gauges to read pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low readings mean a leak search, not a top-off.
5. **Compressor and motor** — Tests compressor windings and amp draw, and checks fan motors.
6. **Controls/EcoNet** — On communicating systems, reads stored fault codes at the board or app.

Ask which of these steps they performed. A quote for refrigerant without a leak search, or a compressor replacement without electrical and capacitor testing first, is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Verify airflow, check coil |
| System runs, poor cooling | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse outside coil, clear debris | Deep coil clean, check charge |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker / contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, find cause |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — high voltage | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines, long run times | Low refrigerant (leak) | Thaw coil, replace filter | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| No air from vents | Blower motor / board fault | Check breaker & filter | Test blower, board, wiring |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor / electrical fault | Stop resetting; call pro | Test compressor, wiring |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries / wiring | Replace batteries | Test C-wire, replace stat |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Regional rates and model differences apply.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor:** $400–$800
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (older R-22 systems cost far more)
- **Control board:** $300–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement if out of warranty)

Rheem compressors and many parts carry a limited parts warranty if the unit is registered — confirm your coverage before paying for parts.

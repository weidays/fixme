## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you're living with when your Rheem or Ruud air conditioner runs but the house stays warm. Rheem central AC condensers don't have a digital display like a furnace board, so there's no numeric code to read. Instead you diagnose by **airflow, refrigerant behavior, and electrical components**.

The problem falls into a few buckets: the system isn't moving enough air, the outdoor unit isn't running, the refrigerant charge is low, or a component (capacitor, contactor, compressor) has failed. Some of these are homeowner-fixable; several require a licensed HVAC tech and are why this page is rated **pro**.

If your indoor fan is fine but the outdoor unit is silent, or you hear a hum-and-click every few minutes, skip to the electrical causes below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the most common cause. Restricted airflow drops capacity and can freeze the evaporator coil solid, at which point no cold air moves at all.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant. You'll see ice on the copper lines or hear the blower running with barely any air.
3. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — a clogged condenser can't reject heat, so the system runs constantly and cools poorly, especially on hot afternoons.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The outdoor fan or compressor won't start; you may hear humming or clicking. This is a defined tech job.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — never "used up" normally. Low charge means a leak that must be located and repaired, then recharged. Warm air and long run times are typical.
6. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit can pit or stick, leaving the condenser dead or chattering.
7. **Thermostat or control wiring fault** — a miswired, dead, or misconfigured thermostat may never call for cooling. Varies by thermostat model.
8. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome. The unit hums but the compressor won't run, or trips the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not** open the condenser, touch refrigerant lines, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of poor cooling and coil freeze-ups.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm it isn't in "fan on" or "hold" from an old schedule.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor air handler/furnace breaker and the **separate outdoor AC breaker**. Reset a tripped breaker once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Closing too many rooms starves airflow.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around and against the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut cooling down. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear the line if you know how; drain tablets help prevent buildup.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice, turn the thermostat to OFF (or fan-only) and let it thaw fully — several hours — before running cooling again. Then replace the filter.

If cooling doesn't return after a fresh filter, a thawed coil, and confirmed power, the remaining causes need a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a predictable sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verifies the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirms 24V is reaching the outdoor contactor.
2. **Checks airflow** — filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil condition.
3. **Measures the capacitor** with a meter (microfarad reading vs. rated value) and inspects the contactor for pitting or chatter.
4. **Connects refrigerant gauges** and takes temperatures to calculate **superheat and subcooling** — this tells them whether the charge is correct, low (leak), or overcharged.
5. **Leak search** if charge is low — using electronic detectors or dye. A recharge without finding the leak is a red flag; the refrigerant will just leak out again.
6. **Tests the compressor** — windings, amp draw, and start behavior.

Ask for the measured capacitor value, the superheat/subcooling numbers, and whether a leak was actually located before any recharge.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor fan runs, warm air, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Inspect blower and coil if unresolved |
| Ice on lines or coil, weak airflow | Frozen coil (airflow or low charge) | Off/fan-only to thaw, new filter | Find root cause: airflow or refrigerant |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor fan OK | Capacitor, contactor, or breaker | Reset breaker once only | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Humming outside, fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor |
| Runs constantly, cools poorly on hot days | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris, hose off gently (power off) | Deep coil clean, check charge |
| Warm air, long run times, no ice yet | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Nothing happens on cooling call | Thermostat or wiring | Batteries, set to COOL | Test wiring and control board |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor or electrical fault | Do not keep resetting | Diagnose compressor/electrical |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Actual prices vary by region and by Rheem/Ruud model series.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $200 professional
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil deep cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $200–$700+ (varies heavily with R-410A pricing and amount)
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,200 — often the point where replacing the condenser makes more financial sense on an older unit

A simple capacitor or filter fix is inexpensive; refrigerant and compressor work is where quotes climb, which is why this is a pro-level problem.

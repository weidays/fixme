## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint homeowners have about a Rheem air conditioner. The system may be running (fan spinning, air moving) but the air coming from the vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

On Rheem systems with an **EcoNet communicating control board**, you may also see an actual alert code or a fault light. If your outdoor or indoor unit is showing a flashing LED, read that first — see **Related codes** below.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, this page helps you find the cause quickly and sanity-check any repair quote. Several of the real causes involve **refrigerant, high-voltage components, or the compressor**, which is why this is rated a pro-level problem.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow so the evaporator coil can freeze or the system can't move enough air to cool.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass clippings, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection, so the system runs but barely cools.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (Heat instead of Cool), or setpoint set too high.
4. **Tripped breaker on the outdoor unit** — the indoor fan runs on a separate circuit, so you feel airflow while the compressor is dead.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil.
6. **Failed run capacitor or contactor** — very common; the compressor/fan hums or won't start.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cools poorly, ices up, or blows only slightly cool air. Requires a certified tech.
8. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the outdoor unit runs the fan but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop at anything that requires opening the sealed system or the electrical panel beyond the breaker.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Cool**, drop the setpoint 5°F below room temp, and replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure it's running. Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around it, and gently rinse the coil fins from outside with a garden hose (power off). Keep 18–24 inches of clearance.
- **Vents and registers:** Confirm supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, **turn the system off and set the fan to ON** to thaw it (can take a few hours). Then replace the filter. If it re-freezes, you need a tech.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged condensate line can trigger a safety float switch that stops cooling. Empty the pan and clear the drain line if accessible.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable sequence — use this to judge whether a quote is fair:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is about 16–22°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Confirms power and controls** — verifies the contactor pulls in, the capacitor tests within rating (µF), and the thermostat calls for cooling correctly.
4. **Measures refrigerant** — attaches gauges to read pressures and calculates **superheat/subcooling** to determine if the charge is low, overcharged, or normal.
5. **Locates leaks** — if charge is low, uses an electronic detector or dye rather than just topping off.
6. **Tests the compressor** — checks amp draw, windings, and start behavior.
7. **On EcoNet models** — pulls stored fault codes from the control board.

Be cautious of any tech who adds refrigerant without checking for a leak, or who recommends a full system replacement before doing these basics.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but isn't cold | Dirty filter/coil, low refrigerant | Replace filter, clean condenser | Measure charge, find leak, clean coil |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, test circuit |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (high voltage) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw with fan ON, new filter | Diagnose charge/airflow |
| House never reaches setpoint | Undersized charge, weak compressor | Confirm thermostat settings | Charge check, compressor test |
| System short-cycles | Overheating, control fault | Clear coil, check filter | Diagnose control board / EcoNet code |
| Water near air handler | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, empty pan | Clear line, check float switch |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US retail with labor; your area and model affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$10 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type — R-410A vs. older R-22 systems)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,800; often the point where full-system replacement is worth pricing, especially on older units

If your Rheem is out of the labor warranty but within the **10-year parts warranty** (registered systems), major parts like the compressor or coil may be covered — you'd pay mostly labor and refrigerant.

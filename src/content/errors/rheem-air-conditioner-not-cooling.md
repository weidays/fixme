## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific Rheem fault code — your air conditioner is running (or trying to), but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On Rheem units with a communicating control board or an EcoNet-enabled system, you may see a related fault or a flashing status light at the outdoor unit, but many no-cooling problems produce no code at all.

The cause is almost always one of three things: **restricted airflow**, **a refrigerant/charge problem**, or **an electrical failure** (capacitor, contactor, or compressor). Some of these are safe homeowner checks; the rest are pro-level work involving high voltage or refrigerant.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow so the coil freezes or can't move heat. Most common and easiest to fix.
2. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — caked with grass, cottonwood, or dust so the unit can't reject heat.
3. **Thermostat issue** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a wiring/comms fault, especially with EcoNet controls.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the fan may spin but the compressor won't start, or you hear humming.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak or no cooling and often a frozen indoor coil. Requires a licensed tech.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
7. **Failed contactor** — worn or pitted contacts that won't energize the compressor.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; blocks cooling entirely until thawed.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — compressor overheats and shuts down on its internal overload.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the unit runs but produces no meaningful cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Everything beyond this involves high voltage or refrigerant — stop and call a pro.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of no-cooling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, fan on **Auto**, and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor breakers. If one has tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply vents are open and unblocked by furniture, and the return grille is clear.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (power off) if they look dirty. Don't bend fins or open the cabinet.
- **Condensate:** If you have a wet float switch or overflowing drain pan, a clogged condensate line can shut the system down. Clearing the line is sometimes DIY, but a flooded pan often signals a frozen coil — see below.
- **Frozen coil check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** and set the fan to **On** to thaw it (can take a few hours). If it re-freezes, you have low airflow or low refrigerant — call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the workflow helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Test electrical** — reads voltage at the contactor, tests the run/start capacitor with a meter, and checks the contactor and compressor windings.
4. **Gauge the refrigerant** — connects manifold gauges to read pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low charge points to a leak.
5. **Leak search** — if charge is low, uses a leak detector or dye rather than just "topping off," which is bad practice.
6. **Confirm the failed component** — condenser fan motor, compressor, board, or comms (on EcoNet systems).

A good tech will show you the readings. Be wary of anyone who wants to add refrigerant without finding the leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter / frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with fan on | Clean coil, restore airflow, check charge |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Failed run capacitor or compressor | None (high voltage) | Test/replace capacitor or diagnose compressor |
| Nothing runs outdoors | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check low-voltage fuse |
| Ice on refrigerant lines | Low charge or low airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find/repair leak, recharge |
| Humming then shutoff | Weak capacitor or seized compressor | None | Test capacitor, check compressor amps |
| House won't reach setpoint on hot days | Undersized charge or dirty coil | Clean/rinse outdoor unit | Verify charge, subcooling, airflow |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries / comms fault | Replace batteries | Check control wiring, EcoNet board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY).
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250.
- **Condensate line clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location; evaporator coil leaks cost more.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $600–$1,500.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often worth comparing against replacing the unit if it's out of warranty).

Rheem parts may be covered under a registered 10-year limited parts warranty, which cuts costs significantly — you'd still pay labor.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on Rheem/Ruud air conditioners — it's a symptom. Your system may be running (blower and outdoor fan spinning) but blowing warm or lukewarm air, or it may be short-cycling, or not turning on at all. Because central ACs are refrigerant-based mechanical systems, the causes range from a $10 filter to a failed compressor.

Some Rheem indoor units with a communicating control board or an EcoNet-enabled system will log a fault (like an open safety switch or a low-pressure lockout) that shows on the thermostat or as a flashing light on the air handler board. If your system is flashing a light, see the related article below. This page covers the general "runs but won't cool" complaint.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the most common and cheapest cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the evaporator coil, cutting cooling.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," wrong temperature, or dead batteries on a battery-powered stat.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or a puddle when it melts.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or outdoor fan won't start, so you get warm air.
5. **Tripped safety switch** — a full condensate drain pan can trip a float switch and shut the system down.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit gets pitted or stuck and stops sending power to the compressor.
7. **Low refrigerant / leak** — reduces or eliminates cooling capacity. Always indicates a leak that must be repaired (pro only).
8. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with debris can't reject heat, so cooling drops sharply.
9. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the outdoor unit hums or trips the breaker but won't cool.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do:

- **Filter:** Replace a dirty filter. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak or no cooling and coil icing.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breaker:** Check the indoor and outdoor breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets can damage the compressor.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water around the indoor unit or the drain pan is full, the float safety may have shut the system off. Clear a visible clog and dry the pan; use drain tablets to prevent buildup.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser fins. Do **not** open the unit or touch electrical parts.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system off (fan to On) to let it thaw for a few hours, then run again with a clean filter. Recurring ice means a pro visit.

Do not open the outdoor disconnect, touch the capacitor, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. Verify the thermostat call for cooling and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor contactor.
2. Measure the run capacitor's microfarad rating against spec and inspect the contactor for pitting or chatter.
3. Check the compressor and fan motor amp draw against nameplate values.
4. Attach refrigerant gauges to read suction and head pressures and measure superheat/subcooling to determine the charge.
5. Inspect the evaporator and condenser coils for dirt and airflow restriction.
6. If refrigerant is low, perform a leak search (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) — a proper diagnosis, not just a "top-off."
7. On EcoNet/communicating systems, pull stored fault history from the board or thermostat.

If a technician wants to just "add a pound of freon" without discussing a leak search, get a second opinion — that's a temporary fix, not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect coil/blower if it recurs |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw with fan on, new filter | Find airflow issue or leak, recharge |
| Fan runs, air not cold | Failed capacitor or contactor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor/contactor |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Diagnose electrical fault |
| System shuts off, water present | Full drain pan / float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, check pump/float |
| Cools poorly on hot days | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris around unit | Professional coil cleaning |
| Compressor hums, no cooling | Bad capacitor or failing compressor | None | Test compressor, replace if failed |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor or wiring fault | Stop resetting | Electrical/compressor diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and system size):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200, or DIY for visible clogs.
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400 replaced.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 replaced.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Outdoor fan motor:** $400–$700.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 depending on leak location; evaporator coil replacement pushes it higher.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+, often near the cost of a new condenser — replacing the unit is frequently the better value on older systems.

If your Rheem is under the standard 10-year parts warranty (registered within 60 days of install), major parts like the compressor may be covered — you'd pay labor only. Ask the tech to check coverage before approving big repairs.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice: your Rheem air conditioner is powered on and maybe even running, but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

On Rheem and Ruud systems this can mean anything from a completely blocked airflow path to a failed compressor. Some newer Rheem units with communicating controls (Prestige/EcoNet-enabled) may log a specific fault or flash an LED code at the board; older single-stage units simply run without cooling and give no code at all. Because behavior varies by model and control board, treat "not cooling" as a starting point, not a diagnosis.

The good news: a surprising share of "not cooling" calls trace back to airflow and thermostat issues you can check yourself in a few minutes.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — Restricts airflow, drops efficiency, and can freeze the evaporator coil until no cold air moves at all.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or dead batteries** — Set to "fan" instead of "cool," wrong temperature, or dead batteries on a battery-powered stat.
3. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer, so air blows but isn't cold.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — Grass clippings, dust, and debris insulate the coil so heat can't be rejected.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — A common part that can prevent the compressor or condenser fan motor from starting. Compressor hums but won't run.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Poor cooling, hissing, or ice on the copper lines. Requires a licensed tech to find and repair.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — Outdoor unit dead while the indoor blower still runs.
8. **Contactor failure** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the condenser never energizes.
9. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — The most expensive outcomes, usually last on the list after everything else checks out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening a sealed refrigerant system or bypassing a safety switch.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many no-cooling complaints.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a tech.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure the disconnect near the condenser is fully seated. Clear leaves, grass, and debris at least 2 feet around it.
- **Vents and registers:** Confirm supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper lines, turn the system to **Off** and set the fan to **On** to thaw it (can take a few hours). Running a frozen system makes it worse.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and cut cooling on many Rheem systems. If the drain pan is full, clear the drain line and add cleaning tablets.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech follows a logical sequence — this is what a fair quote reflects:

1. **Confirms the complaint** and checks thermostat call, filter, and airflow.
2. **Measures temperature split** across the coil (supply vs. return, typically 16–22°F on a healthy system). A low split points to airflow or refrigerant issues.
3. **Checks electrical:** capacitor microfarads, contactor condition, low-voltage fuse, and voltage at the compressor and fan.
4. **Inspects coils** indoor and out for dirt or ice, and checks the condensate/float switch.
5. **Connects gauges** to read susuperheat/subcooling and refrigerant charge — only if electrical and airflow are ruled out.
6. **Leak-searches** if refrigerant is low, using electronic detectors or dye, rather than just adding refrigerant.

Be cautious of any tech who "adds a couple pounds of refrigerant" without searching for a leak — that's a temporary fix that vents regulated refrigerant and hides the real problem.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but not cold | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp split, check blower |
| Ice on indoor coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Find cause: airflow vs. leak |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker or bad capacitor/contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call a tech | Replace capacitor, verify amp draw |
| Weak cooling, hissing near lines | Refrigerant leak | None — call a tech | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Cooling stops, drain pan full | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, test float switch |
| Warm air, dirty outdoor coil | Blocked condenser | Rinse fins gently, clear debris | Deep coil cleaning, check charge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Prices vary by region and system size.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250 professionally; near $0 DIY
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$700 installed
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,500 (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

If your Rheem system is over 12–15 years old and needs a compressor or a major refrigerant repair — especially on older R-22 systems — get a replacement quote alongside the repair.

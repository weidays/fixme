## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single blinking fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Rheem system is running but the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents isn't cold. On a modern Rheem condenser with a communicating board (Prestige, Endeavor with an EcoNet thermostat), you may see a specific fault or a flashing LED on the control board instead of cooling. On older single-stage units (RA13/RA14), there's often no code at all — the system simply runs and struggles.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a $20 filter to a $2,500 compressor, the goal below is to help you rule out the cheap, safe stuff first, then understand what a technician should be checking so you can sanity-check the quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops capacity and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass, cottonwood, and dust block heat rejection, so the system can't dump heat outside.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks airflow and cooling stops.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Rheem systems don't consume refrigerant. If it's low, there's a leak that must be found and repaired.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bulging or dead capacitor keeps the compressor or fan motor from starting; often the outdoor fan spins slow or not at all.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit; pitted or stuck contacts prevent the compressor from engaging.
7. **Thermostat problem or wrong setting** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/communication fault with EcoNet thermostats.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive cause; hums, trips the breaker, or won't start even with a good capacitor.
10. **Clogged condensate line triggering a float switch** — a full drain pan can shut off cooling to prevent water damage.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If they don't restore cooling, stop and call a licensed pro.

- **Air filter:** Pull it out. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Run the system 30 minutes and recheck cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL, the fan is on AUTO, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — that's an electrical fault for a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Kill power at the disconnect first, then clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around and inside the top grille. Keep 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see standing water in the drain pan or near the indoor unit, the line may be clogged and a float switch may have shut cooling off. Clearing the line (wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination) can restore operation.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF and set the fan to ON to thaw it (this can take a few hours). Never chip at ice.

**Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass a float or safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping. Those are pro or safety issues.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you tell a thorough diagnosis from a parts-cannon guess:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature. A healthy system typically shows a 16–22°F "delta-T" split.
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil; confirms the coil isn't iced.
3. **Electrical checks** — tests the capacitor with a meter, inspects the contactor for pitting, and verifies voltage at the condenser.
4. **Refrigerant readings** — connects gauges to read suction/head pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to judge the charge. Low readings point to a leak.
5. **Leak search** — if the charge is low, a good tech finds the leak (electronic detector or dye) rather than just topping off.
6. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw and windings if the compressor won't start or trips the breaker.
7. **Controls** — on EcoNet/communicating models, reads any stored faults from the board or thermostat.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or pressure readings is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air from vents isn't cold, unit runs | Dirty filter/coil or low charge | Replace filter, clear outdoor coil | Measure delta-T, check charge, find leak |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn AC off, fan on to thaw | Fix airflow or repair leak, recharge |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or motor | Cut power, confirm fan is stuck | Test/replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Compressor hums then trips breaker | Bad capacitor or failing compressor | None — call pro | Test capacitor, then compressor windings |
| Water near indoor unit, no cooling | Clogged condensate line/float switch | Clear drain line | Clear line, verify float switch |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries or wiring fault | Replace batteries | Check wiring, transformer, board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate line clearing:** $0 DIY – $150–$250 pro
- **Condenser coil cleaning / tune-up:** $100–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$300
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (more if the coil must be replaced)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against a full condenser swap on older units)

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer R-454B), and whether the part is under Rheem's parts warranty — registered units often carry a 10-year parts warranty, so ask your tech to check the serial number.

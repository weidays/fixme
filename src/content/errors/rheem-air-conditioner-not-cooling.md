## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Most residential Rheem and Ruud split-system air conditioners don't display an alphanumeric error for lack of cooling; instead you notice warm air from the vents, a system that runs constantly, or an indoor temperature that never reaches setpoint.

On Rheem's communicating and inverter models (Prestige series with EcoNet), you may see a status message or fault indicator at the thermostat or on the control board LED. On older single-stage units (RA13, RA14), there's no display at all — you diagnose by observing what runs and what doesn't.

This page covers the whole "no cool air" symptom. If you're seeing a **flashing indicator light on a heat pump**, see the related page instead, since that has its own diagnostic path.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — the single most common cause. A clogged filter starves airflow across the evaporator coil, dropping capacity and often freezing the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. Ice on the coil blocks heat transfer, so air blows but isn't cold.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — leaves, grass, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit (condenser) has its own breaker; if it's off, the indoor fan runs but no cooling happens.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. A bad capacitor stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting; you may hear humming.
6. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/setting issue.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, longer run times, and frozen coils. Requires a licensed tech (EPA-regulated).
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit fails to close.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome. Unit hums, trips the breaker, or runs without cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter** — pull it and look. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to **COOL**, the fan to **AUTO**, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers** — check the panel for a tripped breaker for both the air handler and the outdoor unit. Reset **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets can damage the compressor.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Closed vents reduce airflow and can freeze the coil.
- **Condensate drain** — if you see a full drain pan or water near the indoor unit, a clogged drain may have tripped a float safety switch. You can clear a visible clog and empty the pan; drain tablets help prevent recurrence.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Keep the airflow area clear by at least two feet.

**If the coil is iced up:** turn the system to **OFF** but leave the fan set to **ON** for a few hours to thaw it. Do not chip at the ice. Once thawed, replace the filter before restarting.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work through this sequence so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Airflow and filter check** — confirms the basics first.
2. **Temperature split** — measures the difference between return and supply air (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F). A low split points to refrigerant or airflow issues.
3. **Electrical checks** — tests the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, verifies voltage to the condenser, and inspects the wiring.
4. **Refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to read suction and liquid pressures and measures superheat/subcooling. This tells them if the charge is correct and whether there's a leak.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, they'll use electronic sniffers or dye to find the leak. A tech who just "tops off" without finding the leak is doing it wrong.
6. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw, windings, and start behavior if the compressor is suspect.

Expect a diagnostic fee of **$80–$180**, often applied to the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, filter is filthy | Dirty filter starving airflow | Replace filter | Verify airflow, check coil for ice |
| Ice on indoor coil or line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw, replace filter | Find airflow/refrigerant root cause |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, bad capacitor/contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None | Replace run/start capacitor |
| Runs constantly, weak cooling | Low refrigerant (leak) or dirty condenser | Clean around condenser | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| No response at all | Thermostat, control power, breaker | Check batteries, mode, breaker | Diagnose control circuit/board |
| Trips breaker repeatedly | Failing compressor or short | Stop resetting | Test compressor windings and amps |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor) for Rheem/Ruud AC:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$300
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV/metering device:** $400–$700
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$1,800+ (often better to weigh against full system replacement on older units; check your Rheem parts warranty — many carry 10-year coverage on registered units)

Note that refrigerant type matters: units using R-410A cost less to recharge than newer refrigerants. Costs also run higher in high-labor-rate markets.

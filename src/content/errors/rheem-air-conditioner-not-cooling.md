## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on Rheem air conditioners — it's the symptom homeowners describe when the system runs but the house stays warm, or the outdoor unit won't start at all. Unlike a heat pump flashing a diagnostic LED, a straight AC often gives no visible code and requires interpreting behavior: is the compressor running, is the indoor blower moving air, and is the air coming out actually cold?

Rheem split systems (RA13 through RA20 series) fail to cool for reasons ranging from a trivial dirty filter to a failed compressor. Higher-tier Endeavor and Prestige units with communicating boards may store fault history you can't see without a service tool, while base Classic units give you almost nothing to read. This page walks through the causes in order of likelihood so you can tell a quick fix from a real repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, freezes the indoor coil, and kills cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat when caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood. Cooling drops sharply on hot days.
3. **Tripped condensate float switch** — a blocked drain fills the pan and the safety switch shuts the system down. Common in summer humidity.
4. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a loose C-wire. Simple but easy to overlook.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start. Very common in units over 5-7 years old.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit gets pitted and won't close, so the condenser never starts.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, ice on the lines, and a warm house. Always indicates a leak, never "just needs a top-off."
8. **Failed condenser fan or blower motor** — no airflow across a coil means no heat transfer.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit runs but produces no cooling, often after a hard-start or electrical failure.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do not open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Then wait a few hours — if the indoor coil froze, it must thaw before cooling returns.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temp, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the panel for a tripped breaker on both the air handler and the outdoor unit. Reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** Look for water in or overflowing the drain pan under the indoor unit. A full pan trips the float switch. You can gently clear a visible clog and add drain tablets, but if it refills, call for service.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from the fins and give it at least 2 feet of clearance. Do not spray a running unit.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable process — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature split (should be roughly 16-22°F on a properly charged system).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Tests electrical components** — measures the run capacitor's microfarads, checks the contactor for pitting, and verifies 24V control signal at the outdoor unit.
4. **Reads refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to check suction and liquid line pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to confirm the charge.
5. **Leak search if low** — uses electronic detector or dye to find the leak before adding refrigerant, since a recharge without a repair just leaks out again.
6. **Motor and compressor tests** — checks amp draw against nameplate and tests compressor windings if it won't start.

Be wary of any tech who adds refrigerant without checking for a leak, or who quotes a compressor without testing the capacitor and contactor first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter / frozen coil | Replace filter, let coil thaw | Verify airflow, clear ice, check blower |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor fan runs | Failed capacitor or contactor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor/contactor |
| System shuts off, water in pan | Tripped condensate float switch | Clear visible drain clog, add tablets | Flush drain line, verify switch |
| Ice on refrigerant lines | Low refrigerant or airflow issue | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed fan motor or capacitor | Turn off unit | Replace motor/capacitor |
| Runs constantly, no cooling | Low refrigerant or failed compressor | None | Diagnose charge and compressor |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker or control failure | Reset breaker once | Trace power/control fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Coil cleaning / drain flush:** $75–$250
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 (higher if it's an evaporator coil leak)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often makes replacing an older unit more economical)

If your unit is under warranty, Rheem typically covers parts (5–10 years with registration) but not labor or refrigerant — confirm coverage before authorizing major work.

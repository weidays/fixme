## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific error code — it's the symptom homeowners describe when a Rheem air conditioner runs but the house stays warm, or the outdoor unit won't start at all. Rheem split systems don't display a numeric "not cooling" code; instead, you'll notice warm air at the vents, an outdoor unit that hums but doesn't blow, ice on the copper lines, or a system that short-cycles.

Because the cause can be anything from a $10 filter to a failed compressor, this is a broad diagnostic page. Some higher-end Rheem units (Prestige and Endeavor with communicating boards) may store a fault in the thermostat or control board, but the classic RA-series units give you no on-unit code — you diagnose by symptom.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, reduces cooling, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the outdoor fan or compressor hums but won't spin. Very common on units 5+ years old.
3. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil is caked with dirt, grass, or cottonwood, so heat can't dissipate.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — weak cooling, ice on the suction line, or long run times. Always indicates a leak.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil ices over and blocks air completely.
6. **Failed contactor** — the outdoor unit gets no power to start because the contactor's contacts are pitted or stuck.
7. **Tripped float/safety switch** — a clogged condensate drain trips the float switch and cuts cooling to prevent water damage.
8. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/C-wire fault.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — unit runs but the top fan doesn't turn, causing the compressor to overheat and shut down.
10. **Failed compressor** — the least common but most expensive; the unit trips the breaker or hums and locks out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass a float or safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping the breaker.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset one that's tripped **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal an electrical fault.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (power off) if they're dirty.
- **Condensate drain:** If cooling stopped and you see water near the indoor unit or a full drain pan, the drain may be clogged and a float switch may have tripped. Clearing a visible clog is safe; the pan and switch reset once water drains.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully — this can take a few hours — before running again.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheapest to most expensive, and you can sanity-check the quote against these steps:

1. **Verifies the complaint** — checks supply-air temperature vs. return-air temperature (a healthy split is ~15–20°F).
2. **Checks airflow and filter** — confirms the blower runs and the filter/coil are clean.
3. **Tests electrical components** — measures the run capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, and confirms 24V control signal from the thermostat.
4. **Inspects the outdoor unit** — confirms the condenser fan spins and the compressor draws proper amperage.
5. **Connects refrigerant gauges** — reads suction and liquid pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to judge refrigerant charge. Low readings point to a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, uses electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test to find the leak (this is billable and can take time).
7. **Checks safeties** — inspects the float switch, high/low-pressure switches, and any stored fault on communicating models.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add refrigerant" without searching for the leak — that's a temporary, recurring fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Dirty filter/coil | Replace filter, check vents | Deep-clean coils, check blower |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None (capacitors hold charge) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines/indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw with fan on | Find airflow issue or leak, recharge |
| Long run times, weak cooling | Low refrigerant (leak) | None | Leak search and repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit dead, no start | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Cooling stopped, water at indoor unit | Clogged drain / float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear drain line, test float switch |
| Fan runs, no cold air, breaker trips | Compressor fault | Stop resetting | Test compressor, quote replacement |
| Nothing responds at thermostat | Dead batteries or C-wire fault | Replace batteries | Diagnose control wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and unit size.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 for a service call.
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$650 installed.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500, depending on leak location and how much refrigerant is needed (R-410A cost varies).
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often worth weighing against a full replacement on older units).
- **Full system diagnostic/service call:** $80–$200, frequently credited toward the repair.

If your unit is 12+ years old and facing a compressor or coil replacement, ask your tech to compare the repair cost against a new system — the math often favors replacement.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Rheem air conditioner runs but the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents feels warm or only mildly cool. Rheem split systems (Classic, Classic Plus, Prestige, and the newer Endeavor line) don't usually flash a dedicated "no cooling" code; instead the outdoor and indoor units simply fail to move heat effectively.

If your unit **is** flashing a diagnostic light, read that first — the light pattern narrows the problem considerably. See our companion article referenced under Related codes.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a $10 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you from the cheapest, safest checks up to what only a licensed tech should touch.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; a block of ice can't absorb heat, so vents blow warm.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bulging or dead capacitor keeps the compressor or fan motor from starting. Very common on units 6+ years old.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — matted with grass, dust, or cottonwood, the unit can't reject heat.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — cooling fades gradually; often paired with icing. Requires EPA-certified repair.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit pits or sticks, so the compressor never energizes.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
8. **Thermostat wiring or setting fault** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a loose wire.
9. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the worst case; the outdoor unit hums, trips the breaker, or does nothing.

Whether a two-stage or inverter Endeavor model behaves differently (e.g., staging or communicating faults) **varies by control board** — those models may log a specific error the tech can read.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first — it fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the fan to **AUTO**, and the setpoint several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker on both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) circuits. Reset **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal a real fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't starving airflow. Keep return grilles clear.
- **Outdoor unit:** Check that the condenser coil isn't buried in leaves, grass clippings, or dryer lint. Gently rinse debris off the fins with a garden hose (power off) if you're comfortable — do not bend the fins.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the coil or copper lines, switch the system to **OFF** (fan can stay on) and let it thaw fully — several hours. Running a frozen unit makes things worse.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or a tripped float switch can shut cooling down. If you see standing water, the drain line is likely clogged and needs clearing.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work methodically — this is how you can sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify airflow and filter**, then inspect the evaporator coil and blower for ice or dirt.
2. **Check thermostat call and low-voltage (24V)** signal reaching the contactor.
3. **Measure capacitor microfarads** against the rated value on the label — a failed cap is a cheap, fast find.
4. **Test the contactor** for pitted contacts and confirm the compressor/fan energize.
5. **Read outdoor amp draw** on the compressor and fan motor to spot a seized or overloaded motor.
6. **Attach gauges and take temperatures** to measure superheat/subcooling — this reveals refrigerant charge and whether there's a leak.
7. **Leak search** with electronic detector or dye if the charge is low. A reputable tech finds and repairs the leak rather than just topping off.

If a tech proposes adding refrigerant without looking for a leak, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Confirm airflow, inspect coil |
| Ice on coil/lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, let thaw, replace filter | Find cause; check charge & leaks |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cool | Failed capacitor or contactor | None (line voltage) | Test and replace cap/contactor |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker / blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Trace fault, replace fuse |
| Cooling fades over weeks | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search & repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor or seized compressor | None | Test cap; assess compressor |
| Warm air, house never cools | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil (power off) | Professional coil cleaning |
| System won't turn on at all | Thermostat batteries/settings | Replace batteries, check mode | Check 24V wiring & board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Regional and after-hours rates vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350.
- **Blower or condenser fan motor:** $400–$700.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer R-454B on Endeavor units).
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,500.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+; on an older unit, replacing the whole system often makes more sense.

Diagnostic/service-call fees typically run $75–$200 and are sometimes waived if you approve the repair.

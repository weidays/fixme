## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific Rheem fault code — it's the symptom you notice when the thermostat calls for cool but indoor temperature won't drop. On Rheem and Ruud systems it can point to problems anywhere in the cooling chain: airflow, the refrigerant circuit, electrical components, or the control signal itself.

Rheem central air conditioners don't have a digital display on the condenser, so unlike a furnace there's no blinking code to read here. If your **indoor air handler or furnace board** is flashing, that's a separate diagnostic path — see the Related codes section. This page covers the classic "system is on but the house stays warm" complaint.

Because refrigerant and compressor work require licensed, EPA-certified service, we've marked this as a **pro** repair. There are still safe homeowner checks worth doing first, and knowing what a good tech will do helps you sanity-check any quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil into a block of ice that stops cooling entirely.
2. **Dirty condenser or evaporator coil** — a matted outdoor coil or dust-caked indoor coil sheds heat poorly; cooling gets weak and electric bills climb.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (Heat vs Cool), or a mis-set fan setting. Common and cheap.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the condenser or air handler has lost power on one circuit while the other still runs the fan.
5. **Full condensate pan / clogged drain** — many Rheem air handlers have a float switch that cuts cooling when the pan fills to prevent water damage.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or fan motor hums but won't start, or the outdoor fan won't spin.
7. **Contactor failure** — the relay that powers the condenser sticks open, so the outdoor unit never energizes.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the lines, or hissing. Requires certified diagnosis and repair; behavior varies by whether your unit uses R-410A or the newer R-454B refrigerant in recent models.
9. **Frozen evaporator coil** — the result of #1, #2, or #8; the unit runs but blows warm.
10. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive outcome, often preceded by the smaller failures above.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or a live electrical panel beyond flipping a breaker.

- **Filter:** Pull the air filter. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it and give the system 30 minutes.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers or disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser and gently rinse the outer coil fins with a garden hose (power off).
- **Condensate drain:** If your unit has a float switch and the pan is full, clear the drain line. A wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain outlet often clears a clog; drain tablets help prevent regrowth.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** and set the fan to **On** for an hour to thaw before running again. Persistent freezing means a pro visit.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work the system methodically, not just "add a pound of freon." Expect them to:

1. Verify the thermostat call and confirm 24V is reaching the contactor.
2. Check the run capacitor with a meter and inspect the contactor for pitting or sticking.
3. Measure compressor and fan motor amp draw against the nameplate.
4. Inspect both coils for dirt and the filter for restriction.
5. Attach gauges to read suction and head pressures, then calculate **superheat and subcooling** — this is how they confirm a refrigerant charge issue rather than guessing.
6. If refrigerant is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector or dye) — a reputable tech repairs the leak, doesn't just top off.
7. Check the float switch and condensate path if cooling drops out intermittently.

If a quote jumps straight to "recharge" or "new compressor" without capacitor, airflow, and pressure readings, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs constantly, house stays warm | Dirty filter or coil, low charge | Replace filter, clean outdoor coil | Measure superheat/subcooling, clean evaporator |
| Ice on copper lines or indoor coil | Airflow restriction or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw, replace filter | Find/repair leak, verify charge |
| Outdoor fan not spinning, humming | Failed run capacitor | None — capacitor holds charge | Test and replace capacitor safely |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check control voltage |
| Cooling stops, water near air handler | Full condensate pan / float switch | Clear drain line with wet/dic | Clear drain, replace float switch/pump |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries, blown 24V fuse | Replace batteries | Replace low-voltage fuse, find short |
| Weak airflow from all vents | Clogged filter, blower issue | Replace filter | Inspect blower motor and capacitor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Regional labor rates and refrigerant type (R-410A vs R-454B) shift these figures.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 — DIY.
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 — DIY.
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 professional.
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400 replaced.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 replaced.
- **Low-voltage fuse or wiring repair:** $80–$250.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,800 depending on leak location and refrigerant.
- **Blower motor (indoor):** $400–$900.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ — often the point where replacing the condenser or system is worth pricing.
- **Full system replacement:** $5,000–$12,000+ if the unit is old and the compressor is gone.

If your Rheem AC is under 10 years old, check warranty status — Rheem covers many parts (and the compressor) for a registered original owner, which can drop your out-of-pocket to labor only.

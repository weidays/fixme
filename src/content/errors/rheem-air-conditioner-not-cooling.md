## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on a Rheem air conditioner — most Rheem/Ruud split systems don't display alphanumeric codes at the condenser. Instead, it's the symptom you're reporting: the system runs (or tries to) but your home isn't reaching the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents isn't cold.

Because there's no single code, the cause can be anything from a clogged filter to a failed compressor. This page walks through the likely causes in order, the checks you can safely do yourself, and what a technician should charge to fix each one.

If your outdoor unit is completely dead (no fan, no hum) or your indoor blower won't start, that's a separate no-power problem — but it's covered in the causes below too.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — The single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the evaporator coil of airflow, which drops capacity and can freeze the coil into a block of ice.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — A visible result of low airflow or low refrigerant. Once iced over, the system can't transfer heat and blows lukewarm air.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — The compressor and/or condenser fan capacitor is a frequent failure. The outdoor fan may spin slowly, hum, or not start; the compressor may click but not run.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — A slow leak reduces cooling capacity over years. This is never "normal loss" — sealed systems don't consume refrigerant. Requires a certified tech.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — Cottonwood, grass clippings, and dirt on the outdoor coil block heat rejection, so the system runs constantly without cooling well.
6. **Contactor failure** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit can pit or stick, preventing the compressor and fan from energizing.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — The outdoor disconnect, breaker, or the 3–5 amp fuse on the indoor control board may have opened.
8. **Failed compressor** — The worst case. Windings short or the compressor seizes, and the unit either won't start or trips the breaker. Often not economical to repair on older units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Filter:** Pull the filter at the return grille or air handler. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Then wait — a frozen coil can take several hours to thaw.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery-powered model. Try lowering it a few degrees to force a call for cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser may have their own). Reset once. If it trips again immediately, stop — that's a fault for a technician.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Confirm the pull-out disconnect box near the condenser is fully seated.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged condensate line can trigger a float switch that shuts off cooling. Check for standing water at the air handler.
- **Ice:** If you see frost or ice on the copper lines or coil, **turn the AC off** and set the fan to ON to thaw it. Running a frozen system can damage the compressor.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety or float switch, or repeatedly reset a breaker that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work in this order, and you can sanity-check their quote against it:

1. **Verify the complaint** — Confirm airflow, thermostat call, and whether both indoor and outdoor units energize.
2. **Check electrical** — Measure incoming voltage, contactor operation, and capacitor microfarad values with a meter. A weak capacitor is cheap and common.
3. **Inspect airflow** — Filter condition, blower operation, and coil cleanliness.
4. **Gauge the refrigerant** — Connect manifold gauges, read suction/liquid pressures, and check superheat/subcooling against Rheem's charging chart for that model. This tells them whether the charge is correct.
5. **Leak search** — If refrigerant is low, they should find the leak (electronic detector, dye, or bubble test) — not just add gas and leave.
6. **Compressor test** — Measure amp draw and winding resistance if the compressor won't start or trips the breaker.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add a couple pounds of refrigerant" without discussing where it went. That's a leak, and topping off is a temporary fix you'll pay for again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airflow weak, coil iced over | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, run fan to thaw | Clean coil, verify blower speed |
| Vents blow lukewarm, coil frozen | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, thaw, then call | Locate and repair leak, recharge |
| Outdoor fan hums or won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit fully dead | Breaker/disconnect/fuse | Check breaker & disconnect once | Replace fuse/contactor, find cause |
| Runs constantly, poor cooling | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse debris off exterior gently | Deep-clean coil, check charge |
| Compressor clicks, breaker trips | Failing compressor/contactor | Stop resetting; call | Amp/winding test, replace as needed |
| No water at drain, unit off | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear visible standing water | Clear drain line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Regional rates and unit size affect these.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY).
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250.
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Low-voltage fuse / minor electrical:** $100–$250.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs older R-22 is far pricier).
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,800.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500. On a unit over 10–12 years old, weigh this against full system replacement.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom homeowners describe when a Rheem or Ruud air conditioner runs but the air coming from the vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. Because it's a symptom and not a stored code, the underlying cause can be anything from a dirty filter to a dead compressor.

On systems with a Rheem **EcoNet** control or a modern control board, you may see a related fault indication (a flashing LED or an app alert) that narrows things down. On older Rheem/Ruud units with a basic board, there's often no code at all — diagnosis is purely by measurement. What the board reports varies by model, so don't assume your unit behaves like a neighbor's.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can freeze the evaporator. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so heat can't dump outside.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil.
4. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a miswired/failing thermostat.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge always means a leak.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit may be dead while the indoor blower still runs.
7. **Failed run capacitor** — a common, cheap-to-replace part; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
8. **Failing contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit sticks or won't pull in.
9. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the outdoor unit runs the fan but produces no cooling and often trips on overload.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check the panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure it's not buried in leaves, mulch, or overgrown plants. Clear at least 2 feet of space around it. Gently rinse the coil fins with a garden hose (never a pressure washer).
- **Supply and return vents:** Confirm they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, **turn the system off** and set the fan to ON to thaw it. Do not run cooling on a frozen coil.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and shut cooling down. Check that the drain line is clear and the pan isn't overflowing.

Do not open sealed refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch — those are pro-only tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work methodically, not just "add a pound of Freon." Expect them to:

1. Confirm the complaint and check thermostat call, mode, and setpoint.
2. Verify airflow — inspect the filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. Measure the **temperature split** across the indoor coil (typically 15–22°F when cooling properly).
4. Test line voltage at the disconnect and low-voltage control signal (24V) to the outdoor unit.
5. Check the **run capacitor** and **contactor** with a meter.
6. Connect gauges to read suction/liquid pressures and calculate **superheat and subcooling** to determine the charge.
7. If refrigerant is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) — a proper repair fixes the leak, not just tops off charge.
8. On EcoNet-equipped systems, read stored fault data through the control or app.

A good quote will name the specific failed part and the measured values behind it.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm air, dirty filter | Clogged filter, low airflow | Replace filter | Inspect blower and coil for restriction |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Frozen evaporator | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Find airflow or refrigerant cause |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cool air | Low refrigerant / bad compressor | None — call pro | Gauge readings, leak search, compressor test |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor blows | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, low-voltage fuse, wiring |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| House won't hit setpoint | Dirty condenser, undersized charge | Rinse coil, clear debris | Verify charge, check subcooling |
| Nothing runs at all | Thermostat, power, float switch | Check mode, batteries, drain | Trace 24V circuit and safety switches |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, including parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $8–$30 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0–$10 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$200
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **TXV / metering device:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800–$2,500 (often near the cost of a new system; weigh repair vs. replace on older units)

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type (R-410A vs. newer refrigerants), and whether the unit is under Rheem's parts warranty — registered Rheem systems often carry a 10-year parts warranty, which can cut compressor and coil costs to labor only.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint homeowners bring to a Rheem air conditioner. The system may be running (fan spinning, outdoor unit humming) but the air coming from the registers is warm or only mildly cool, or the house simply never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

Because "not cooling" is a symptom rather than a stored code, your Rheem unit won't necessarily flash a diagnostic light for it. Some higher-end **Prestige/EcoNet-connected** models will report specific faults (like a locked-out compressor or comfort control alert) through the EcoNet app or thermostat, while basic **Classic series** units give you nothing but the symptom itself. If your outdoor unit is flashing a light, see the related page below.

The underlying cause falls into three buckets: **airflow problems**, **electrical/component failures**, or **refrigerant/sealed-system issues**. The first bucket is often DIY; the last two usually require a licensed tech.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," wrong setpoint, or dead batteries in a battery-powered stat.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs, so you feel airflow but no cooling.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan can't start. You may hear humming or clicking outside with no fan spin.
5. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — caked with dust, grass, or cottonwood; the system can't reject heat, so cooling collapses on hot days.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks the coil and warm air blows.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the compressor never energizes.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced capacity, warm air, sometimes ice. Requires leak repair, not just a "top-off."
9. **Failed compressor** — the heart of the system won't pump. The most expensive outcome and typically the last thing a tech confirms.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop before anything involving refrigerant, capacitors, or opening electrical panels beyond the breaker.

- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Run the system 30 minutes and recheck cooling.
- **Verify the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Look at the breakers.** Find the breakers for the indoor air handler and the outdoor condenser. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Inspect supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking registers. Closed vents starve airflow.
- **Check for ice.** Look at the copper refrigerant lines and the indoor coil area. If you see frost or ice, **turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON**, and let it thaw for a few hours before restarting.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a safety float switch that shuts cooling down. Clear standing water and confirm the line is draining.

If cooling doesn't return after the filter, thermostat, and breaker checks, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap-and-likely to expensive-and-rare — so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirms airflow and thermostat call** — verifies 24V signal reaching the outdoor unit and measures temperature split across the indoor coil (typically 16–22°F on a healthy system).
2. **Checks electrical components** — tests the capacitor with a meter, inspects the contactor for pitting, and confirms the compressor and fan motor draw proper amperage.
3. **Inspects coils and airflow path** — looks for a dirty condenser or a frozen/dirty evaporator.
4. **Connects gauges to read pressures** — this is where refrigerant charge is evaluated. Low pressures point to a leak; the tech should then perform a **leak search** (electronic sniffer, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just adding refrigerant.
5. **Confirms compressor health** — if pressures, power, and capacitor are all good but the compressor won't pump, it's tested for windings and mechanical failure.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "you need refrigerant" or "you need a compressor" without measuring the capacitor, contactor, and temperature split first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter, run 30 min | Check duct/blower if unresolved |
| Fan blows but never cools | Wrong thermostat mode / dead batteries | Set to COOL/AUTO, replace batteries | Test thermostat & 24V control wiring |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Find cause of trip, replace fuse |
| Humming outside, fan not spinning | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace run/start capacitor |
| Outdoor coil caked in debris | Dirty condenser | Gently rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean, check pressures |
| Ice on lines, warm air | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, fan ON to thaw | Diagnose airflow/charge, find leak |
| Clicking, no compressor start | Failed contactor | None — call pro | Replace contactor, test compressor |
| Cools poorly on hot days only | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, evacuate, recharge |
| No cooling, high amp draw / breaker trips | Failed compressor | None — call pro | Test and replace compressor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Rheem parts are widely available, so pricing is fairly typical for the industry.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$450
- **Contactor:** $150–$400
- **Condensate drain clear / float switch:** $100–$250
- **Blower motor:** $400–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against system replacement if the unit is 10+ years old)

Diagnostic/service call fees typically run $80–$180 and are often credited toward the repair. On older R-22 systems, refrigerant cost alone can push repairs higher and make replacement the smarter choice.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single fault code — it's a symptom. On most Rheem split-system air conditioners, the outdoor unit and indoor blower are told to run by your thermostat, and cool air only happens when the compressor, fans, and refrigerant charge are all working together. If any one link in that chain fails, the system either blows warm air, doesn't blow at all, or short-cycles.

Some Rheem systems paired with a communicating thermostat (like the Rheem EcoNet controls) may display a status or fault message, but the exact wording **varies by control board and thermostat model**. A basic RA13 with a standard thermostat gives no code at all — you diagnose it by behavior. Don't assume a specific code exists on your unit unless your thermostat actually shows one.

This is rated a **pro-level** problem because the most damaging causes (low refrigerant, compressor and capacitor faults) require certified tools and EPA credentials. The safe checks below are worth doing first, but expect to call a technician for anything beyond airflow.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow so badly the coil can freeze or the system can't move heat. The single most common "no cooling" call.
2. **Dirty or blocked outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, dryer lint, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a failed thermostat that never calls for cooling.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit and indoor unit often have separate breakers; one can trip while the other runs.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or fan motor hums or won't start; often the fan blade needs a push to spin (a classic bad-cap symptom).
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that sends power to the compressor pits, sticks, or fails to close, so the outdoor unit stays dead.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — warm air, ice on the copper lines, or long run times with poor cooling. Never a "top-off" fix — the leak must be found.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow (see #1) or low charge (see #7); ice blocks cooling entirely.
9. **Clogged condensate drain / full float switch** — a safety float switch shuts the system off to prevent water damage.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case. It hums, trips the breaker, or is dead. Expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the electrical panel of the outdoor unit, touch the capacitor, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Restricted airflow is the #1 cause of no cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering it 5°F to force a call.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker (indoor air handler/furnace and outdoor condenser may be on separate breakers). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or leaves choking the coil. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (power off, water flowing top to bottom). Don't bend the fins.
- **Condensate drain:** If your system has a float switch and the drain line is clogged, the unit won't run. Check for standing water in the drain pan; clearing the line may restore cooling.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** to thaw it (can take several hours). Then replace the filter. If it re-freezes, you likely have low refrigerant — call a pro.

If none of these fixes it, the problem is electrical or refrigerant-related. That's technician territory.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works methodically, not by guessing. Here's what a legitimate diagnosis looks like so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling** — verifies thermostat sends 24V to the contactor.
2. **Check the contactor and capacitor** — measures capacitor microfarads against the rated value and inspects the contactor for pitting/failure.
3. **Measure electrical draw** — amperage on the compressor and fan motor to spot a failing or locked compressor.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures** — gauges on the service ports plus temperature readings to calculate superheat/subcooling and determine if the charge is correct.
5. **Leak search** — if charge is low, they use electronic detectors, UV dye, or bubbles to find the leak. Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.
6. **Inspect coils and airflow** — evaporator and condenser coil condition, blower operation, and static pressure.

Ask for the actual measured values (capacitor reading, subcooling, amp draw). A tech who won't share numbers may be guessing.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter, thaw coil with fan ON | Verify airflow, check charge |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker, bad contactor/cap | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor or capacitor |
| Compressor hums, fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — do not open panel | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Filter swap, thaw unit | Find/repair leak, recharge |
| Long runs, never gets cold | Low refrigerant, dirty condenser | Rinse outdoor coil | Pressure test, leak search |
| Unit off, water in drain pan | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain line | Clear/repair drain, reset switch |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Failing compressor or short | Do not keep resetting | Amp draw test, compressor check |
| No response at all | Thermostat or control failure | Check batteries/settings | Test board, wiring, thermostat |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 (basic) to $400+ (smart/communicating)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often worth weighing against a full system replacement on older units)

If the compressor is failing and the unit is 12+ years old or uses R-22, get a replacement quote alongside the repair quote before deciding.

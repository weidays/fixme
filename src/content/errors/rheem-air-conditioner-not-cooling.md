## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on Rheem air conditioners — it's the symptom you notice when the system runs but the house won't reach the set temperature, or the air from the vents feels warm. Unlike a heat pump throwing a flashing fault light, a straight-cool Rheem AC often gives no diagnostic blink at all; you simply feel that something is wrong.

The cause can be anything from a $0 clogged filter to a $2,500 compressor failure. This page walks you through what's safe to check yourself and how a technician narrows it down, so you can tell a fair diagnosis from an oversold repair.

> Behavior varies by model. Two-stage and variable-speed Rheem units (Prestige/Endeavor) have communicating boards that may log faults readable at the thermostat, while basic single-stage RA13/RA14 units typically do not.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, causing weak or warm airflow and, in bad cases, a frozen evaporator coil.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling capacity drops sharply, especially on hot days.
3. **Failed run/dual capacitor** — the compressor and/or condenser fan won't start; you may hear humming with no fan spin.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — gradual loss of cooling, longer run times, ice on the copper lines. Always a leak, never "normal use."
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power; indoor blower may still run.
7. **Thermostat problems** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a miswired/failing thermostat.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor fan doesn't spin, unit overheats and shuts down on high-pressure limit.
9. **Contactor failure** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive outcome; the unit hums, trips the breaker, or won't start at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. If any require opening the electrical panel or the outdoor disconnect beyond flipping a breaker, stop and call a pro.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Run the system 30 minutes and recheck cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — that's an electrical fault for a technician.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Closing too many vents starves airflow.
- **Condensate line:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear a visible clog if you can safely reach it.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for a fan that isn't spinning, ice on the copper lines, or heavy debris/leaves blocking the coil. **Turn the unit off at the thermostat** if you see ice, and let it thaw before running again.

Do **not** open the outdoor disconnect, discharge a capacitor, add refrigerant, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — knowing it helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition.
3. **Tests electrical** — reads the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, and confirms voltage at the outdoor unit.
4. **Attaches refrigerant gauges** — reads suction/head pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge the refrigerant charge. Low readings trigger a **leak search** (dye, electronic sniffer, or nitrogen).
5. **Inspects the compressor** — amp draw and start behavior if the unit hums or trips.
6. **Reads board faults** on communicating Prestige/Endeavor models.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or to "replace the compressor" without electrical testing first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower & duct static |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan still | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test & replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker/contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Poor cooling on hot days | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil gently with hose | Deep coil clean, check charge |
| Fan won't spin, unit hot | Bad fan motor | None — call pro | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Unit trips breaker repeatedly | Compressor or short | Stop resetting — call pro | Electrical test, compressor check |
| No cooling, water in pan | Clogged condensate/float switch | Clear visible clog | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Dual run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $150–$600+ (varies by type and amount; older R-22 systems cost far more)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500 (often worth weighing against a full system replacement on older units)

Prices vary by region, system size, and refrigerant type. A diagnostic fee of $75–$200 is normal and often credited toward the repair.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Your Rheem or Ruud air conditioner may be running (outdoor fan spinning, indoor blower moving air) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint.

Unlike a flashing fault light on a heat pump control board, a straight cooling AC often gives no on-screen diagnostic. That means the cause has to be narrowed down by symptom and measurement. The range of causes runs from trivial (dirty filter) to expensive (dead compressor), so this page walks you through what's genuinely DIY and what requires a licensed technician with gauges and refrigerant certification.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — Restricted airflow starves the indoor coil, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil solid.
2. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — Ice on the coil from low airflow or low refrigerant blocks cooling entirely. You'll often see poor or no airflow.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — Grass, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection so the system can't dump heat outside.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — A weak or bulged capacitor keeps the compressor or condenser fan from starting. Very common failure.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — The system runs but can't cool; often shows ice, hissing, or oily residue at fittings.
6. **Faulty contactor** — Pitted or stuck contacts prevent the outdoor unit from energizing.
7. **Thermostat or wiring fault** — Miscalibration, dead batteries, or a broken 24V wire can stop a cooling call.
8. **Failed compressor** — The most expensive cause; the outdoor unit hums but won't start, or trips the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening a sealed refrigerant line or bypassing a switch.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the #1 fix.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser and gently hose off the outside fins (power off first).
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and shut cooling down. Check for standing water in the pan and clear a visible clog.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully — several hours — before restarting.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence, not guesswork. Here's what a fair diagnosis looks like:

1. **Verify the cooling call** — confirms the thermostat is sending 24V to the outdoor contactor.
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, coil, and blower amperage; measures temperature split across the indoor coil (typically 16–22°F on a healthy system).
3. **Test electrical components** — measures the run capacitor's microfarads against the rating printed on it, checks the contactor and compressor windings.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — reads suction and liquid pressures and superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct. Rheem's newer R-454B units require specific handling and different target pressures than older R-410A units.
5. **Leak search** — if low on charge, uses electronic or dye leak detection to find the source before recharging.
6. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw and windings if the unit won't start.

Ask for the measured numbers. A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or superheat reading is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with fan on | Verify airflow, clean coil |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw fully, replace filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Low refrigerant or bad compressor | None — call a pro | Gauge pressures, test compressor |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor or compressor | None | Test/replace capacitor or compressor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Contactor, breaker, or 24V fault | Reset breaker once | Test contactor and control wiring |
| Warm air, hissing/oily fittings | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak repair and recharge |
| House won't reach setpoint on hot days | Undersized charge or dirty condenser | Clean condenser fins | Verify charge and airflow |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your region and unit age affect them.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-454B costs more than R-410A)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often worth weighing against a full system replacement on older units)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180, frequently credited toward the repair

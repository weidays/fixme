## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on Rheem equipment — it's the symptom homeowners describe when the system runs but the house won't cool down, or when the outdoor unit won't start at all. Rheem central air conditioners (RA-series condensers paired with an indoor coil and air handler or furnace) don't display a numeric code for this the way a communicating EcoNet system might flash faults.

Instead, "not cooling" points to one of several underlying failures: airflow restriction, an electrical component failure at the condenser, a refrigerant problem, or a control/thermostat issue. Some of these you can rule out safely in a few minutes. Others require gauges, a meter, and EPA certification — meaning a pro visit. This page is marked **pro** because the most likely paid repairs (refrigerant, compressor, capacitor under load) involve high voltage and sealed systems.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can freeze the evaporator so no cold air moves. The #1 cause of "suddenly not cooling."
2. **Thermostat set wrong or dead batteries** — set to "fan" instead of "cool," or a low temperature the AC can't reach, or a battery-powered stat that lost power.
3. **Tripped breaker or disconnect** — the outdoor condenser has its own breaker and often a pull disconnect at the unit. If it's tripped, the compressor and fan won't run while the indoor blower still does.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common condenser failure. The fan or compressor hums, won't start, or the outdoor fan spins but the compressor doesn't.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, long run times, ice on the copper lines, and eventually a warm house. This is a repair, not a "recharge."
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low refrigerant or poor airflow; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the condenser burns or sticks, so the outdoor unit gets no power.
8. **Condenser fan motor failure** — the compressor overheats and shuts down on thermal overload without a working fan.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit trips or hums but never produces cold air.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner. Do not open the sealed condenser cabinet or touch capacitors — they store a dangerous charge.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Then set the system to fan-only for 2–3 hours if you saw ice on the coil or lines, to let it thaw.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the fan to **Auto**, and the setpoint several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor air-handler breaker and the separate outdoor AC breaker. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a tech.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Near the condenser is a pull-out disconnect box. Make sure it's fully seated. (Do not open the condenser panels.)
- **Vents and returns:** Confirm supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or dust.
- **Condensate:** A clogged drain can trip a float safety switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water at the indoor unit's drain pan; a full pan or a tripped float means call for a drain cleaning.
- **Outdoor coil:** Gently rinse leaves, grass, and dirt off the outside of the condenser fins with a garden hose (unit off). A caked coil can't reject heat.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheapest to most expensive, and a quote should reflect that order:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check power and controls** — confirms 24V call at the contactor, tests the capacitor with a meter, inspects the contactor for pitting.
3. **Check the condenser fan and compressor** — confirms both start and draw correct amperage.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — reads suction and head pressures against the charging chart. Low pressures indicate a leak or restriction.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before adding anything.
6. **Airflow verification** — checks blower operation and coil condition if freezing was reported.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or "replace the compressor" without confirming the capacitor and electrical are good first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor blower runs, air not cold | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter, run fan-only to thaw | Confirm charge and airflow |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker / disconnect | Reset breaker once, seat disconnect | Test contactor and 24V control |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — high voltage | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines | Low refrigerant / airflow | Thaw, replace filter | Leak search and repair, recharge |
| Weak cooling, long run times | Low refrigerant / dirty coil | Rinse outdoor coil | Gauge pressures, find leak |
| Unit trips breaker repeatedly | Compressor / electrical fault | Stop resetting, call pro | Amp draw test, component check |
| Water at indoor unit, no cooling | Clogged condensate / float switch | Check drain pan | Clear drain, reset float |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $5–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$300
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$650
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often the point where full replacement is considered on older units)

Diagnostic/service-call fees typically run $75–$200 and are often credited toward the repair.

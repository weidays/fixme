## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on Rheem equipment — it's a symptom. Your Rheem air conditioner may be running (the outdoor fan spins, air moves at the vents) yet the air feels warm, or the system runs constantly without ever reaching the thermostat setpoint.

Because this is a symptom rather than a stored code, there's no single blinking-light pattern that points to the cause. On systems with a communicating thermostat (Rheem EcoNet) you may see a status or comfort alert instead. What actually varies:

- **Older RA13/RA14 units** with a contactor and start capacitor tend to fail at the capacitor or contactor.
- **Newer RA16/RA17/RA20 units** may store faults on the control board or report them through EcoNet.
- If your outdoor unit has a **flashing fault light**, see the related guide below — that points to a diagnostic code, not just "not cooling."

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the most common cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor coil, which then blocks cooling entirely.
2. **Dirty condenser (outdoor) coil** — grass clippings, dust, and cottonwood insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a swollen or dead capacitor stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting; you may hear a hum.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs, so you get airflow but no cold air.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the copper line, and long run times. Always a leak, never "used up."
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; ice blocks heat transfer completely.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the compressor doesn't pull in.
8. **Bad thermostat, dead batteries, or wiring** — the system never gets the call to cool.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor overheats and shuts down on its internal protector.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive; the unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here — don't open the sealed refrigerant system or bypass any switch.

- **Filter:** Pull and inspect the filter. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Run the system with the new filter.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker for both the air handler and the outdoor unit. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (power off first).
- **Condensate drain:** If the indoor unit has a drain safety switch and the pan is full, a clogged drain can shut cooling down. Clear a visible clog and check the line runs freely.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF and the fan to ON for a few hours to thaw before calling — running it iced up makes things worse.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path — this is what you're paying for:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor contactor.
2. **Check line voltage and amp draw** at the compressor and fan motor to spot electrical failures.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter (a swollen cap or one reading out of spec gets replaced).
4. **Inspect the contactor** for pitted or stuck contacts.
5. **Take temperature split** — supply vs. return air. A weak split points to airflow or charge problems.
6. **Connect gauges** to read suction and head pressures and compare to the target superheat/subcooling for your model.
7. **Leak search** if charge is low — dye, electronic sniffer, or soap bubbles. A reputable tech won't just "top off."
8. **Coil inspection** for restriction or freezing.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without a pressure reading and a leak search, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, filter looks gray | Dirty filter | Replace filter, run system | None if fixed |
| Ice on copper lines / coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find cause, check charge/airflow |
| Outdoor fan won't spin, humming | Failed capacitor | Note the hum, kill power | Test and replace capacitor |
| Indoor blows, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker / contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Weak cooling, long runtimes | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit filthy | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean |
| Runs constantly, no cold air | Failed compressor | None — call a pro | Compressor test/replacement |
| Nothing turns on | Dead thermostat / batteries | Replace batteries, check settings | Diagnose thermostat/wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Regional labor rates vary widely.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV / metering device:** $500–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$1,800 (often worth weighing against a new condenser if out of warranty)

Rheem parts may be covered under the standard limited warranty (typically 10 years on registered systems), which can cut part costs — you'll usually still pay labor.

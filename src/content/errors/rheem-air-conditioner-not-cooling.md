## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you'll notice when a Rheem or Ruud central air conditioner runs but fails to lower your home's temperature. Unlike a heat pump flashing a diagnostic light, most standard split-system ACs don't display an error; you diagnose by observing what the equipment does.

The problem falls into a few buckets: the system won't turn on at all, the indoor blower runs but air is warm, the outdoor unit hums but won't start, or the system runs constantly and never reaches setpoint. Each points to a different root cause. On two-stage and variable-speed Rheem models (Prestige series), the control board or communicating thermostat may log a fault — check that display before assuming there's no code.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil. Most common and cheapest fix.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil ices over and stops absorbing heat.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood, it can't reject heat, so the system runs but doesn't cool.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bad capacitor stops the compressor or condenser fan; you may hear humming but no startup.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; requires EPA-certified leak repair and recharge, never just a "top-off."
6. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit or air handler loses power.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/setup fault.
8. **Failing condenser fan motor or contactor** — outdoor fan won't spin, causing overheat shutdown.
9. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive; the unit won't produce cold air at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if you'd need to open sealed refrigerant lines, bypass a safety switch, or access high-voltage components.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check the panel for a tripped breaker serving the outdoor unit and the air handler. Reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant line, switch the thermostat to OFF (or FAN ONLY) and let it thaw completely — several hours. Running frozen can damage the compressor.
- **Condensate drain:** If the indoor unit sits in a pan with a float switch and cooling stopped, a clogged drain may have tripped it. Clear standing water with a wet/dry vac at the drain line outlet.
- **Outdoor unit:** Gently clear leaves and debris from around the condenser and rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (power off). Do not use a pressure washer.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work in this order — use it to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat operation and settings.
2. **Confirm power and control voltage** at the disconnect, contactor, and low-voltage circuit; check the fuse on the board.
3. **Inspect airflow** — filter, coil, blower operation — and measure temperature split across the indoor coil (typically ~16–22°F on a healthy system).
4. **Test the capacitor** with a meter and check the contactor and compressor for proper amp draw.
5. **Attach gauges** to read suction/discharge pressures and superheat/subcooling — this tells them if refrigerant is low and whether there's a leak.
6. **Leak search** with an electronic detector or dye if charge is low.
7. **Confirm the diagnosis** before recommending a compressor or coil replacement — a tech should never sell a compressor on a hunch.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or "replace compressor" without pressure and electrical readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower runs, air is warm | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil on FAN ONLY | Check airflow, coil, refrigerant charge |
| Nothing turns on | Tripped breaker or dead thermostat | Reset breaker once; replace batteries | Test control voltage, board fuse, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None (high voltage) | Test and replace run/start capacitor |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant or dirty condenser | Rinse condenser fins with power off | Leak search, recharge, coil cleaning |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw fully, replace filter | Diagnose airflow vs. charge |
| Cooling stops, water in pan | Clogged condensate drain | Vacuum drain line | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Bad fan motor or capacitor | None (high voltage) | Replace fan motor or capacitor |
| No cold air at all, breaker holds | Compressor failure | None | Confirm with amp/pressure test; replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and by single-stage vs. two-stage/variable Rheem models.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 — DIY.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 pro.
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$300.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. R-32 on newer Rheem units).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ — often near the cost of a new condenser, so weigh replacement.

If your Rheem is under 12 years old, check for parts warranty coverage before authorizing a major part — Rheem's registered limited warranty may cover the component cost, leaving you labor only.

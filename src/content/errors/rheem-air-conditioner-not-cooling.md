## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on a display — it's the symptom Rheem homeowners report most often. Your air conditioner may be running (indoor blower and/or outdoor fan spinning) but the air coming from your vents is warm, lukewarm, or never gets the house down to the thermostat setpoint.

Because Rheem central AC units don't have a screen that shows a numeric "not cooling" code, the diagnosis depends on *what exactly* is or isn't happening: Is the outdoor unit running? Is the indoor blower running? Is there ice on the lines? These clues narrow the cause down considerably.

If your **heat pump** is showing a blinking indicator light instead, that's a different situation — see the Related codes section below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can eventually freeze the evaporator coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow (dirty filter/blower) or low refrigerant. Ice insulates the coil so it can't absorb heat.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan can't start. You may hear a hum or clicking outside with no fan spinning.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** (outdoor unit) — matted with grass, dust, or debris, it can't reject heat, so the house never cools.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Rheem systems don't "use up" refrigerant; low charge always means a leak. Requires EPA-certified repair.
6. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the compressor never energizes.
7. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or miswiring.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** at the disconnect.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive cause; the outdoor unit tries to start but overloads or won't run.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the checks any homeowner can do safely:

- **Filter:** Pull and inspect it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering it 3–4 degrees to force a call for cooling.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel and the outdoor disconnect. If a breaker is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Ice check:** Look at the copper refrigerant lines and indoor coil. If you see frost or ice, turn the system to **FAN ONLY** (or OFF) for a few hours to let it thaw, then run cooling again with a clean filter.
- **Condensate drain:** If the indoor unit shut off, a full drain pan or clogged condensate line may have tripped a float switch. Clear standing water and, if accessible, flush the drain line.
- **Outdoor coil:** Gently rinse leaves, grass clippings, and dirt off the outdoor unit's fins with a garden hose (power off first, spray from inside out if you can access it).

**Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass a float or safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Rheem tech follows a logical sequence — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the call for cooling** at the thermostat and checks 24V control voltage.
2. **Verifies airflow** — inspects filter, blower operation, and looks for a frozen coil.
3. **Checks the capacitor** with a multimeter (measuring microfarads against the rated value on the label).
4. **Inspects the contactor** for pitting and confirms it pulls in when cooling is called.
5. **Measures refrigerant pressures** with gauges and takes superheat/subcooling readings to determine if the charge is correct or if there's a leak.
6. **Performs a leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or bubble test) if the charge is low — a legitimate repair requires finding and fixing the leak, not just "topping off."
7. **Tests the compressor** for proper amp draw, windings, and starting.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or "replace the whole system" without diagnosing the specific failed component.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airflow weak, air lukewarm | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower & duct static |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Frozen coil (low airflow or refrigerant) | Fan-only to thaw, new filter | Diagnose airflow/charge cause |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan won't spin | Failed capacitor | None (electrical) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit totally silent | Contactor or breaker | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| House won't reach setpoint, warm air | Low refrigerant (leak) | None | Leak search & repair, recharge |
| Outdoor coil packed with debris | Dirty condenser | Rinse coil with hose | Deep coil cleaning |
| Nothing turns on at all | Thermostat / power | Check mode, batteries, breaker | Trace control wiring |
| Unit shut off, water in pan | Clogged condensate / float | Clear drain line | Clear line, test float switch |
| Outdoor unit tries then trips | Failing compressor | Stop resetting; call pro | Compressor test / replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $0–$30 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200 (or DIY)
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500+ (depends on leak location; evaporator coil leaks are the costly end)
- **Refrigerant recharge only (after leak fixed):** $150–$600 depending on refrigerant type and amount
- **Blower motor:** $400–$800
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often it's worth comparing to full system replacement, especially on older R-410A units)

If your unit is old and out of warranty and needs a compressor, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison. Rheem registered systems may still have parts coverage — have your model and serial number ready.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on Rheem air conditioners — it's a symptom. Rheem single-stage and two-stage ACs typically don't display alphanumeric error codes on the equipment itself the way a furnace board does. Instead, you notice the house won't reach setpoint, the air from the vents feels warm or only slightly cool, or the outdoor unit isn't running.

If you have a **Rheem communicating (EcoNet) thermostat or control**, you may see a system message or fault notification in the EcoNet app instead of a hardware code. On non-communicating setups, there's no readout — you diagnose by observation.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a $10 filter to a $2,500 compressor, this page walks you through what's safe to check yourself and how a pro narrows it down.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (Heat vs Cool), setpoint too high, or a failed thermostat/loose wiring.
3. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer so no cold air comes out.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler has lost power.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common electrical failure; the compressor and/or fan motor won't start or start intermittently.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the condenser never energizes.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces or eliminates cooling; always indicates a leak, never "normal" loss.
8. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil is caked with debris, so the system can't reject heat.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor unit overheats and shuts down on internal protection.
10. **Compressor failure** — least common but most expensive; the heart of the system won't pump refrigerant.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything feels beyond this list.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Run the system 30 minutes and recheck cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, fan on **Auto**, and the setpoint is at least 3-4°F below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (indoor air handler and outdoor unit often have separate breakers). Reset **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips mean a fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Ice on the lines or coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, **turn the system to Off but leave the fan On** for a few hours to thaw. Running a frozen system can damage the compressor.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch that shuts the system down. Check for standing water in the drain pan; a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain line clears many clogs.

Do **not** open the outdoor unit's electrical panel, touch the capacitor (it stores a dangerous charge even with power off), or attempt anything with refrigerant lines.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech works from cheapest and most likely toward expensive:

1. **Verifies the complaint** — measures supply vs return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15-20°F).
2. **Confirms airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Checks power and controls** — tests the thermostat call, 24V control voltage, contactor, and both line-voltage legs at the outdoor unit.
4. **Tests the capacitor** — measures microfarads against the rating printed on it; a weak cap is a top find.
5. **Reads refrigerant pressures** — gauges on both service ports, compared to the outdoor temperature, tell whether the charge and metering are correct.
6. **Leak search if charge is low** — with electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen; they should locate the leak, not just top off.
7. **Compressor and motor tests** — amp draw and windings if the outdoor unit hums but won't run.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without airflow and electrical checks first, ask questions.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak or warm airflow indoors | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with fan on | Clean coil, restore airflow, check charge |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries; check breaker | Test control voltage, replace thermostat |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor blows | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, trace power fault |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — capacitor holds charge | Test and replace capacitor |
| Fan spins, no cold air, ice on lines | Low refrigerant / airflow | Thaw system, replace filter | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor coil caked with dirt | Dirty condenser | Gently rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean, verify performance |
| System short-cycles or trips repeatedly | Electrical fault / compressor | Stop resetting; call pro | Diagnose amp draw, compressor test |
| Water around indoor unit, system off | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain, vacuum line | Clear line, replace float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10-$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150-$400+ for communicating/EcoNet models
- **Run capacitor:** $150-$350
- **Contactor:** $150-$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350-$650
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150-$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500-$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer R-454B)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000-$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200-$2,500 — at this point, weigh repair against replacing the unit, especially if it's over 10 years old

Prices vary by region, unit size, and refrigerant type. Get a second opinion on any quote above $1,000.

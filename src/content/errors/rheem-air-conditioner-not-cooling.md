## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a numeric fault code — it's the symptom Rheem homeowners describe most often. Your air conditioner may be running (outdoor fan spinning, indoor blower moving air) but the air coming from the vents feels lukewarm, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint on a hot day.

Because Rheem split systems (RA13 through RA20 series and matched air handlers) don't display a plain-English "not cooling" message, the cause has to be diagnosed from behavior. On units with a communicating control board or an Econet/Endeavor system, you may see a related fault indication — but the classic single-stage RA13/RA14 units simply run without producing cold air. What follows applies broadly across Rheem residential split systems, with notes where behavior varies by model.

If your outdoor unit shows a **flashing fault light**, see the related page below before continuing — that points to a specific board-detected fault.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops cooling capacity and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass clippings, cottonwood and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil ices over and airflow nearly stops.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — poor cooling, longer run times, possible ice. Never a "top-off" — the leak must be found and repaired.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan won't start reliably; sometimes only the fan runs.
6. **Tripped condensate float switch** — a full or clogged drain pan trips the safety, cutting cooling (often stops the whole system).
7. **Bad contactor** — the outdoor unit won't energize the compressor and fan.
8. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — miscalibration, dead batteries, or a loose C-wire.
9. **Compressor failure** — least common but most expensive; the outdoor unit hums or won't start.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are homeowner-safe. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

- **Filter:** Pull it. If it's gray or clogged, replace it and give the system an hour to recover.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and several degrees below room temp. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm it isn't in "fan only" mode.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor breakers. Reset **once** if tripped. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the exterior coil with a garden hose (not a pressure washer) with power off.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see standing water in the pan or a full drain line, a tripped float switch may be the cause. Clear a visible clog and add drain tablets. Don't disable the float switch.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully — often 1–3 hours — before running again.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheapest to most expensive, and you should hear this reflected in the quote:

1. **Verify airflow** — inspects filter, blower, return sizing and coil for ice or dirt.
2. **Measures temperature split** — return vs. supply air (typically 16–22°F on a healthy system). A low split points to charge or airflow issues.
3. **Checks capacitor and contactor** — tests capacitance against the microfarad rating printed on the cap; inspects contactor points.
4. **Reads refrigerant pressures and superheat/subcooling** — with gauges to confirm proper charge; a low reading triggers a leak search (electronic detector, dye or nitrogen).
5. **Tests the compressor** — windings, amp draw and start behavior.
6. **Confirms controls** — thermostat call, C-wire, float switch and (on communicating models) any stored board faults.

Ask the tech to show you the temperature split and any pressure readings. A quote for a compressor or refrigerant work without documented measurements is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air from vents is warm, unit runs | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Clean coil, check blower |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Frozen coil (airflow or charge) | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find cause, check charge/airflow |
| Weak cooling, long run times | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Failed capacitor/contactor | None — call a pro | Test and replace cap/contactor |
| Nothing runs, water in pan | Tripped float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, verify float |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Weak cap or bad compressor | None — call a pro | Test compressor, replace if failed |
| System won't turn on at all | Breaker / thermostat / C-wire | Reset breaker once, check batteries | Trace wiring, control fault |
| Dirty outdoor coil, poor cooling | Blocked condenser | Rinse coil, clear debris | Deep coil clean, comb fins |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Warranty coverage on Rheem parts (typically 10 years registered) can lower these significantly.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 DIY
- **Condensate drain clearing / tablets:** $0–$200
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Coil deep-clean (condenser or evaporator):** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$400
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $300–$1,600 depending on leak location (a leaking evaporator coil is the high end)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often makes replacement worth considering on older units)

If your unit uses R-22 refrigerant (older RA13-era systems), recharge costs run much higher due to phase-out pricing — factor that into any repair-vs-replace decision.

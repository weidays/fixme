## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific flashing fault code — it's the symptom most Rheem and Ruud air-conditioner owners describe when the system runs but the house stays warm. On Rheem equipment, cooling failures rarely light up a numeric error on the outdoor unit; instead the thermostat keeps calling, the indoor blower runs, and either the outdoor condenser doesn't engage or it runs without producing cold air.

Because Rheem uses several different control platforms across its lineup (basic single-stage boards on Classic units up to communicating EcoNet controls on Prestige/Endeavor systems), the exact behavior varies. Communicating systems may log a fault in the thermostat or EcoNet app, while older single-stage condensers simply run or stay silent with no diagnostic feedback. This page covers the general "won't cool" problem across those platforms.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so the system runs constantly without cooling.
3. **Tripped breaker or pulled outdoor disconnect** — the indoor blower runs on a separate circuit, so warm air blows while the condenser sits dead.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common Rheem failure; the compressor or condenser fan won't start, often with a humming sound.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the copper line, and long run times. Requires professional leak repair.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the condenser doesn't pull in, so the outdoor unit stays silent.
8. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a broken C-wire on communicating systems.
9. **Compressor failure** — the worst case; the unit tries to start but can't, or trips the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter alone can cause total cooling loss.
- **Breaker & disconnect:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker on the AC circuit, and the outdoor disconnect box near the condenser. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets can damage the compressor.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** Clear grass, leaves, and debris from around the condenser and gently rinse the outside of the coil fins with a garden hose (power off).
- **Condensate drain:** A full drain pan can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Look for standing water; a wet/dry vac on the drain line outlet can clear a clog.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully before running again. Persistent icing means a real problem — call a pro.

Do **not** open sealed refrigerant lines, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work in a logical order so you can sanity-check the visit:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, coil, and blower; a dirty evaporator or restricted airflow is ruled out first.
3. **Electrical checks** — test the capacitor with a meter, confirm the contactor pulls in, and verify voltage at the condenser.
4. **Refrigerant analysis** — connect gauges to read pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. This tells them if the charge is correct, low, or overcharged.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, a good tech finds the leak (electronic detector or dye) rather than just topping off.
6. **Compressor test** — measure amp draw and windings if the compressor won't start or trips the breaker.

Be cautious of any quote to "add a pound of Freon" without a leak search — that's a temporary fix, not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, bad contactor, or capacitor | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test/replace contactor or capacitor |
| Outdoor fan runs but no cold air | Failed compressor capacitor or low refrigerant | Replace filter, rinse coil | Test capacitor; check charge & leaks |
| Weak cooling, long run times | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Clean coil, replace filter | Measure pressures, find leak |
| Ice on copper lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Diagnose airflow or charge issue |
| Water around indoor unit, system shuts off | Clogged condensate drain, float switch | Clear drain line | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Breaker trips every time it starts | Shorted compressor or wiring fault | Stop resetting; call pro | Test compressor windings & wiring |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries or C-wire fault | Replace batteries | Repair wiring / control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor). Actual prices vary by region and contractor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Breaker reset / vent clearing:** $0 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200 (or DIY)
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 (depends on leak location)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

If your Rheem system is under the standard 10-year parts warranty (with registration), the part may be covered — but you'll still pay labor and refrigerant.

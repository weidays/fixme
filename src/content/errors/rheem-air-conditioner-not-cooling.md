## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific Rheem fault code — it's the symptom homeowners describe when the system runs but the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents feels warm. On EcoNet-enabled Rheem systems, the thermostat or app may log a related alert (low-charge, high pressure, or communication faults), but many older Rheem/Ruud units simply keep running with no code at all.

Because "not cooling" can be caused by anything from a $20 filter to a $2,500 compressor, the goal of this page is to help you rule out the cheap, safe stuff first and then understand what a technician should be checking so you can sanity-check the quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or restricted airflow** — the single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the indoor coil, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. A coil covered in ice can't absorb heat, so vents blow slightly cool then warm.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common outdoor failure. The compressor and/or fan hum or won't start; the unit runs but doesn't cool.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so cooling drops sharply on hot days.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Rheem systems are sealed. Low charge means a leak, not "topping off." Symptoms: long run times, ice, warm air.
6. **Tripped breaker or outdoor disconnect off** — the outdoor unit is silent while the indoor blower still runs, so you get airflow with no cooling.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit pits or sticks, so the compressor never energizes.
8. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/communication fault (common on EcoNet setups).
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor runs but overheats and shuts down on high pressure without airflow across the coil.
10. **Failed compressor or control board** — least common but most expensive; the heart of the system no longer pumps.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If none resolve it, call a licensed HVAC tech.

- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL and the temperature is below room temp. Set the fan to AUTO. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Replace or clean the air filter.** A clogged filter is the #1 cause. Swap in a clean one and give the system 30 minutes.
- **Check both breakers.** Central AC uses two: one for the indoor air handler/furnace and one for the outdoor unit. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the outdoor disconnect.** The pull-out box on the wall near the condenser can be switched off. Make sure it's fully seated.
- **Look and listen at the outdoor unit.** Is the fan spinning? Total silence usually means power, capacitor, or contactor issues.
- **Check for ice.** If you see frost or ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw for a few hours before running again — then replace the filter.
- **Rinse the outdoor coil.** With the unit off, gently hose the outside of the condenser coil (top to bottom) to clear debris. Don't use a pressure washer.
- **Clear the condensate drain.** A backed-up drain can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Confirm the drain line isn't clogged and the pan isn't full.

**Do not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, bypass the float switch or any safety, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech follows a logical airflow-then-refrigerant path. Expect them to:

1. **Confirm the complaint and airflow** — check filter, blower operation, and return/supply temperatures (the "delta-T"). A healthy split is roughly 15–20°F.
2. **Inspect for a frozen coil** and identify whether it's airflow or charge related.
3. **Measure electrical components** — test the run capacitor with a meter (not by eye), check the contactor, and verify voltage at the compressor and fan.
4. **Connect gauges and take readings** — superheat and subcooling tell them whether the charge is correct. Low charge points to a leak.
5. **Leak search if charge is low** — with electronic detector or dye. A legitimate shop repairs the leak; they don't just refill it.
6. **Check the compressor and fan motor** windings and amp draw if the unit won't start.
7. **On EcoNet systems**, pull stored alerts from the thermostat/app and check communication between the indoor and outdoor boards.

Ask for the measured delta-T, capacitor reading, and refrigerant pressures. A quote that jumps straight to "you need refrigerant" or "you need a compressor" without these measurements deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vents blow warm, unit runs | Dirty filter / airflow | Replace filter, wait 30 min | Measure delta-T, inspect blower |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Leak search, verify charge |
| Outdoor unit silent | Breaker/disconnect off, capacitor | Reset breaker once, check disconnect | Test capacitor, contactor, board |
| Fan spins, no cool air | Failed capacitor or compressor | None — call a pro | Test capacitor, check compressor amps |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant leak | None — call a pro | Superheat/subcool, leak repair |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Fan motor or capacitor | Turn off to prevent damage | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Cooling drops on hot days | Dirty condenser coil | Hose coil gently with unit off | Deep coil cleaning, charge check |
| Thermostat blank/wrong mode | Dead batteries, settings | Replace batteries, set to COOL | Check wiring/EcoNet comms |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your region, unit size, and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer R-454B) affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Coil cleaning (condenser or evaporator):** $75–$300.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200.
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$500 (more for EcoNet-compatible units).
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location.
- **TXV / metering device:** $400–$800.
- **Control board (EcoNet or standard):** $300–$700.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing to a full outdoor unit replacement).

If the system is older than 12–15 years and needs a compressor, ask whether replacement makes more financial sense — especially with the refrigerant phase-out driving up R-410A costs.

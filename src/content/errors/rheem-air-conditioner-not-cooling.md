## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific Rheem fault code. Your Rheem air conditioner may be running — the outdoor fan spins and air moves from the vents — but the air isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. In other cases the outdoor unit won't start at all.

Rheem AC systems have three jobs happening at once: the indoor blower moves air across a cold evaporator coil, the compressor pumps refrigerant, and the outdoor condenser rejects heat. When any one of these fails or is restricted, you lose cooling. Because most residential Rheem ACs don't display alphanumeric fault codes on the equipment itself (unlike some furnaces or inverter/communicating systems), you diagnose by behavior and measurements.

If you have a Prestige communicating system with an EcoNet thermostat, the app or thermostat may show a fault or alert — note that message, as it narrows the cause considerably.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer so warm air blows.
3. **Dirty condenser coil** — a clogged outdoor coil can't reject heat, so cooling drops off, especially on hot days.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces capacity and can freeze the indoor coil. Always indicates a leak.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or fan hums or won't start; a very common electrical failure.
6. **Failed contactor** — the outdoor unit gets no power to start even when the thermostat calls.
7. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or wiring/programming fault.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor disconnect fuse or panel breaker off.
9. **Failed compressor** — the unit runs but produces no cooling; the most expensive failure.
10. **Control board / inverter fault** — on Prestige and inverter models, a board fault can stop cooling; usually paired with an EcoNet alert.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do not open the electrical compartment, touch refrigerant lines, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

- **Filter:** Pull the air filter. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (indoor air handler and outdoor unit may be on separate breakers). Reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Ice check:** Look at the copper lines and indoor coil access if visible. If you see ice, turn the system to OFF (fan ON is fine) and let it thaw fully — often several hours — before running again.
- **Condensate:** Check the condensate drain and pan. A clogged drain with a float switch will shut the system off. Clear standing water and check the drain line outlet.
- **Outdoor unit:** Confirm the fan is spinning and the coil isn't buried in leaves, grass, or debris. Keep at least two feet of clearance.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work methodically — this list helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirm the thermostat is sending a Y signal to the outdoor unit.
2. **Check line voltage and control voltage** — at the disconnect, contactor, and 24V circuit.
3. **Test the capacitor** — with a multimeter against its rated microfarad value.
4. **Inspect and test the contactor** — for pitting and proper pull-in.
5. **Measure superheat and subcooling** — by connecting gauges to read refrigerant charge and system performance. This is the key step for diagnosing low charge, restrictions, or a failing compressor.
6. **Check the temperature split** — the difference between return and supply air (typically 15–22°F when healthy).
7. **Inspect coils** — both evaporator and condenser for dirt, ice, or restriction.
8. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, using electronic detector or dye.
9. **Read board/EcoNet faults** — on communicating Prestige systems.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "top off refrigerant" without finding the leak — that's not a repair, it's a temporary and EPA-discouraged patch.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air from vents not cold, system running | Dirty filter or low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Check airflow, coil, charge |
| Ice on copper lines or indoor coil | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn off, thaw fully, change filter | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit hums but fan won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — leave it off | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, no fan | Breaker/fuse, contactor, or board | Check breaker/disconnect once | Test contactor, fuse, board |
| Runs constantly, never reaches setpoint | Dirty condenser or undercharge | Clear debris, clean coil gently | Measure charge, clean coil |
| Warm air, compressor runs | Failed compressor or lost charge | None | Diagnose compressor/refrigerant |
| System shuts off shortly after starting | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain line and pan | Clear drain, verify switch |
| EcoNet shows an alert | Board or sensor fault | Note the message | Read fault, test board/sensors |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and unit size.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Control board (inverter/communicating models):** $400–$900
- **Fan motor replacement:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth weighing against a new system)

If your unit uses R-22 refrigerant (older RAKA/RAND-era systems), leak repairs get expensive fast because R-22 is phased out and costly — replacement is often the smarter spend.

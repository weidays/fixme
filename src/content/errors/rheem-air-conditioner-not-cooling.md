## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific flashing fault code on Rheem equipment — it's the symptom homeowners describe when the system runs but the house won't reach setpoint, or the air from the vents feels warm. On EcoNet-enabled Rheem systems you may see a temperature-differential alert or a communicating fault in the app, but most standard Rheem condensers (RA13/RA14/RA16) have no digital display at all, so you're diagnosing by behavior.

The underlying problem falls into one of three buckets: **airflow** (filter, coil, blower), **refrigerant** (low charge, leak, restriction), or **electrical** (capacitor, contactor, compressor, thermostat/control board). Whether the outdoor fan and compressor run is the single biggest clue, so note that before you call anyone.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The #1 cause and the first thing every tech checks.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice on the coil or the outdoor line set blocks cooling entirely.
3. **Low refrigerant charge / leak** — undercharged systems run constantly without cooling. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means a leak.
4. **Failed dual run capacitor** — a very common failure. The condenser fan may spin while the compressor stays silent, or nothing outside runs.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — a mud-and-grass-caked outdoor coil can't reject heat, so the house stays warm even though everything runs.
6. **Faulty contactor** — the relay that powers the compressor and fan can pit or fail to pull in.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/C-wire fault (varies by thermostat model).
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Failing compressor** — the least likely but most expensive; often preceded by hard starts, breaker trips, or loud hum.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it (usually 16x25x1 or 20x25x1 — check your air handler's printed size).
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips point to a real fault.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines at the outdoor unit or on the indoor coil, turn the system to OFF and the fan to ON for 1–3 hours to thaw it. Running a frozen system makes it worse.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or a tripped float switch can shut the system down. Check for standing water and clear a visible clog at the drain line.
- **Outdoor unit:** With the disconnect pulled, gently rinse leaves, grass, and dirt off the outside of the condenser coil with a garden hose (low pressure, from inside out if you can).

Do **not** open the electrical compartment to test capacitors, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch — those steps belong to a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent HVAC tech should:

1. **Confirm the complaint** — measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F). A low split confirms a cooling problem.
2. **Check airflow** — inspect the filter, blower wheel, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Test electrical** — measure the run capacitor's microfarads against its rating, check the contactor, and verify voltage at the compressor and fan.
4. **Read refrigerant** — connect gauges to check suction/head pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to confirm proper charge.
5. **Leak-search if low** — using an electronic detector or dye before recharging; adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.
6. **Inspect the compressor** — amp draw and windings if all else checks out.

Ask for the measured temperature split and refrigerant readings. A quote to "just add refrigerant" without a leak search is worth questioning.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Clean blower/coil |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cooling | Failed capacitor / compressor | None — call pro | Test & replace capacitor or compressor |
| Nothing runs outside | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, trace fault |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant / leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| House warm, unit filthy outside | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil exterior | Deep chemical coil clean |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries / C-wire | Replace batteries | Diagnose wiring/board |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US pricing including parts and labor; your area and unit size vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0–$10 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 installed
- **Coil cleaning (condenser or evaporator):** $150–$400
- **Run/fan capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A units cost more)
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often worth weighing against replacing the unit if out of warranty)

Many Rheem parts carry a 5–10 year limited parts warranty if the unit was registered — confirm your coverage before paying for parts.

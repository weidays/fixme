## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on your Rheem air conditioner — it's the symptom you notice: the system runs (or tries to) but the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents isn't cold. Rheem split systems generally don't display a plain-English "not cooling" message. Instead, you'll see the equipment behave in one of a few ways:

- The indoor blower runs but the outdoor condenser never starts.
- Both units run but the air is only slightly cool or room temperature.
- The system short-cycles (turns on and off repeatedly).
- Ice forms on the indoor coil or the copper line at the outdoor unit.

On communicating Rheem systems (Prestige/EcoNet), you may see a fault or status message on the thermostat instead. On older single-stage RA13/RA14 units, there's no diagnostics display at all — you're reading behavior and airflow.

This page covers the general "runs but won't cool" problem. If your outdoor **heat pump** unit has a flashing fault light, see the related page below instead.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow.** The single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops capacity and can freeze the coil, killing cooling entirely.
2. **Frozen indoor evaporator coil.** Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant. Once iced up, no cold air moves.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor.** A weak or dead capacitor stops the compressor or fan motor from starting — the outdoor unit hums or does nothing.
4. **Bad contactor.** The relay that powers the outdoor unit can pit or stick, preventing the condenser from energizing.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** Reduced cooling, longer run times, and sometimes ice. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means a leak.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse.** Kills power to the condenser or the control board.
7. **Clogged condensate drain / tripped float switch.** A full drain pan safety switch will shut down cooling to prevent water damage.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor.** The compressor may run but overheat and shut down without airflow across the coil.
9. **Thermostat problem or dead batteries.** Miscommunication or a blank thermostat stops the call for cooling.
10. **Compressor failure.** Least common but most expensive — the compressor won't start or has lost pumping ability.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If they don't fix it, call a licensed HVAC tech.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and at least 3–5°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm the fan is set to AUTO.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker for the air handler and the outdoor unit. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — a repeat trip means an electrical fault.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Confirm it's not smothered by leaves, grass, or bushes. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance. Do **not** stick anything into the fan or open the electrical cover.
- **Condensate:** If you have a drain pan with a float switch and see standing water, a clog may have shut the system down. You can clear a visible clog and empty the pan, but leave switch wiring to a pro.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the refrigerant line, turn the system to OFF and set the fan to ON to thaw it (can take a few hours). Running an iced unit can damage the compressor.

**Do not** add refrigerant, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint and airflow.** They'll check the filter, blower operation, and static pressure, and confirm the thermostat is calling for cooling.
2. **Check for ice.** A frozen coil is thawed before further testing, because you can't take accurate readings on an iced system.
3. **Electrical test at the condenser.** They measure incoming voltage, then test the **capacitor** (with a meter), the **contactor**, and the low-voltage 24V signal and fuse.
4. **Amp draw on compressor and fan motor.** High or zero amps points to a failing motor or compressor.
5. **Refrigerant readings.** Using gauges, they check suction/head pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low charge with a specific pattern indicates a leak, prompting a leak search (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen).
6. **Temperature split.** They measure the difference between return and supply air (typically ~16–22°F on a healthy system) to confirm actual cooling capacity.

A trustworthy quote names the specific failed part and the test result — not just "it needs freon."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower, static pressure |
| Ice on coil or line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, fan ON to thaw | Find airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Indoor runs, outdoor dead | Contactor or capacitor | Check breaker once | Test/replace contactor or capacitor |
| Outdoor hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None safe | Replace capacitor, verify amps |
| Cools poorly, long runs | Low refrigerant (leak) | None safe | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| System shut off, water in pan | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear visible clog, empty pan | Flush drain, test float switch |
| Compressor runs, no cold air | Compressor lost capacity | None safe | Full refrigerant + compressor test |
| Blank thermostat | Dead batteries or 24V fuse | Replace batteries | Test transformer and fuse |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Your area, unit age, and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22) all move these numbers.

- **Air filter:** $0–$30 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY).
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condensate drain clear + float switch:** $100–$300.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-22 recharges are far pricier).
- **TXV / metering device:** $400–$800.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000+.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+, at which point replacing the whole condenser or system is often the better value on an older unit.

If your unit is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, get a second opinion and a replacement quote before committing.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific Rheem fault code — it is the symptom you notice when the system runs but the air coming out of the registers is warm, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On Rheem equipment with a diagnostic board (such as Prestige communicating systems or units paired with an EcoNet or Comfort Control thermostat), the actual cause may show as a fault light or an error on the thermostat display. On older single-stage Rheem units there is often no code at all — just poor performance.

Because "not cooling" can stem from airflow, electrical, or refrigerant problems, the goal here is to separate the safe DIY checks from the issues that legitimately require a licensed HVAC technician (severity: **pro**). Anything involving refrigerant, the compressor, or hard-wired electrical work is not a homeowner job.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — the single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the evaporator coil of airflow, which drops capacity and can eventually freeze the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow, low refrigerant, or a dirty coil. A frozen coil will blow warm air and often drip water near the indoor unit.
3. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil packed with grass, dust or cottonwood can't reject heat, so cooling collapses on hot days.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure. The outdoor fan or compressor won't start (you may hear humming or clicking).
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity and can freeze the coil. This requires leak repair, not just a "top-off."
6. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
7. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode (Fan vs. Cool), dead batteries, or a miswired/failing thermostat.
8. **Contactor failure** — the outdoor contactor that energizes the compressor is pitted or stuck.
9. **Compressor failure** — least common but most expensive; often the last diagnosis after others are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform. Do them in order.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on Auto. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 cause.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure registers are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs, and the return grille isn't clogged.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for grass clippings, leaves or debris packed against the condenser. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (unit off) if they're visibly dirty.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines at the outdoor unit or water pooling indoors, **turn the system to Off (fan can stay On)** and let it thaw for several hours before running again.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut cooling down. Check for standing water in the drain pan; a wet/dry vac on the drain line outlet can clear a clog.

Do **not** open sealed electrical compartments, touch the capacitor, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech follows a logical sequence, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil condition.
3. **Test electrical** — measure the run/start capacitor with a meter, check the contactor, and confirm the outdoor unit is getting the correct voltage.
4. **Read the board/thermostat** — on Prestige/EcoNet systems, pull stored fault codes.
5. **Connect refrigerant gauges** — check pressures and superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct. Low readings prompt a leak search (dye, electronic sniffer, or nitrogen).
6. **Test the compressor** — check windings and amp draw if it won't start.

A legitimate diagnosis should end with a specific failed part, not just "needs Freon." If a tech proposes adding refrigerant, they should also identify and repair the leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, house won't cool | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Clean coil, check blower |
| Ice on lines, water indoors | Frozen coil | Turn off, let thaw, replace filter | Find airflow or refrigerant cause |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker / capacitor / contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Humming, outdoor fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor |
| Cools weakly on hot days | Dirty condenser or low charge | Rinse outdoor coil | Check charge, repair leak |
| Cooling stopped, pan full of water | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear drain line | Clear line, verify float |
| No response at all | Thermostat / power | Check mode, batteries, breaker | Diagnose wiring/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and after-hours rates vary):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (depends on leak location; evaporator coil leaks cost more)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$1,800+ (often makes replacement of an older unit worth considering)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $85–$200, frequently applied toward the repair

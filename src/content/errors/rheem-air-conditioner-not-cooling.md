## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Most Rheem and Ruud split-system air conditioners don't display alphanumeric error codes on the outdoor unit itself. Instead, the outdoor control board (on some models) may flash an LED status pattern, or the fault shows up only at a communicating thermostat on Prestige-series equipment.

What you're seeing is one of a few scenarios:

- The system runs (fan and/or compressor) but the air from the vents isn't cold.
- The indoor blower runs but the outdoor unit never starts.
- Nothing runs at all when the thermostat calls for cooling.

Each points to a different root cause, and the fix ranges from a free filter change to a compressor replacement. This page helps you narrow it down before spending money.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricted airflow is the single most common cooling complaint. It can freeze the indoor coil and choke output.
2. **Dirty condenser coil / blocked outdoor unit** — Grass clippings, leaves, or a mud film on the outdoor coil kills heat rejection, so the system runs constantly but barely cools.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — If the outdoor unit is dead, a tripped double-pole breaker or a blown 3-5A control fuse is a frequent culprit.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — A common failure. The compressor or condenser fan hums, struggles, or won't start. Inexpensive part, must be replaced by someone who can safely discharge it.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Weak cooling, ice on the copper lines, longer run times. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge always means a leak.
6. **Failed contactor** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit burns or pits, so the compressor never engages.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Often a downstream symptom of low airflow (dirty filter) or low refrigerant. The coil ices over and blocks cooling.
8. **Failed compressor** — The most expensive outcome. The unit tries to start, trips the breaker, or is silent with power present.
9. **Thermostat fault or wiring issue** — A dead thermostat, dead batteries, or a broken C-wire can stop the cooling call entirely.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do not open the outdoor unit's electrical panel or touch the capacitor.

- **Filter:** Pull the air filter. If it's gray or clogged, replace it and give the system 30 minutes to recover.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and at least 5°F below room temperature. If it's battery-powered, install fresh batteries. Confirm the display is on and calling for cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker serving the AC (usually a double-pole 30-50A) and the indoor air handler. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping means a real fault.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around and on top of the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (water only, low pressure) if it's visibly dirty.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear the line if you're comfortable doing so.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the copper refrigerant lines or the indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully — several hours — before restarting.

If cooling doesn't return after these steps, it's time for a licensed technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — Measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16-22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — Inspects filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Confirm the cooling call** — Verifies 24V at the thermostat and contactor to isolate control vs. mechanical problems.
4. **Test electrical components** — Reads the capacitor's microfarads against the rating, checks the contactor, and measures compressor amp draw.
5. **Check refrigerant charge** — Connects gauges and reads pressures/superheat/subcooling. Low readings trigger a leak search (electronic detector or dye), not just a "top-off."
6. **Assess the compressor** — Tests windings and start behavior if it won't run or trips the breaker.
7. **Inspect the LED status light (if equipped)** — On boards that flash codes, the tech reads the pattern against the wiring diagram on the panel door.

Insist on a leak search before agreeing to any refrigerant recharge. Refilling without fixing the leak wastes money and violates EPA rules.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but isn't cold | Dirty filter or dirty condenser coil | Replace filter, rinse outdoor coil | Deep coil clean, airflow test |
| Weak cooling, ice on line set | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, let coil thaw | Leak search, seal, recharge |
| Outdoor unit humming, won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None — do not open panel | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit completely dead | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace fuse/contactor, find cause |
| Indoor coil iced over | Low airflow or low charge | Filter change, thaw system | Diagnose airflow vs. refrigerant |
| Breaker trips on cooling call | Compressor or wiring short | Do not keep resetting | Compressor/electrical diagnosis |
| Nothing runs, thermostat blank | Dead batteries or C-wire fault | Replace batteries | Repair thermostat wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Actual prices vary by region and whether the unit is in warranty.

- **Air filter:** $10-$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75-$250
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75-$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150-$400
- **Contactor:** $150-$350
- **Low-voltage fuse:** $75-$150
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600-$1,600 (higher for R-22 systems or evaporator coil leaks)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900-$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500-$2,500+ (often points toward full replacement on older units)
- **Full system replacement:** $5,000-$12,000+

If your Rheem uses R-22 refrigerant (typically pre-2010 units) and needs a coil or compressor, replacement is usually the better value — R-22 is phased out and very expensive.

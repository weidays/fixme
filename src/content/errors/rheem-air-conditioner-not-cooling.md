## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you'll notice at the thermostat and vents. Your Rheem air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, air moving) but the air is warm, or the outdoor unit may not start at all. Rheem systems with an EcoNet communicating control may show a related fault or error on the thermostat display; older contactor-based units give no code and require inspection.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, this page walks you from the cheapest, safest checks up to the diagnostics a technician performs. Cooling problems that involve refrigerant, electrical components, or the compressor are **pro-level** repairs.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the most common and cheapest cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor coil and stop cooling.
2. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power, so only the indoor blower runs.
3. **Failed dual run capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor and/or condenser fan can't start. You may hear a hum or clicking.
4. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer and warm air comes out.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cooling gradually weakens; requires a licensed tech to locate the leak and recharge.
6. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the outdoor fan won't spin, the unit overheats and shuts down on a high-pressure limit.
7. **Contactor failure** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, preventing start.
8. **Thermostat or control-board fault** — wrong settings, dead batteries, or on EcoNet systems a communication error.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit runs but produces no cold air, or trips on start.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed system or touching electrical components.

- **Thermostat:** Set to COOL and the setpoint several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Confirm the fan is set to AUTO, not just ON.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of poor or no cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals a real fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from the condenser coil. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance around it.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper lines, turn the system to OFF and the fan to ON. Let it thaw fully (a few hours) before restarting.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut cooling down. Empty a full pan and clear a visible clog.

Do **not** open the condenser electrical panel, discharge a capacitor, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch — those are technician tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence. Knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the complaint** — verifies thermostat call for cooling and measures supply/return temperature split (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Checks power and controls** — confirms 240V at the disconnect and 24V control signal to the contactor.
3. **Tests the capacitor** — measures microfarads against the rating printed on it; a weak cap is replaced.
4. **Inspects the contactor and fan motor** — looks for pitted contacts and confirms the condenser fan runs.
5. **Reads refrigerant pressures** — attaches gauges and checks against manufacturer superheat/subcooling targets. Low readings point to a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before recharging (topping off alone is not a repair).
7. **Checks the coils** — inspects the evaporator and condenser for dirt, ice, or restriction.
8. **On EcoNet systems** — reads any stored fault codes from the communicating control.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower runs, air is warm | Outdoor unit not powered | Check/reset outdoor breaker once | Test contactor, capacitor, wiring |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Failed compressor or low refrigerant | None | Read pressures, test compressor |
| Humming outdoor unit, no start | Failed run capacitor | None (do not open panel) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter, thaw with fan ON | Find airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Outdoor fan won't spin | Failed condenser fan motor | Clear debris, confirm power | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Weak cooling that got worse | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| System short-cycles | Dirty coil, low refrigerant, control fault | Clean condenser, replace filter | Diagnose pressures/controls |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, thermostat | Check breaker, thermostat batteries | Test transformer, board, fuse |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0
- **Dual run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing has risen)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often worth comparing against a full condenser or system replacement, especially on older units)
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180, often credited toward the repair

If your Rheem unit is more than 12–15 years old and faces a compressor or coil failure, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison before committing.

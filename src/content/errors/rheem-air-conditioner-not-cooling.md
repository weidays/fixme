## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on a Rheem air conditioner — it's a symptom. Most Rheem/Ruud straight-cool split systems don't have a diagnostic display on the outdoor unit; they rely on the thermostat, the indoor air handler board, and physical component behavior to tell the story.

In practice, "not cooling" means one of three things is happening:

- **No airflow** — the blower or ductwork isn't moving conditioned air.
- **Airflow but no cold** — the compressor or refrigerant circuit isn't removing heat.
- **The outdoor unit isn't starting** — a capacitor, contactor, control signal, or safety switch is stopping the compressor and fan.

Because the root causes range from a $20 filter to a $2,500 compressor, the goal below is to help you rule out the cheap, safe stuff first and understand exactly what a technician should be checking before they quote you.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection, so the AC runs but barely cools.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "on" vs. "auto"), or a miswired/failing thermostat.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or condenser fan hums but won't start, or trips the breaker.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, long run times, and sometimes ice on the copper line. Requires an EPA-certified tech.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks the coil and stops cooling entirely.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor disconnect fuse or the low-voltage transformer fuse on the air handler board.
8. **Contactor failure** — the outdoor relay that powers the compressor is pitted, stuck, or not pulling in.
9. **Blower motor or capacitor failure** — no indoor airflow even though the thermostat calls for cooling.
10. **Compressor failure** — the worst case: the unit is seized, grounded, or has failed windings.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop at anything that requires opening the sealed system or bypassing a safety.

- **Filter:** Pull the indoor filter. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it and run the system for an hour.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Cool** and the fan to **Auto**, then lower the setpoint 3–5°F below room temperature. If it's a battery thermostat, install fresh batteries.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) breaker and the outdoor AC breaker. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, leave it off and call a pro — repeated trips mean an electrical fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure supply registers are open and unblocked and the return grille isn't covered by furniture or a rug.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, weeds, and debris at least 2 feet around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (spray straight in, low pressure) with the power **off** at the disconnect.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch. A clogged condensate line can shut the system down on many air handlers. Clear standing water and debris at the drain if you can reach it safely.
- **Check for ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** but leave the **fan On** to thaw it. Do not run cooling on a frozen coil.

If cooling is still weak or absent after fresh filter, correct thermostat settings, clean coil, and a full thaw — it's a pro job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Rheem tech works systematically. Use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirms 24V signal (Y terminal) is reaching the outdoor contactor.
2. **Measure airflow and temperature split** — checks the delta-T across the indoor coil (typically ~16–22°F on a healthy system).
3. **Inspect the capacitor and contactor** — tests microfarad rating on the run capacitor and checks the contactor for pitting or failure to pull in.
4. **Check amp draw** — measures compressor and fan motor current against the nameplate (RLA/FLA) to spot a struggling or locked compressor.
5. **Connect refrigerant gauges** — reads suction and head pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to judge charge. **Low charge means a leak** — they should offer a leak search, not just a top-off.
6. **Inspect the coils and blower** — confirms airflow isn't the hidden root cause of icing or weak cooling.

Be cautious if a tech jumps straight to "you just need refrigerant" without checking airflow, the capacitor, and for a leak. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up" — if it's low, something is leaking.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter or blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents | Check blower, duct static pressure |
| Runs constantly, barely cool | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse outdoor coil, clear debris | Deep coil clean, check charge |
| Ice on lines/indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off cooling, run fan to thaw | Find airflow issue or refrigerant leak |
| Outdoor fan/compressor hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — capacitors hold charge | Test and replace capacitor |
| No indoor airflow at all | Blower motor/capacitor or board | Check indoor breaker | Test blower, transformer fuse, board |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace fuse/contactor, find fault |
| Long run times, warm supply | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — EPA-regulated | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Breaker trips on start | Shorted/grounded compressor or wiring | Leave off, don't re-reset | Megohm test, compressor evaluation |
| Water around indoor unit | Clogged condensate/float switch | Clear drain if reachable | Clear line, verify float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor. Actual pricing varies by region and unit size (tonnage).

- **Air filter:** $15–$40 (DIY).
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250 as part of a service call.
- **Thermostat batteries:** under $10 (DIY).
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condensate line clearing / float switch:** $100–$250.
- **Blower motor:** $400–$700 installed.
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location.
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $200–$600 depending on refrigerant type and amount (R-410A/R-454B).
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500; often the point where replacing the whole condenser or system makes more sense on older units.

If your Rheem AC is out of warranty and the compressor has failed, get a quote for a full system replacement too — a new compressor in a 12+ year old unit is rarely worth it.

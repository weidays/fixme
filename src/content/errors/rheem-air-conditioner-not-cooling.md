## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single fault code on Rheem equipment — it's a symptom. Your Rheem air conditioner may be running (the outdoor unit hums, the indoor blower moves air) but the air coming from the vents is warm, lukewarm, or simply not cold enough to satisfy the thermostat.

On EcoNet-enabled Rheem systems, the thermostat or app may display a specific fault or communication code instead. On older Classic and Classic Plus units without smart controls, there's no diagnostic display at all — you just feel the problem. Because "not cooling" can stem from anything from a $20 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you from the cheapest, safest checks up to what only a licensed HVAC tech should handle.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, cutting cooling capacity and sometimes freezing the coil solid.
2. **Dirty condenser coil / blocked outdoor unit** — dirt, grass clippings, or debris block heat rejection so the system can't dump heat outside.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks airflow and warm air comes out the vents.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the fan or compressor tries to start but can't; you may hear humming or clicking.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system runs constantly but never cools. Requires a certified tech.
6. **Thermostat problem or wrong setting** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a failed C-wire connection.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — one leg of power lost, so the outdoor unit or blower won't run.
8. **Failed contactor or control board** — the outdoor unit never energizes.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit runs but doesn't compress refrigerant.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of poor cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the fan is on **AUTO**, and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets are dangerous.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure the condenser isn't buried in leaves, grass, or debris. Clear at least 2 feet of space around it. Gently rinse the fins with a garden hose (power off) if they look dirty.
- **Supply vents & returns:** Confirm they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** If the indoor unit is near a full drip pan or you see water, a clogged condensate line may have tripped a safety float switch and shut the system down. Drain tablets can help maintain a clean line.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** for a few hours to thaw it, then restart. If it re-freezes, you need a technician.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass safety switches, or add refrigerant.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is ~16–22°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — filter, coil, blower speed.
3. **Inspects electrical** — tests the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, verifies voltage at the outdoor unit.
4. **Reads refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to check for low charge, then confirms **superheat and subcooling** to judge whether the charge is correct or a leak exists.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak (a quality tech won't just top off and leave).
6. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw and windings if the compressor is suspect.

Be wary of any tech who wants to add refrigerant without first finding a leak. That's a temporary fix that costs you again next season.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, filter dark | Clogged filter | Replace filter | — |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw with fan | Find airflow or leak cause |
| Outdoor unit humming, not spinning | Failed capacitor | — | Test & replace capacitor |
| Runs constantly, weak cooling | Low refrigerant / leak | — | Leak search & recharge |
| Outdoor coil caked in dirt | Dirty condenser | Rinse fins with hose | Deep coil cleaning |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker / contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor/board |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries / wiring | Replace batteries | Repair C-wire/thermostat |
| System runs, no cold at all | Failed compressor | — | Replace compressor |
| Water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear line, add tablets | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **New thermostat:** $150–$400
- **Control/EcoNet board:** $300–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

A basic diagnostic service call typically runs **$75–$150**, often applied toward the repair.

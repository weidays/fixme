## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on a Rheem air conditioner — it's the symptom you notice when the system runs (or tries to) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. Rheem central AC condensers don't typically flash a numeric code for this at the outdoor unit; instead you diagnose it by working through the mechanical and electrical chain: airflow, refrigerant charge, and the compressor circuit.

Because this covers a wide range of underlying problems, this page ranks the likely causes and shows what's safe to check yourself versus what needs a licensed technician. Anything involving refrigerant, the sealed system, or a compressor that won't start is professional work.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can freeze the evaporator so no cold air moves.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice on the indoor coil or copper lines blocks heat transfer.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common no-cool cause; the fan or compressor hums but won't start, or trips off quickly.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — cooling weakens over weeks; often the last few feet of the large copper line are cold and sweating heavily or icing.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so cooling fades on hot days.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit gets pitted or stuck; the unit may not energize at all.
7. **Thermostat or wiring fault** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a broken low-voltage wire prevents a cooling call.
8. **Tripped breaker / blown fuse** — the outdoor disconnect or panel breaker is off.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive; the unit runs the fan but never cools, often after repeated breaker trips.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of no-cool calls.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Breaker/disconnect:** Check the indoor panel breaker and the outdoor disconnect near the condenser. If a breaker is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — don't keep resetting it.
- **Ice check:** Look at the indoor coil area and the copper lines at the outdoor unit. If you see ice, turn the system to OFF but leave the fan set to ON to help it thaw, then call a tech. Running a frozen system can damage the compressor.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trigger a float switch and shut the system down. Check for standing water in the drain pan; drain tablets help prevent future clogs.
- **Condenser clearance:** Rinse visible grass and debris off the outdoor coil with a gentle garden hose (power off) and clear at least two feet of space around the unit.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or open electrical panels containing the capacitor — capacitors hold a dangerous charge even with power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will typically:

1. Verify the thermostat is calling for cooling and confirm 24V reaches the outdoor unit.
2. Measure the run capacitor's microfarad rating against the nameplate — a weak capacitor is a fast, cheap find.
3. Inspect the contactor for pitting and confirm it pulls in.
4. Check airflow and the evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
5. Connect gauges to read suction and liquid pressures and take superheat/subcooling measurements — this reveals low charge, restriction, or overcharge.
6. If low on refrigerant, perform a leak search (electronic detector, dye, or bubble test) rather than just topping off.
7. Test the compressor windings and amp draw if it won't start.

A good quote names the specific failed part and the measured reading behind it. Be cautious of any tech who wants to add refrigerant without discussing a leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan runs, air not cold, filter dirty | Clogged filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, check for coil damage |
| Ice on indoor coil or copper line | Frozen evaporator | Set to OFF, fan ON to thaw | Find airflow or charge cause |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — hazardous | Test and replace capacitor |
| Cooling fades over weeks | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Unit won't energize at all | Bad contactor or breaker | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Weak cooling on hot days | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil, clear debris | Deep coil cleaning, charge check |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Electrical fault / seized compressor | Leave off | Diagnose compressor/wiring |
| No cooling call at all | Thermostat setting/batteries | Set COOL, new batteries | Check low-voltage wiring |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US costs including labor; your area and model vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0–$15 (DIY)
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against system replacement on older units)

If your Rheem is under the original parts warranty, the failed part may be covered — but labor and refrigerant usually are not. Keep your model and serial numbers handy.

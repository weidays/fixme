## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code, on Rheem and Ruud air conditioners. Your system may be running — the indoor blower and outdoor fan spinning — but the air coming from the vents is warm, lukewarm, or never gets the house to the thermostat setpoint. On EcoNet-enabled Rheem systems you may see a temperature deviation or system alert in the app rather than a blinking fault code.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, the goal is to narrow it down. Some causes are safe DIY checks. Others — anything involving refrigerant, the compressor, or the sealed system — legally and practically require a licensed HVAC technician, which is why this is rated a **pro** repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — Grass, dust, and cottonwood clog the fins and the unit can't reject heat.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — From low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — Weak cooling, longer run times, hissing, or ice. Requires a pro to find and seal the leak.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — Compressor or fan motor hums but won't start, or the outdoor unit trips.
6. **Thermostat problem** — Dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/communication fault (common on EcoNet setups).
7. **Contactor failure** — Outdoor unit gets no power to the compressor; often a pitted or stuck contactor.
8. **Failed compressor** — The most expensive outcome; unit won't cool at all despite calling for it.
9. **Blocked condensate drain triggering a safety float switch** — Cuts cooling to prevent water damage.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or bypassing a safety switch.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. A tripped breaker that immediately re-trips means stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for grass clippings, leaves, or debris packed against the fins. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (water flowing top to bottom) with power OFF at the disconnect.
- **Condensate drain:** Check the drain pan for standing water. A full pan can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Clear a visible clog and add drain tablets.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, switch to FAN ONLY for a few hours to thaw before running cooling again — and replace the filter.

Do not add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping its breaker.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap to expensive, and you can sanity-check a quote against this sequence:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — Confirms the thermostat is sending 24V and the system is actually demanding cooling.
2. **Check airflow and filter** — Measures temperature split across the coil (typically ~15–20°F for a healthy system).
3. **Inspect coils** — Looks at both evaporator and condenser coils for dirt, ice, or bent fins.
4. **Test electrical components** — Measures the capacitor's microfarads against its rating and checks the contactor for pitting or failure to pull in.
5. **Measure refrigerant pressures** — Connects gauges to read suction and liquid pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak.
6. **Leak search** — If charge is low, uses electronic detectors or dye to locate the leak before adding refrigerant.
7. **Test the compressor** — Checks amp draw, windings, and start behavior if all else checks out.

Be wary of any tech who "tops off" refrigerant without searching for a leak — that's treating the symptom, not the cause.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Dirty filter | Replace the filter | Measure airflow, check coil |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Fan-only to thaw, new filter | Find leak, check charge |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit doesn't run at all | Contactor or breaker | Check breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Long run times, hissing | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search and repair |
| Nothing cools, thermostat blank | Thermostat/power | Replace batteries, check breaker | Diagnose wiring/comms |
| Cooling cuts out, water in pan | Clogged condensate/float switch | Clear drain, add tablets | Flush line, test float switch |
| No cooling, compressor silent | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test/replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Regional rates vary.

- **Air filter:** $10 – $40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75 – $250
- **Thermostat batteries or replacement:** $0 – $300
- **Run capacitor:** $150 – $400
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100 – $300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400 – $1,500 (depends on leak location; evaporator coil replacement is at the high end)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800 – $2,500+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

If your system is under warranty, Rheem covers many parts (typically 10 years with registration), but you'll still pay labor. Always confirm warranty status before authorizing a big repair.

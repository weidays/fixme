## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code—it's the symptom homeowners describe when a Rheem air conditioner runs (or tries to run) but the house stays warm. Rheem central ACs generally don't display alphanumeric codes on the outdoor unit the way a furnace or heat pump board does; the diagnosis comes from what the system is *doing*: the outdoor fan spins but air is warm, the indoor blower runs but supply air isn't cold, or the outdoor unit hums and shuts off.

Because "not cooling" covers a wide range of failures—from a $20 filter to a $2,000 compressor—the goal of this page is to help you rule out the cheap, safe causes yourself and understand what a technician should check before you approve a big repair.

If your unit is a **heat pump** showing a blinking indicator, see the related page below instead—that behavior is diagnosed differently.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked return** — Restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil. The single most common and cheapest cause.
2. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — Grass, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat. Very common in summer.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — A weak or bulged dual capacitor keeps the compressor or fan from starting; you may hear a hum then a click-off.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — Outdoor unit gets no power, or the 24V control circuit is dead.
5. **Bad contactor** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit pits, sticks, or fails to pull in, so the compressor never energizes.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Warm supply air, ice on the copper line, long run times. Always caused by a leak, never normal.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — From low airflow or low charge; the coil turns to ice and blocks air completely.
8. **Thermostat or wiring fault** — Wrong mode, dead batteries, or a broken thermostat wire calling for nothing.
9. **Failed compressor** — Won't start, trips the breaker, or runs without cooling. The most expensive outcome.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system off at the thermostat first:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. If it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank, replace the batteries.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see light through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak or no cooling.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking airflow in the rooms that feel warm.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor AC breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop—do not keep resetting; call a pro.
- **Outdoor unit:** Turn off the system, then clear grass, leaves, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (no pressure washer) if they look dirty.
- **Condensate:** A full drain or float switch can shut cooling down. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear the line if you know where it is. If unsure, leave it for the tech.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, **turn off cooling but leave the fan ON** to thaw it, then run again. If it re-freezes, that's a low-airflow or refrigerant problem for a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass the float switch, or remove electrical panels on the outdoor unit—there's stored energy in the capacitor that can injure you.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Rheem tech should:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — Confirm 24V from the thermostat to the contactor and check the low-voltage fuse.
2. **Measure airflow and temperature split** — A healthy system drops supply air roughly 15–22°F below return air. A small split points to low charge or poor airflow.
3. **Test the capacitor and contactor** — Microfarad reading on the capacitor against its rating, and inspecting the contactor for pitting or failure to pull in.
4. **Check refrigerant charge** — Gauges on the service ports, reading pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low readings mean a **leak**, which should be located (dye, electronic sniffer, or nitrogen) before recharging.
5. **Inspect the compressor** — Amp draw, start behavior, and whether it's tripping on internal overload.
6. **Look for a frozen coil and its root cause** — They won't just thaw and recharge; they'll find *why* it froze.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without leak-testing or measuring superheat/subcooling deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents | Check duct static, blower motor |
| Outdoor unit warm, warm supply air | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse fins with hose (unit off) | Deep-clean coil, check charge |
| Outdoor hum then click-off | Failed run capacitor | None—stored charge is dangerous | Test/replace dual capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Tripped breaker / dead 24V fuse | Reset breaker once | Replace fuse, find short/contactor |
| Ice on copper line or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Fan ON to thaw, replace filter | Find airflow issue or leak |
| Cold air but house never cools | Undersized/low charge, leaky ducts | Nothing | Charge check, duct/leak inspection |
| Breaker trips repeatedly on start | Failing compressor or short | Stop resetting; shut off | Diagnose compressor, wiring |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area, model, and warranty status change them.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 DIY.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200, or DIY with tablets.
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$300 installed.
- **Low-voltage fuse / thermostat wiring:** $100–$300.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing varies).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,200+; often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units. Parts may be covered under Rheem's warranty (typically 10 years if registered), but labor usually is not.

Get an itemized quote and confirm whether the part is under Rheem warranty before approving major work.

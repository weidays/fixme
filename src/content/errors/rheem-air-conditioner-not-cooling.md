## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single fault code — it's a symptom. Your Rheem air conditioner may be running (indoor blower and/or outdoor fan spinning) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On Rheem/Ruud systems, "not cooling" can point to anything from a clogged filter to a failed compressor.

Some Rheem systems with communicating controls (like Prestige/EcoNet models) will store a diagnostic code or show a message at the thermostat. Basic single-stage units (Classic series) generally have no display, so you're diagnosing by symptom. **Behavior varies by model and control board** — check your EcoNet app or thermostat for any stored fault before assuming a mechanical cause.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, ices the indoor coil, and kills cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or dead batteries** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," wrong setpoint, or a blank display.
3. **Tripped breaker to the outdoor unit** — the indoor blower runs on a separate circuit, so you feel airflow but the condenser is dead.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure that stops the compressor or outdoor fan from starting.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks cooling entirely.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces capacity; the system runs constantly but barely cools. Requires a licensed tech.
8. **Contactor failure** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit won't pull in.
9. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive cause; the unit hums or trips but won't cool.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should attempt:

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Then run the system for 30 minutes.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm mode is **COOL** and the setpoint is at least 3–5°F below room temp. Replace batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor air handler/furnace circuit and the outdoor condenser circuit. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a tech.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** Turn off power, then look for grass clippings, leaves, or debris packed against the condenser fins. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (low pressure, top to bottom).
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture or closed.
- **Condensate:** Check for standing water in the drain pan or near the indoor unit. A full pan can trip a float switch that shuts the system off. Clear a visible clog if you can reach the drain line.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, switch the thermostat to **OFF** but leave the **fan ON** to thaw it (can take a few hours), then check the filter.

**Do not** open sealed refrigerant lines, bypass float or safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will typically:

- Verify the thermostat call and confirm 24V is reaching the contactor.
- Measure the run capacitor's microfarads against the rating on its label.
- Check the contactor for pitted or welded contacts.
- Attach gauges to read suction/head pressures and calculate **superheat and subcooling** to determine the refrigerant charge — this is how they confirm a leak vs. a mechanical fault.
- Measure the temperature split (return vs. supply air); a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F.
- Inspect the evaporator and condenser coils for restriction or freezing.
- Test compressor windings and amp draw if it won't start.
- On EcoNet/communicating models, pull stored fault codes from the control board.

If a tech quotes a compressor or refrigerant repair, they should show you the pressure readings and leak-search results — not just replace parts on a guess.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter, run 30 min | Check blower, ductwork |
| Blower runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, find trip cause |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Failed run capacitor | None | Test & replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw with fan on, change filter | Find leak, correct charge |
| Runs constantly, barely cools | Low refrigerant / dirty coil | Rinse condenser coil | Leak search, recharge |
| Humming, trips breaker | Compressor or hard-start fault | Stop — don't re-reset | Test compressor, start components |
| Water near indoor unit, system off | Full pan / float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, replace float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0
- **Run or dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate float switch:** $75–$200
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $300–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500 (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

Prices vary by region, unit size, and whether the system is under Rheem's parts warranty (labor is usually not covered).

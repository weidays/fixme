## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a fault code on the display — it's a symptom. Your Rheem air conditioner is powered and may even be running, but the air coming out of your vents isn't getting cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. Rheem split systems don't usually flash a specific "no cooling" code; instead, you'll notice weak airflow, warm air, ice on the lines, or a system that runs constantly without lowering the temperature.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a $15 filter to a $2,500 compressor, the goal here is to help you rule out the cheap, safe fixes first and understand what a technician should be checking before they quote you a big repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter chokes airflow across the evaporator coil, which can freeze the coil and stop cooling entirely.
2. **Thermostat problem** — Dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" or "heat"), a bad C-wire connection, or a failing thermostat.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant. A block of ice on the coil stops heat transfer, so vents blow warm or barely-moving air.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — Grass clippings, cottonwood, and dirt insulate the coil so it can't dump heat outside.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — A very common electrical failure. The compressor or condenser fan hums but won't start, or the fan spins slowly.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — The outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
7. **Bad contactor** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit fails, so the condenser never energizes.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Weak cooling, longer run times, ice on the copper lines. This is never "normal use" — refrigerant doesn't get consumed.
9. **Failing compressor** — The heart of the system. If it can't build pressure, you get warm air. Expensive to replace.
10. **Reversing valve (heat-pump models only)** — A stuck valve leaves the system in heating mode.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Stop before anything involving refrigerant, capacitors, or wiring.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Run the system 30 minutes and recheck for improvement.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it takes them.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker feeding the **outdoor unit** and the **indoor air handler/furnace**. Reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply vents are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Confirm the fan is spinning and the coil isn't buried in leaves or grass. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (power off) if it's visibly dirty.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a float switch and shut the system down. Check the pan; a wet/dry vac on the drain outlet can clear a clog.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to **OFF** (fan can stay on to help it thaw) and let it melt fully — often several hours — before restarting. If it re-freezes, you have an airflow or refrigerant problem for a pro.

If none of these restores cooling, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech works from cheap to expensive, and you can sanity-check their quote against these steps:

- **Verify airflow and temperature split.** They'll measure the temperature difference between return and supply air (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F). A low split points to airflow or refrigerant issues.
- **Inspect the evaporator and condenser coils** for dirt, ice, or damage.
- **Check electrical components** — measuring the run capacitor with a meter, testing the contactor, and confirming voltage at the outdoor unit.
- **Attach refrigerant gauges** to read suction and head pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. This tells them whether the charge is correct and hints at where a leak might be.
- **Leak search** with an electronic detector or dye if refrigerant is low. Reputable techs find and repair the leak rather than just topping off.
- **Test the compressor** for proper amp draw and starting, and (on heat pumps) check the reversing valve.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace compressor" without a pressure reading and leak check.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter, thaw coil if iced | Check blower and duct static pressure |
| Nothing cools, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries, check breaker | Diagnose transformer, C-wire, wiring |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, let thaw fully | Find airflow restriction or refrigerant leak |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Failed capacitor or compressor | None — stop here | Test/replace capacitor; test compressor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor blower runs | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check load |
| Runs constantly, house stays warm | Low refrigerant / dirty condenser | Rinse outdoor coil | Leak search, recharge, verify charge |
| Humming, then shutdown | Failed capacitor / seized compressor | None | Replace capacitor or compressor |
| Heat pump blows warm in cool mode | Stuck reversing valve | None | Test/replace reversing valve |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor. Your area and contractor rates will vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 installed
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150–$300 pro
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$2,000+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often worth weighing against full system replacement on older units)
- **Reversing valve (heat pumps):** $600–$1,200

If your unit is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison — on a 12+ year old system, replacement often makes more financial sense.

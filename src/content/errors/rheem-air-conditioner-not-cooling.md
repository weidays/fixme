## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on Rheem air conditioners — it's a symptom. Rheem split-system ACs (the RA13 through RA20 series) don't have a digital display on the outdoor unit, so instead of a numeric code you'll notice the house isn't reaching the thermostat setpoint even though the system appears to be "on."

This can mean the outdoor condenser is running but not producing cold air, the indoor blower is moving warm air, or the system won't start a cooling cycle at all. Because a Rheem AC depends on the indoor furnace/air handler board, the thermostat, and the outdoor unit all working together, the root cause can sit in any of those areas.

If your unit has a communicating thermostat (common on Prestige and Endeavor systems), the thermostat itself may display a fault or "system" message — behavior varies by model and by which thermostat is paired, so check your thermostat's manual for its specific alerts.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — The single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the coil, reducing cooling and eventually icing the evaporator.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant. The coil turns to a block of ice and blocks air completely.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — A weak or bulged dual capacitor stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting. You may hear a hum or clicking outside.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — The system runs constantly but blows barely-cool air. Requires an EPA-certified tech to find and seal the leak, then recharge.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — The outdoor coil can't reject heat, so the system runs but cooling drops off, especially on hot afternoons.
6. **Failed contactor** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit gets pitted or stuck, so the condenser won't energize.
7. **Thermostat problem** — Dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/communication fault, especially on communicating Prestige/Endeavor setups.
8. **Compressor failure** — The most expensive outcome. The condenser hums but won't start, or trips the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter** — Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers** — Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents** — Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow. Open all vents.
- **Outdoor unit clearance** — Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Leave at least two feet of space.
- **Condensate drain** — A clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut the system down. Check for standing water near the indoor unit and clear a visible clog if you can safely reach it.
- **Check for ice** — If the indoor coil or the outdoor refrigerant line is frosted, turn the system to OFF and the fan to ON to thaw it. If ice returns, you have a low-airflow or refrigerant problem for a tech.

Do **not** open the outdoor electrical panel, touch the capacitor, or attempt anything with refrigerant. Capacitors hold a dangerous charge even with power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** and checks thermostat mode, setpoint, and call for cooling at the board.
2. **Measures airflow and temperature split** — the difference between return and supply air should be roughly 15–20°F. A low split points to airflow or refrigerant issues.
3. **Inspects the filter and coils** for restriction and icing.
4. **Checks capacitor microfarads** with a meter against the rating printed on the cap.
5. **Tests the contactor** for pitting and proper pull-in when there's a call.
6. **Connects refrigerant gauges** to read pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling — this reveals a low charge or a leak. (This step requires EPA certification.)
7. **Runs a leak search** if the charge is low, using an electronic detector or dye.
8. **Tests the compressor windings and amp draw** if it won't start or trips the breaker.

If a tech quotes a compressor or refrigerant recharge without first checking the filter, airflow, and capacitor, ask them to walk you through the readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blows warm air, filter looks gray/clogged | Dirty filter starving the coil | Replace the filter, run fan to thaw any ice | Confirm airflow; clean coil if needed |
| Coil or line is frosted/iced | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set to OFF/fan ON to thaw, replace filter | Check charge and airflow, find leak |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — do not open panel | Test and replace capacitor |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Failed contactor or breaker | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Runs constantly, weak cooling on hot days | Low refrigerant or dirty condenser coil | Rinse debris off outdoor unit exterior | Gauge pressures, clean coil, repair leak |
| Nothing runs, thermostat blank | Dead thermostat batteries or power | Replace batteries, check breaker | Diagnose control wiring/board |
| Breaker trips when AC starts | Compressor or electrical fault | Reset once; stop if it trips again | Test compressor and wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and whether you're in a service contract.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$300
- **Refrigerant recharge (after leak repair):** $200–$600 depending on refrigerant type and amount
- **Refrigerant leak repair:** $500–$1,500 depending on location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500 (often worth weighing against a full replacement on older units)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200, frequently credited toward the repair

Note: R-410A refrigerant pricing has risen with the 2025 phase-down, so leak repairs and recharges on older systems can run higher than in past years.

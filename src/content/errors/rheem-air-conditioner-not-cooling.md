## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a stored fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Rheem or Ruud air conditioner runs but the house never reaches the set temperature. The outdoor unit may hum, the indoor blower may push air, yet that air is warm or only slightly cool.

Because central AC has no single "not cooling" error, the cause could sit anywhere in the system: airflow, electrical components, the refrigerant circuit, or the thermostat/controls. Some newer Rheem units with communicating controls (EcoNet-enabled) may show a specific alert in the app or thermostat, but the classic single-stage condensers just run without cooling.

This page covers the general split-system diagnosis. If your unit is a **heat pump** and you see a blinking light, see the related page below instead.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or restricted airflow** — the single most common cause. Starves the evaporator coil and can freeze it into a block of ice.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass clippings, dust, and cottonwood clog the fins, so the unit can't reject heat.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer and cool air stops.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bulging or dead capacitor prevents the compressor or fan motor from starting. Very common in hot climates.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cooling capacity drops; you may hear hissing or see ice on the copper lines. Requires a pro.
6. **Faulty contactor** — the outdoor electrical switch that pulls in the compressor pits and fails to make contact.
7. **Thermostat problems** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or wiring/calibration faults.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor fan not spinning causes the unit to overheat and trip.
9. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive outcome; the unit hums or clicks but won't cool. Least likely but most serious.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no covers off the outdoor disconnect, no refrigerant, no bypassing switches.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL, fan on AUTO, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temp. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause.
- **Breakers:** Check the panel for a tripped breaker for the AC. Reset **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets can damage equipment.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or leaves smothering the condenser. Gently clear the area (unit off). Do not open the electrical panel.
- **Frozen coil / iced lines:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON to help thaw) for a few hours before calling. Running it frozen makes things worse.
- **Condensate drain:** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a float switch and shut off cooling. Check that the pan isn't overflowing.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from the cheapest, most likely cause up — you should hear them do this:

1. **Airflow and temperature split:** Measures the temperature drop across the indoor coil (return vs supply). A healthy split is roughly 15–20°F. Low split points to refrigerant or airflow issues.
2. **Electrical checks:** Tests the capacitor with a meter (a dead cap is a fast, cheap find), checks the contactor for pitting, and verifies the compressor and fan motor draw amps within spec.
3. **Refrigerant pressures:** Connects gauges to read suction and head pressure and calculate superheat/subcooling. This tells them if the charge is low and whether there's a leak — **not** just "top it off."
4. **Leak search:** If refrigerant is low, they should perform a leak search (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen) before adding any refrigerant. Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is bad practice.
5. **Compressor test:** If the unit hums but won't start after ruling out the capacitor and contactor, they'll check the compressor windings for a locked rotor or open winding.

Ask to see the measured temperature split and refrigerant readings — a legitimate quote is backed by numbers, not guesses.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Dirty filter / restricted airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Clean blower/coil, check ductwork |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Diagnose charge, leak search |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or fan motor | Check breaker only | Test/replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Unit hums, won't start | Bad capacitor or contactor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor/contactor |
| Hissing sound, poor cooling | Refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Leak repair + recharge |
| Warm rooms, no ice | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris around unit | Deep coil cleaning |
| Nothing cools, blower runs | Thermostat or wiring | Check mode/batteries | Test thermostat & wiring |
| Long runs, high bills, low cooling | Failing compressor | None — call pro | Compressor test/replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and unit age):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $100–$300
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$700
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer R-454B on 2025+ units, which affects material cost)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often makes replacing the whole condenser worth considering on older units)

If your Rheem condenser is 12+ years old and facing a compressor or coil failure, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison — a new leak in an aging unit can be throwing good money after bad.

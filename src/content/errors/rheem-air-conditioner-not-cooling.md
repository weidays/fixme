## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on a Rheem air conditioner — it's a symptom. Your system may be running (fan spinning, air moving) but the air coming from the vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On units with an EcoNet communicating control board, you may see an alert or fault light rather than a numeric code.

Because "not cooling" can stem from anything as simple as a dirty filter to a dead compressor, this page walks you through the safe checks you can do yourself, then explains how a technician diagnoses the rest — so you can sanity-check any quote you're given.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor coil and stop cooling entirely.
2. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — matted with grass, dust, or cottonwood, it can't reject heat. Very common in mid-summer.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bulged or dead capacitor keeps the compressor or fan motor from starting. Extremely common failure part.
4. **Low refrigerant due to a leak** — causes weak or no cooling and often ice on the lines. Requires an EPA-certified tech.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant. The unit blows warm because the coil is a block of ice.
6. **Faulty contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit fails, so the compressor and fan never energize.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/comms fault (more likely on EcoNet setups).
8. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Failed compressor** — the least likely but most expensive. The unit hums or clicks but won't cool.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If none of them fix it, call a licensed HVAC tech.

- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Check the air filter.** A dirty filter is the #1 cause. Replace it if it's gray or clogged — this alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Check the breaker.** Look in your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (usually a double breaker). Reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets are unsafe.
- **Check your vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Look at the outdoor unit.** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser so it can breathe. Give it 2 feet of clearance.
- **Check for ice.** If the copper line or indoor coil is frozen, turn the system to OFF (or FAN ONLY) for a few hours to let it thaw, then run cooling again. If it re-freezes, you have a deeper problem.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A clogged drain or full pan can trip a safety switch and shut cooling down. Clear standing water if you can access the pan safely.

Do **not** open the outdoor disconnect, touch the capacitor, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. Those steps are for a qualified technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — here's what to expect so you can spot a rushed or padded quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint and checks the thermostat call** for cooling reaching the equipment.
2. **Measures the temperature split** (return vs. supply air). A healthy split is roughly 15–20°F.
3. **Inspects filter and coils** for airflow restriction and checks for a frozen coil.
4. **Tests electrical components** — capacitor microfarad reading, contactor operation, and voltage at the unit.
5. **Checks refrigerant charge with gauges.** Low pressure points to a leak; the tech should then perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, bubble solution, or dye), not just top off the charge.
6. **Amp-draws the compressor** to confirm it's actually running and not seized or drawing locked-rotor amps.
7. **On EcoNet/communicating systems,** reads stored fault history from the control board.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add a couple pounds of refrigerant" without looking for a leak — that's a temporary fix and, on newer refrigerants, an expensive one.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air moving but not cold | Dirty filter / dirty coils | Replace filter, clear condenser | Deep coil cleaning, airflow check |
| Ice on indoor coil or copper line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, let thaw, replace filter | Find restriction or refrigerant leak |
| Outdoor unit silent, no cooling | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, test power |
| Outdoor unit hums but fan won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Weak cooling, house won't hold temp | Low refrigerant leak | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| No response at all | Thermostat / low-voltage fuse | Replace batteries, check mode | Replace fuse, trace wiring |
| Loud hum/click, warm air | Failed or seizing compressor | None | Diagnose compressor, quote repair vs. replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and unit age.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Coil cleaning (condenser or evaporator):** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$300
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Low-voltage fuse:** $75–$150
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,200+ (often not worth it out of warranty)
- **Full condenser/system replacement:** $4,500–$9,000+

Note: many Rheem units carry a 10-year parts warranty if registered, which can significantly cut the cost of a compressor, coil, or capacitor — labor is usually not covered. Ask your tech to check the warranty status by serial number.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint Rheem and Ruud homeowners report. Your air conditioner may run but blow warm or lukewarm air, run constantly without lowering the temperature, or the outdoor unit may not start at all.

Because central AC cooling depends on several systems working together — airflow, refrigerant, electrical, and controls — "not cooling" can trace back to something as simple as a clogged filter or as serious as a failed compressor. Some Rheem communicating systems (with the EcoNet control) will display a specific fault or flashing code when a sensor or board detects a problem; basic single-stage models often show nothing and just run warm.

If your outdoor unit shows a flashing fault light, see the related page below before troubleshooting here.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, causes the coil to ice over, and kills cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer and the unit blows warm.
3. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a setpoint/schedule issue.
4. **Tripped breaker on the outdoor unit** — the condenser gets no power, so only the indoor fan runs (warm air).
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor and/or outdoor fan can't start; you may hear a hum or clicking outside.
6. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't close.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, ice, and hissing or bubbling sounds. Requires a licensed tech.
8. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — blocked by dirt, grass, or debris, reducing heat rejection.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the outdoor unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform without tools or training:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it up to light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers** — check your electrical panel for both the indoor and outdoor AC breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil / ice** — if you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (or FAN only) and let it thaw fully for several hours before running again.
- **Condensate drain** — a clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and shut cooling down. If your drain line drips outside, confirm it's clear; a wet-vac at the outdoor termination can help.

Do not open the outdoor disconnect, touch the capacitor, or attempt any refrigerant work — those steps are covered by the technician section for a reason.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified Rheem tech will typically:

1. Verify the thermostat call for cooling reaches the equipment (24V control check).
2. Confirm airflow — inspect filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. Check the outdoor disconnect, breaker, contactor, and capacitor with a multimeter.
4. Measure compressor and fan motor amp draw against the nameplate.
5. Connect refrigerant gauges to read pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling — this tells them if refrigerant is low and whether there's a leak.
6. If low, perform a leak search (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) before adding anything.
7. On EcoNet/communicating models, read stored fault codes from the control board.

A trustworthy quote names the specific failed part and the measured reading behind it — not just "it needs Freon."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor fan runs, air is warm | Outdoor unit not running | Check/reset outdoor breaker once | Test contactor, capacitor, compressor |
| Ice on lines or coil | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace filter, thaw fully | Check airflow and refrigerant charge |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — leave power off | Replace capacitor, verify amp draw |
| Weak cooling, hissing sound | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Failed compressor | None | Test and replace compressor |
| System short-cycles | Dirty condenser or control fault | Rinse debris off outdoor coil | Inspect controls, pressures, wiring |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker or thermostat | Reset breaker once, check thermostat | Diagnose power/control circuit |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter** — $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries** — under $10 (DIY)
- **Run capacitor** — $150–$400
- **Contactor** — $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning** — $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair** — $200–$1,500 depending on leak location; evaporator coil replacement pushes the high end
- **Refrigerant recharge** — $100–$300 per pound (R-410A pricing varies)
- **Condenser fan motor** — $300–$650
- **Compressor replacement** — $1,500–$2,500+, often making a full outdoor unit replacement worth considering on older systems

If your Rheem system is under warranty, the compressor and coil may be covered for parts — confirm registration and keep receipts, as labor is usually separate.

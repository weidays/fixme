## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Rheem or Ruud air conditioner runs but the air coming from your vents is warm, lukewarm, or just never gets the house to setpoint. Some newer Rheem communicating systems (Endeavor line with EcoNet controls) will show a fault or status message on the thermostat, but conventional RA-series condensers have no display, so you're working from behavior alone.

A Rheem AC "not cooling" almost always falls into one of three buckets:

- **Airflow problem** — the system can't move enough air (dirty filter, iced coil, blocked returns).
- **Electrical problem** — the compressor or fan isn't getting power or can't start (capacitor, contactor, breaker).
- **Refrigerant problem** — a sealed-system leak has dropped the charge below what's needed to cool.

The first bucket has safe DIY checks. The other two are **pro-level** — they involve high voltage, refrigerant handling, and EPA certification requirements.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or restricted airflow** — the single most common cause. Starves the evaporator coil, which can then freeze into a block of ice and stop cooling entirely.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," wrong mode, dead batteries, or a miscalibrated/dying stat.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor condenser is on its own breaker; if it trips, the indoor blower still runs and blows warm air.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — extremely common in hot climates. The compressor or condenser fan hums but won't spin up.
5. **Bad contactor** — the relay that sends power to the condenser gets pitted or stuck; the outdoor unit won't energize.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — you'll see poor cooling, longer run times, ice on the lines, or hissing. Always a leak, never "used up."
8. **Dirty/blocked condenser coil** — the outdoor coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor may run but overheat and shut down on its internal overload.
10. **Failed compressor** — the least common but most expensive; the unit won't cool at all, may trip breakers, or hum and cut out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the condenser electrical panel, touch the capacitor, or add refrigerant.

- **Air filter** — Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of no-cool calls.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto** (not On). Replace the batteries if it's battery powered.
- **Breakers** — Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker labeled for the AC or condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal a real fault.
- **Vents and returns** — Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — Look for grass, leaves, or debris packed against the coil fins. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (unit off) from the outside in.
- **Ice check** — If you see ice on the refrigerant line or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** but leave the **fan On** for a few hours to thaw it. If it ices again after thawing, you have an airflow or refrigerant issue — call a tech.
- **Condensate** — A full drain pan or clogged condensate line can trip a float safety switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water; clearing a visible clog is fine, but wiring is not.

If cooling doesn't return after these checks, it's a pro job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works methodically — here's what a legitimate diagnosis looks like so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** and checks thermostat calls and mode.
2. **Measures temperature split** — the difference between return and supply air (typically 16-22°F on a healthy system). A low split points to refrigerant or airflow.
3. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
4. **Tests electrical** — measures voltage at the contactor, tests the run/start capacitor with a meter (a bulging cap is an obvious tell), and checks the contactor for pitting.
5. **Checks the condenser fan and compressor** — verifies both energize and draw normal amperage.
6. **Gauges the refrigerant** — connects manifold gauges to read pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling. Low readings mean a leak.
7. **Leak search** — if charge is low, uses electronic detector or dye to find the leak before recharging (topping off without finding the leak is a red flag).

Expect a **$75-$200 diagnostic fee**, often credited toward the repair. A tech quoting a full recharge without a leak search is cutting corners.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower runs, vents warm | Dirty filter / iced coil | Replace filter; thaw with fan on | Restore airflow, find airflow restriction |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, check wiring |
| Condenser hums, fan/compressor won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — high voltage | Test and replace capacitor |
| Long run times, house won't cool down | Low refrigerant / dirty condenser | Rinse outdoor coil | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice on lines, poor cooling | Low charge or low airflow | Thaw unit, change filter | Diagnose leak vs. airflow |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Short, failing compressor, or fan motor | Do not keep resetting | Amp-draw test, electrical fault repair |
| No cooling at all, buzzing | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test / replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Prices vary by region and by whether your unit is a single-stage RA13/14 or a two-stage/communicating Endeavor system.

- **Air filter:** $10-$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150-$400
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150-$400
- **Contactor:** $150-$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350-$650
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $150-$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600-$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far pricier)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000-$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500-$2,500+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)
- **Diagnostic fee:** $75-$200, frequently credited toward the repair

On systems 12-15+ years old with a failed compressor or major leak, ask your tech to compare the repair cost against a full replacement — the math often favors a new system.

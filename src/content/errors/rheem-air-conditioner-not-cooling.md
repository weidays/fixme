## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Your Rheem air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, thermostat calling), but the air coming out of your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the setpoint. On EcoNet-equipped Rheem systems you may see a related fault on the thermostat or control board; on standard single-stage units, there's often no code at all — just warm air.

The root issue is almost always one of three things: airflow is restricted, the refrigerant circuit isn't moving heat (low charge or a failed compressor), or an electrical component that starts the compressor has failed. This page walks through what you can safely check yourself and what a technician will do — so you can judge whether a quote is fair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, freezes the indoor coil, and kills cooling. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Tripped breaker or failed contactor** — the outdoor unit gets no power, so the compressor never runs even though the indoor blower does.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan motor hums or won't start. Very common in hot-climate Rheem units.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharge means little to no cooling, often with ice on the copper lines. This is a repair, never a "top-off."
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low charge; blocks the whole system until it thaws.
7. **Failed compressor** — the expensive worst case; the unit runs but produces no cold air and may trip on overload.
8. **Thermostat, wiring, or control board fault** — the call for cooling isn't reaching the outdoor unit. More common on EcoNet/communicating systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or touching capacitors.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and lower the setpoint 5°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Recheck cooling after a few hours.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the coil fins with a garden hose (never a pressure washer) with the unit powered off.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water pooling or a full drain pan, clear the drain line. A safety float switch can shut the system down when the drain clogs.
- **Ice on the lines:** If the copper line or indoor coil is frozen, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw for a few hours before running cooling again.

Do **not** open the sealed system, add refrigerant, discharge capacitors, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verifies the complaint and airflow** — checks filter, coil, and blower operation first. A quote for refrigerant before checking airflow is a red flag.
2. **Measures voltage at the contactor and capacitor** — confirms the outdoor unit is getting power and the capacitor is within its rated microfarads (µF).
3. **Checks the contactor and compressor windings** — verifies the compressor is actually energizing and not tripping on overload.
4. **Connects gauges and reads superheat/subcooling** — this tells them whether the charge is correct, low, or overcharged. Low charge triggers a leak search.
5. **Leak search if undercharged** — with electronic detector or dye. A legitimate refrigerant repair fixes the leak, then recharges to spec.
6. **Checks the thermostat and control signals** — on EcoNet/communicating Rheem systems, they'll read board diagnostics and fault history.

Expect a diagnostic fee of **$75–$200**, often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter, thaw coil | Check airflow, charge, blower |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Failed capacitor or contactor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor/contactor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Diagnose electrical fault |
| Ice on copper lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw with fan ON | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| House never cools enough | Low refrigerant or dirty coil | Clean/rinse condenser coil | Measure charge, leak search |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Weak capacitor or failing compressor | None | Test capacitor, then compressor |
| No cooling call reaching unit | Thermostat/wiring/board | Replace tstat batteries | Read board diagnostics, wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor installed:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$300
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$250
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 (more for communicating EcoNet models)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV or metering device:** $500–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,500 (often worth comparing against a new condenser if the unit is 10+ years old)

If your Rheem uses R-22 refrigerant, recharge costs are significantly higher due to phase-out; replacement often makes more financial sense.

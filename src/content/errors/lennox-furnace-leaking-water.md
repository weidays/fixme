## What this code means

"Leaking water" is not a specific Lennox blink or E-code — it is a physical symptom you find as a puddle under or around the furnace, or water dripping from the cabinet. On most modern Lennox systems, water at the furnace comes from the **condensate system** of a high-efficiency (condensing) furnace, from the **air-conditioning coil** sitting on top of the furnace, or from a **flue/venting** problem.

High-efficiency Lennox furnaces (90%+ AFUE, such as the SLP99V, EL296V and similar) are designed to produce condensate — acidic water wrung out of the flue gases. That water is supposed to flow through a **collector box, condensate trap, and drain line**. Any blockage or crack in that path shows up as a leak.

Because a leak can also indicate a **cracked secondary heat exchanger** — a genuine safety and health concern — this is rated **pro**. There is no fault code to reset; instead, the safest response is to shut the furnace down and have the source identified.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged condensate drain line or trap** — Algae, dust and debris block the drain, so condensate backs up and overflows. The single most common cause on condensing furnaces.
2. **Full or overflowing condensate trap** — The trap collects debris or dries out/cracks and leaks at its seams or fittings.
3. **Condensate pump failure** (if your furnace uses one) — The pump reservoir overflows when the pump fails, floats stick, or its discharge line clogs.
4. **A/C evaporator coil drain issues** — In summer, the coil above the furnace produces condensate; a clogged coil drain pan or line drips onto the furnace and looks like a furnace leak.
5. **Clogged or improperly sloped flue/vent (PVC)** — Condensate pools in the venting instead of draining back to the trap, then leaks at joints.
6. **Cracked collector box or hoses** — Internal plastic condensate components crack with age and leak inside the cabinet.
7. **Cracked secondary heat exchanger** — Less common but serious; produces condensate leaks and requires professional inspection.

### A Lennox-specific wrinkle

On many Lennox condensing furnaces the condensate path — collector box, drain hoses and trap — sits **behind the blower access panel**, not out in the open on the side of the cabinet. That matters for two reasons: a leak from those components can run down inside the cabinet toward the blower and control board before you ever see a drop on the floor, and there is no way for a homeowner to inspect that assembly without opening the cabinet, which is technician work.

Lennox installs also commonly use an inline drain arrangement where the installer **selects a left- or right-side drain-hose routing in the field** to suit the closet, attic or basement layout. If a furnace was moved, re-piped, or the drain was re-routed after the original install, the hose configuration and the slope of the drain line are worth mentioning to the technician — a leak that started right after other work is often a routing or slope issue rather than a failed part.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform, in this order:

- **Turn the furnace off** at the thermostat (set to Off).
- **Cut power at the breaker or the furnace service switch** before you go near standing water. Do this first if there is any water at the furnace base, near wiring, or near the control board area.
- **With the power off, sponge or wet-vac the standing water** away from the furnace base and electrical parts. Never wet-vac around a furnace that still has power to it.
- **Check your air filter.** A severely clogged filter can cause coil freeze/thaw that drips water in cooling season; replace it if dirty.
- **Look at the condensate drain line's exit.** If it terminates at a floor drain, sink or outside, make sure that endpoint isn't clogged or frozen and the line isn't kinked.
- **Check the condensate pump** (if you have one) — confirm it has power and its reservoir isn't overflowing. Do not open or disassemble it.
- **Look for A/C-related dripping** — if it's summer and the leak is coming from the coil box on top, note that for the technician.

Do **not** open the furnace cabinet, remove hoses, poke at the collector box, or run the furnace to "dry it out." Anything inside the cabinet is technician work.

**If a CO alarm sounds, anyone in the home feels dizzy, nauseated or headachy, or you smell gas or burning: leave the house immediately and call 911 or your gas utility from outside.** Do not stop to troubleshoot, and call the HVAC company only after the home has been cleared.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- Confirms whether the water is **combustion condensate, A/C condensate, or flue condensation** by tracing where it originates.
- Inspects and clears the **condensate drain line and trap**, often flushing or vacuuming the blockage.
- Tests the **condensate pump**, floats and discharge line.
- Checks **flue/vent slope and joints** for pooling condensate and improper pitch.
- Inspects the **collector box and internal condensate hoses** for cracks, including the assembly behind the blower access panel.
- Verifies the **drain-hose routing and slope** are correct for how the unit is installed.
- Performs a **heat exchanger inspection** (visual, camera, and sometimes combustion analysis) to rule out a cracked secondary heat exchanger.
- Checks the **evaporator coil drain pan and line** if the leak is seasonal/cooling-related.

A good quote should name the specific source — vague "it's leaking" without identifying the component is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Puddle under furnace, worse when running in heat | Clogged condensate drain/trap | Turn off, check drain endpoint isn't blocked/frozen | Clear/flush trap and drain line, verify flow |
| Water only in summer, from top coil box | A/C coil drain pan/line clog | Replace dirty filter, note timing | Clear coil drain, flush pan, check pan for cracks |
| Overflowing plastic pump box, gurgling | Failed condensate pump | Confirm pump has power; don't open it | Test/replace pump, clear discharge line |
| Water dripping from PVC vent joints | Improper flue slope or blockage | None — leave venting alone | Re-slope/reseal vent, clear flue condensate |
| Leak plus soot, rust, or odd flame | Cracked heat exchanger — possible CO hazard | **If a CO alarm sounds, anyone feels ill, or you smell gas or burning: leave the house and call 911 / the gas utility first.** Otherwise shut the furnace off and don't run it | Camera/combustion inspection, replace exchanger |
| Leak near control board, electrical | Any of the above reaching board | Kill power at breaker first, then wet-vac water | Dry/inspect board, fix leak source |

## Repair costs

- **Drain line/trap clearing:** $0 DIY (endpoint only) – $150–$250 professional cleaning.
- **Condensate pump replacement:** $200–$600 installed.
- **Condensate trap or hose replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Flue/vent re-sloping or resealing:** $150–$450 depending on access.
- **Collector box replacement:** $300–$700 (part plus labor).
- **A/C coil drain pan/line service:** $150–$400.
- **Cracked secondary heat exchanger:** $1,200–$3,000+ installed, even when the part itself is still covered by a parts warranty, because the labor to pull and rebuild the furnace is substantial. On older units, replacing the furnace is often more economical.

Prices vary by region, accessibility and whether parts are under Lennox warranty. Labor is typically covered only if you have a labor warranty or service plan; the Lennox parts warranty may cover the heat exchanger itself but usually not the labor to install it.

Before you match a leak to a "code," check how your furnace actually reports faults. Lennox fault display varies significantly by control board: newer units such as the SLP99V and EL296V use a **7-segment unit controller** that shows alphanumeric alert codes, while older ML- and SL-series boards use **LED flash/blink codes** and have no E-codes at all. Read the code chart on the label inside your furnace's access panel rather than assuming one universal Lennox code set — the same symptom can carry a different code, or no code, depending on the board in your cabinet.

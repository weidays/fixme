## What this code means

"Blowing warm air" is a symptom, not a stored fault code. The indoor blower is running, but the air from the registers is room temperature or warmer.

A healthy Trane system normally delivers air about 15–20°F cooler than the air going into the return. If you are getting little or no difference, heat is not being removed from the air. The cause is usually one of these:

- The outdoor unit (compressor and condenser fan) is not running.
- The unit is running but can't move heat, because of low refrigerant or a blocked coil.

Two related topics already have their own pages, so this page doesn't repeat them:
- If the outdoor unit is completely silent, see Compressor not running.
- If the indoor coil is iced over, see Frozen evaporator coil.

**Communicating units:** On XV-series and ComfortLink/Link systems, the communicating thermostat (such as the ComfortLink II or Link UX360) may show an active alert or a fault history. Note any message before you reset anything.

**Reset behavior varies by model:**
- Most conventional units resume on their own once the condition clears, such as after the compressor's internal overload cools down.
- Communicating units may hold certain lockouts until power is cycled or a technician clears them.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor (outdoor unit).** This is the most common cause. The compressor hums or won't start, and the outdoor fan may stall. It is especially common after summer heat waves.
2. **Failed or pitted contactor.** The outdoor unit doesn't get power even though the thermostat is calling for cooling. Insects or ants in the contactor are a classic cause.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** The unit runs but cools weakly. Common leak points:
   - Older Trane spine-fin outdoor coils
   - Evaporator coils with formicary (pinhole) corrosion
   - Flare or service-valve fittings
4. **Dirty or blocked outdoor coil.** Cottonwood, grass clippings or dirt reduce heat rejection. Pressures rise, and the compressor may trip on its internal overload.
5. **Thermostat set wrong.** Common examples:
   - It is set to FAN ON or HEAT instead of COOL.
   - The setpoint is above room temperature.
   - Weak batteries are dropping the call for cooling.
6. **Tripped outdoor breaker or pulled disconnect.** The indoor fan still runs off the air handler or furnace circuit, so it blows uncooled air.
7. **Compressor overheating on its internal overload.** This is often a result of causes 1, 3 or 4. The compressor stops for 30–60+ minutes, then restarts.
8. **Control or communication fault (variable-speed/Link units).** Possible problems include:
   - Sensor faults
   - Lost communication between the indoor and outdoor units
   - Inverter or drive faults

   These appear as thermostat alerts and need a technician with the right diagnostic tools.
9. **Failed compressor.** This is less common, and it should be confirmed only after the capacitor, contactor and wiring have been ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it is set to COOL, the fan is on AUTO, and the setpoint is at least 3°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it is dirty. A clogged filter cuts airflow and can lead to coil icing.
- **Breakers and disconnect:** Check the breaker labeled for the AC or condenser. Reset it once if it is tripped. If it trips again, stop and see the Breaker keeps tripping page. Also confirm the disconnect box near the outdoor unit is fully seated.
- **Look and listen at the outdoor unit (don't open it):** Is the fan spinning? Is the compressor running, or just humming? Hearing a hum with no fan is a strong sign of capacitor failure. Turn cooling off and call a technician.
- **Outdoor clearance:** Gently rinse off loose debris from the outside of the coil with a garden hose on a low setting. Keep about 2 feet of clearance around the unit.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return registers are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate:** Check whether a float or condensate safety switch has shut off cooling because of a full pan or clogged drain. Clear the drain line opening at the access tee if it is accessible.
- **One reset:** If the thermostat shows a lockout, turn the system off at the thermostat and breaker for 5 minutes, then restore power once. Don't keep repeating this.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically:
- Confirm the thermostat is sending a cooling call (24V at Y and C at the outdoor unit).
- Check whether the contactor is pulling in, and measure voltage across it.
- Test the run capacitor's microfarad rating against its label rating (±6% tolerance is typical).
- Measure compressor amp draw and check the windings if it won't start.
- Measure the temperature drop across the indoor coil.
- Attach gauges or probes and check superheat or subcooling against Trane's charging chart. Most Trane TXV systems are charged by subcooling.
- If the charge is low, run an electronic or nitrogen leak search before recharging.
- On Link/XV units, read fault history from the thermostat or service tool, and check the communication wiring.

**Red flag:** Be wary of a quote that recharges refrigerant without any leak search or explanation of the leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor unit hums, fan not spinning | Failed run capacitor | Turn cooling off and call | Test and replace the capacitor |
| Outdoor unit totally silent | Breaker, disconnect, contactor, no 24V signal | Check the thermostat, reset the breaker once | Diagnose the contactor and control wiring |
| Unit runs, air only slightly cool | Low refrigerant / leak | Replace the filter, call | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Unit runs, then stops for long periods | Overheating on overload | Rinse the outdoor coil gently | Clean the coil, check charge and capacitor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low charge or poor airflow | Turn off, change the filter, let it thaw | Diagnose airflow and charge |
| Thermostat shows an alert (Link/XV) | Communication or drive fault | Note the message, power-cycle once | Read fault history, repair the control |
| Blank thermostat, fan runs warm | Thermostat or power issue | Replace the batteries, check settings | Check the transformer and wiring |

## Repair costs

These are typical US price ranges, including labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Thermostat batteries or filter (DIY) | $5–$40 |
| Service call / diagnostic | $80–$200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Outdoor coil professional cleaning | $100–$300 |
| Leak search | $200–$600 |
| Refrigerant recharge, R-410A (per lb, installed) | $60–$150 |
| Minor leak repair (fitting or valve) | $200–$800 |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$2,800 |
| Condenser coil replacement | $1,500–$3,000 |
| Communicating control board / inverter drive | $500–$1,800 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800–$4,000+ |

Warranty coverage on parts can cut these costs sharply, but labor and refrigerant are usually billed separately.

## What this code means

Three flashes on a Goodman furnace control board means the **pressure switch is stuck open**. In plain terms: the inducer motor (the small fan that pulls exhaust out of the furnace) is running, but the pressure switch never sensed enough draft to close and confirm the furnace is venting safely.

The pressure switch is a safety interlock. Until it closes, the board will **not** open the gas valve or light the burners — so you get no heat. This is by design: it prevents the furnace from firing when it can't prove it is exhausting combustion gases.

Common triggers are a blocked flue or intake pipe, a weak or failing inducer, cracked or disconnected pressure hoses, or a condensate backup on high-efficiency (condensing) models.

> **Note on the legend:** The flash-code meaning is printed on the label inside the blower door. Most Goodman boards use 3 flashes for "pressure switch stuck open," but a few board revisions number codes differently. Always confirm against your own unit's chart before ordering parts.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

Which cause sits at #1 depends on what kind of furnace you have. On an **80% (non-condensing) furnace with a metal flue**, a blocked or restricted vent is usually the first suspect. On a **90%+ condensing furnace with PVC vent pipes and a condensate drain**, condensate backup at the trap, drain line, or inducer drain port is arguably the single most common trigger — so on those units, swap #1 and #2 below.

1. **Blocked or restricted flue/intake pipe** — Debris, ice, a bird nest, or snow at the vent termination reduces draft so the switch can't close. Most common in fall startup and after storms.
2. **Condensate backup (condensing/high-efficiency models)** — A clogged drain line or trap fills the pressure switch tubing area with water, blocking the pressure signal. Very common on 90%+ furnaces.
3. **Cracked, kinked, or disconnected pressure switch hose/tubing** — The small rubber hose to the switch is loose, split, or plugged with condensate.
4. **Weak or failing inducer motor** — The inducer spins but no longer moves enough air to generate the needed pressure differential.
5. **Failed pressure switch itself** — The switch diaphragm is stuck open and won't close even with proper draft.
6. **Blocked inducer housing or drain port** — Sediment or condensate in the inducer housing chokes the airflow the switch measures.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do. Everything else is inside the cabinet and is technician work.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to Heat and the setpoint is above room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A severely clogged filter can disrupt airflow. Replace it if it's dirty — this is cheap and easy. Filter sizes vary widely between Goodman cabinets, so buy the size printed on the edge of the filter you pull out rather than guessing.
- **Breaker / furnace switch:** Cycle the furnace breaker off and on once, and confirm the wall switch by the furnace (looks like a light switch) is on. This gives the board **one** chance to reset. If it locks out again, stop resetting it and call a technician.
- **Outdoor vent pipes:** Go outside and look at the PVC or metal vent/intake terminations. Clear away snow, ice, leaves, nests, or anything blocking the openings. Do **not** disassemble anything.
- **Condensate line (condensing models):** Look for standing water or a full drain pan. If the drain line is accessible and clearly clogged, you can gently flush or brush the visible portion. Do not open the furnace to reach internal tubing.

If the 3-flash code returns after these checks, stop and call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A pro follows the airflow and pressure signal from end to end. Knowing the steps helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the code** and observes the inducer starting on a call for heat.
2. **Inspects the flue and intake** for blockages, proper slope, and correct termination.
3. **Checks the pressure hose(s)** for cracks, kinks, water, or disconnection, then reconnects or replaces as needed.
4. **Tests the pressure switch** with a manometer — measuring actual inducer draft against the switch's rated set point. This tells them whether the switch or the airflow is the problem.
5. **Clears condensate blockages** in the trap, drain, and inducer drain port on high-efficiency units.
6. **Evaluates the inducer motor** for correct RPM and airflow if draft is low despite clear vents.
7. **Replaces the pressure switch or inducer** only after confirming which component is out of spec.

Be cautious of any tech who replaces the switch without measuring draft first — a bad reading often points to a blocked vent or weak inducer, not the switch.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inducer runs, no ignition, 3 flashes | Pressure switch never closes | Check outdoor vents; reset breaker once | Manometer test of draft vs. switch set point |
| Fails only in wind/cold/snow | Partially blocked or iced flue | Clear snow/ice from terminations | Inspect vent slope and termination clearance |
| Water pooling near furnace | Condensate backup | Flush accessible drain line | Clear trap, inducer drain port, internal tubing |
| Intermittent 3-flash faults | Cracked/loose pressure hose | None — internal | Inspect and replace pressure tubing |
| Inducer noisy or weak | Failing inducer motor | None — internal | Measure inducer airflow; replace motor |
| Vents clear, drain clear, still faults | Failed pressure switch | None — internal | Confirm with meter, replace switch |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US prices including parts and labor. Your area and furnace age will move these.

- **Clearing a blocked vent or condensate line:** $0 DIY (visible portions) to **$150–$250** for a service call and internal cleaning.
- **Replacing a pressure switch:** **$150–$350** installed.
- **Replacing pressure hose/tubing:** **$100–$200** as part of a service visit.
- **Replacing the inducer motor or inducer assembly:** roughly **$400–$1,200** installed. Condensing-furnace inducer assemblies are the expensive end — the OEM part alone often runs several hundred dollars before labor, so a quote well above $500 is not automatically a red flag.
- **Diagnostic / service call:** **$90–$250**, often applied to the repair. Higher-cost metros and after-hours or weekend calls sit at the top of that range.

If your furnace is well past 15 years and needs an inducer plus other parts, ask about repair-vs-replace before spending on a major component — an inducer assembly on an older condensing unit can reach a meaningful fraction of a replacement furnace, and getting a second quote is reasonable at that price.

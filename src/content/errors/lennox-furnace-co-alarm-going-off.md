## What this code means

**Emergency: act before you read further.**

1. **Get everyone out now, including pets.** Go into fresh air outside.
2. **Call 911 or your gas utility's emergency line from outside.** Tell them your carbon monoxide alarm is going off.
3. If you can do it on your way out without delay, **turn the thermostat to OFF.** Do not stay inside to investigate.
4. **Do not go back in** until responders say the air is safe.
5. **Seek medical care** for anyone with headache, nausea, dizziness or confusion.
6. **Leave the furnace off** until a licensed technician has inspected it.

A CO alarm is not a Lennox error code. It comes from a separate household detector. Lennox furnaces have no built-in CO sensor and will not display a code for carbon monoxide.

The furnace's safety switches can catch some related faults, such as blocked venting or flame rollout. But a furnace can produce dangerous CO while running normally with no fault light. When the alarm sounds near a running furnace, assume the furnace may be making CO or failing to vent it. Then rule it out with real measurements.

Note that the furnace is not the only possible source. Water heaters, gas ranges, fireplaces, generators and attached garages with running cars all trigger CO alarms. Responders will check them all.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Blocked or disconnected venting.** This includes:
   - Bird nests, debris, snow or ice blocking the flue or PVC termination.
   - Separated, sagging or corroded vent pipe.
   - A failed chimney liner on 80% models.

   Severe blockages often trip the furnace's pressure switch. Partial blockages and leaks may not trip it. How a pressure switch fault shows up depends on the control board. Some Lennox boards show an error code on a display, while many older SureLight and G-series boards blink an LED flash code instead. No single code applies to every model, so check the code chart or manual for your specific furnace.
2. **Incomplete combustion.** This happens when burners are dirty or misaligned, gas pressure is wrong, there is too little combustion air, or the furnace has been converted to propane improperly. Incomplete combustion produces high CO in the flue gases.
3. **Cracked or failed heat exchanger.** This is more common on older units. A crack can let combustion gases mix with house air. It often shows up as flame disturbance when the blower starts, and is sometimes accompanied by flame rollout.
4. **Backdrafting on atmospheric or 80% systems.** Exhaust spills back into the house instead of going up the flue. Causes include:
   - A shared water heater flue that has lost draft.
   - Strong exhaust fans pulling air out of a tight house.
   - A furnace closet that does not get enough combustion air.
5. **Another appliance or source.** The water heater, range, fireplace, generator or garage may be producing the CO.
6. **Detector fault or end of life.** An expired or defective detector can false alarm. Only conclude this after CO testing shows normal levels.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These checks come only **after** you have evacuated, called emergency services, and been cleared to go back inside.

- **Keep the furnace off** at the thermostat until a technician has inspected it. Do not do a "test run" to see if the alarm comes back.
- **Look at outdoor vent terminations from outside.** You may see snow, ice, a nest or leaves blocking the PVC pipes or flue cap.
  - If you can safely clear snow or ice by hand, do so.
  - Report what you found to the technician.
  - Still do not restart the furnace until it is cleared to run.
- **Check the detector itself.** Read its display or manual to tell the difference between a CO alarm, a low-battery chirp and an end-of-life signal.
  - Replace the batteries, or replace the whole detector if it is past its date.
  - Never use this as a reason to skip professional testing after a full alarm.
- **Look for other sources.** Check for a car or generator running near the house, or a gas range that was left on.
- **Make sure the furnace's exterior panels are seated.** A missing or loose front door panel on the blower compartment can pull combustion gases into the airflow. If you find one loose, tell the technician.

Do not open the furnace, inspect the burners, or reset any safety switches yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A thorough CO call should include the following. Use this list to judge whether the inspection was complete.

- **Ambient CO readings** taken throughout the home with a calibrated meter, to confirm or rule out a real CO event.
- **A flue-gas combustion analysis**, which measures CO (air-free), O2 and draft in the vent. This shows whether the burners are producing excess CO.
- **A full venting inspection.** The technician checks pipe slope, joints, terminations, chimney liner condition, and whether the venting meets Lennox's installation-manual requirements for the model.
- **Burner and flame inspection.** The technician looks for lifting, yellow, or disturbed flames. They also watch whether the flame changes when the blower starts, which is a classic sign of a cracked heat exchanger.
- **Heat exchanger inspection.** This is done with a camera, an assessment of how the flame behaves, and sometimes a CO test in the supply air.
- **Gas pressure checks** of manifold and inlet pressure against the rating plate.
- **Backdraft and worst-case depressurization tests** with exhaust fans running.
- **Testing of other combustion appliances** in the home.

Be cautious about a quote that condemns the heat exchanger without camera evidence, flame-change evidence or combustion readings. Ask to see photos. A second opinion is reasonable before a replacement worth $4,000 or more.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alarm plus headaches or nausea in the household | Real CO exposure | Evacuate, call 911, get medical care | Find the source and test CO; red-tag the unit if unsafe |
| Alarm during a snowstorm on a 90%+ model | Iced or snow-blocked PVC termination | After you are cleared, clear snow from the vent outside; keep the furnace off | Inspect venting and verify combustion before restarting |
| Alarm, plus soot or rust near the flue or furnace | Backdrafting or incomplete combustion | Keep the furnace off | Combustion analysis, burner service, venting and draft repair |
| Flame wavers or rolls when the blower starts | Cracked heat exchanger | Keep the furnace off | Camera inspection; replace the heat exchanger or the furnace |
| Alarm when the water heater and exhaust fans are running | Backdraft from depressurization or a shared flue | Keep the furnace off; report the pattern to the technician | Draft test, combustion air correction, liner or vent repair |
| Alarm with the furnace off | Another appliance, a vehicle or a generator | Evacuate; check for running engines | Test all appliances |
| Chirping, not full alarm | Low battery or detector at end of life | Replace the battery or the detector | None needed if CO testing is normal |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges (2026):

- **Emergency CO response** by the fire department or utility: usually free.
- **HVAC CO and combustion diagnostic:** $100–$300, more for after-hours calls.
- **New CO detector:** $25–$60 each.
- **Clearing a vent termination blockage:** $100–$250.
- **Vent pipe repair or reconnection:** $150–$600.
- **Chimney liner** for an 80% furnace or shared water heater flue: $1,000–$3,000.
- **Burner cleaning and adjustment, plus gas pressure setting:** $150–$400.
- **Combustion air correction:** $200–$800.
- **Heat exchanger replacement:** $1,500–$3,500. Parts may be covered by Lennox's heat exchanger warranty, which is often 20 years to lifetime on registered units. Labor usually is not covered.
- **Full furnace replacement:** $3,500–$8,000 or more installed. This is common when the heat exchanger fails on an older unit.

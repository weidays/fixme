## What this code means

"Leaking water" is not a fault code that a Trane AC displays. It is a symptom: water is showing up around the indoor unit (the air handler or the coil on top of your furnace), on the ceiling below an attic unit, or along the refrigerant lines.

When your AC cools, the indoor evaporator coil pulls moisture out of the air. That water drips into a drain pan and flows out through a PVC condensate line to a floor drain, the outdoors, or a condensate pump. A leak almost always means one of three things:

- Water can't get out because the drain is clogged.
- Water is escaping through a damaged pan or fitting.
- The system is making water in places it shouldn't, such as a sweating duct or a thawing frozen coil.

Many installations include a **float (overflow) safety switch** in the drain pan, the secondary pan, or the drain line. When water backs up, the switch cuts power to the cooling call, so the AC stops cooling. It **resets automatically** once the water level drops. No reset button or power cycle is needed. Whether you have a float switch depends on the installer and local code, not on the Trane model.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged condensate drain line.** Algae, sludge, dust, and debris build up in the line or P-trap. This is the most common cause by a wide margin, especially early in cooling season.
2. **Failed or clogged condensate pump.** This applies to basement and closet installs that pump water up and out. Possible failures include a stuck float, a failed motor, a clogged check valve, or a kinked discharge tube.
3. **Dirty air filter causing a frozen coil, then overflow during thaw.** Low airflow ices the coil. When it melts, the water can overwhelm the pan. The frozen-coil diagnosis is covered separately.
4. **Rusted, cracked, or dislodged drain pan.** Older metal primary pans rust through. Secondary emergency pans under attic units can also corrode or fill and spill.
5. **Improper drain installation.** Examples include a missing or incorrect P-trap on a draw-through air handler, insufficient slope, or a disconnected fitting. Without a proper trap, negative pressure can hold water in the pan until it overflows.
6. **Low refrigerant charge (leak).** This causes the coil to freeze and then overflow during thaw. It is technician-only.
7. **Condensation on uninsulated ducts or refrigerant lines.** "Sweating" in humid attics or crawlspaces is often mistaken for a unit leak.
8. **Unit not level.** The air handler or furnace may be tilted so water runs away from the drain outlet.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Turn the AC **off at the thermostat** first. If water is near the furnace, the air handler, or electrical outlets, also switch off the HVAC breaker or the service switch.

- **Contain the water.** Mop or shop-vac the area and put towels down. In attic installs, check the ceiling below for staining or sagging. If the ceiling sags, keep people clear and call a pro.
- **Check the air filter.** If it's dirty, replace it. A clogged filter is a leading cause of coil icing and thaw overflow.
- **Look for ice.** If you see frost on the refrigerant lines or the coil area, leave the system off (or run fan-only) for several hours to thaw. Then see the frozen evaporator coil guidance.
- **Check the condensate drain outlet.** Find where the line ends, usually outdoors near the foundation or at a floor drain. If it's dry while the pan is full, the line is clogged.
- **Clear the drain line from the outside end.** Hold a wet/dry vac to the exterior outlet, seal around it with your hand or a rag, and run it for 1–2 minutes. Many lines also have a capped vertical access tee near the indoor unit. You can pour about a cup of plain white vinegar into it, wait 30 minutes, then flush with water. Don't use bleach if your drain has PVC cement joints you're unsure of, and never mix bleach with other cleaners.
- **Check the secondary (emergency) pan** under attic units. If it has water, your primary drain is failing. Empty it with a shop vac.
- **Check the condensate pump** if you have one. Make sure it's plugged in, the discharge tube isn't kinked, and the reservoir isn't full of sludge. Unplug it before looking inside the reservoir. Repairing or replacing the pump is best left to a pro if it doesn't run once cleared.
- **Restart and watch.** Turn cooling back on and confirm water drips from the drain outlet within 15–30 minutes. If the float switch had tripped, cooling should resume on its own once the water clears.
- **Re-seat any exterior access panel** you removed to view the drain area. Don't open any panels inside the cabinet.

If the leak returns within a day, the drain clears but cooling still stops, or you see ice again, call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Use this list to judge whether a quote reflects real work:

- Inspect the primary drain pan, trap, and line. Clear clogs with nitrogen, a pump, or a vacuum, and confirm free flow.
- Verify the P-trap is correct and properly sized for the air handler's static pressure, and that the line slopes at least 1/4" per foot.
- Test the float/overflow switch for proper operation and wiring.
- Inspect the drain pan and secondary pan for rust, cracks, or displacement.
- Test the condensate pump: float, motor, check valve, and safety switch.
- Check airflow (filter, blower, static pressure) and look for coil icing.
- Check refrigerant pressures, superheat, and subcooling if icing is suspected. If charge is low, search for the leak. Topping off without a leak search is a red flag.
- Check the unit is level, and inspect duct and line-set insulation for sweating.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water pooling around indoor unit, no drip at drain outlet | Clogged condensate line | Shut off AC, shop-vac drain outlet, vinegar flush | Power-flush line, clear trap, correct slope |
| AC stopped cooling, pan full of water | Float switch tripped by backup | Clear drain; switch auto-resets when water drops | Test and replace float switch if faulty |
| Water around condensate pump, pump silent | Failed or clogged pump | Check plug and tube; clean reservoir with power off | Replace pump or check valve |
| Ice on coil or lines, then water leak | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace filter, thaw coil with system off | Leak search, repair, recharge; airflow fix |
| Water in secondary pan / ceiling stain | Primary pan or drain failing | Empty pan, clear drain, monitor | Replace primary pan or repair drain |
| Rusty drips from bottom of air handler | Corroded drain pan | Contain water, call pro | Replace pan (sometimes coil) |
| Water on ducts or lines in attic | Condensation from poor insulation | Note location for tech | Re-insulate ducts/line set, seal leaks |
| Leak only when running, unit visibly tilted | Unit not level | None beyond reporting | Re-level unit, adjust drain |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| DIY drain clearing (vinegar, shop vac) | $0 – $30 |
| Professional drain line cleaning/clearing | $100 – $250 |
| Float/overflow safety switch replacement | $100 – $250 |
| Condensate pump replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Drain line or P-trap re-pipe / correction | $150 – $450 |
| Secondary (emergency) drain pan replacement | $200 – $600 |
| Primary drain pan replacement | $300 – $900 (labor-heavy; may require pulling the coil) |
| Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge | $400 – $1,800+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type) |
| Duct / line-set insulation repair | $150 – $800 |
| Evaporator coil replacement (if pan is integral or coil is leaking) | $1,200 – $2,500+ |

Water damage to drywall, ceilings, or flooring is separate and can easily exceed the HVAC repair. Catching a leak early is the best way to keep costs down. Check whether the coil or pan is still under Trane's parts warranty. Labor is often not covered unless you bought an extended plan.

## What this code means

Your Goodman furnace proves it actually has a flame using a single metal rod — the flame sensor — mounted in the burner flame. When the burners light, the flame conducts a tiny electrical current (measured in microamps) from the rod back to the control board. This is called flame rectification, and it tells the board "flame is present, keep the gas valve open."

When that signal is too weak or absent, the board can't confirm flame. To stay safe, it closes the gas valve within a few seconds — even if the burners lit perfectly. On many Goodman/Amana control boards this shows as a **7-flash low flame signal** code, and repeated failures push the furnace into **ignition lockout**.

Flame sensor problems are one of the most common causes of a furnace that lights and then shuts off a few seconds later. The good news: the sensor itself is usually just dirty, not broken. The catch is that safe cleaning and confirmation require opening the cabinet and taking a microamp reading, so this is rated **pro**.

Whether the fault auto-resets or holds depends on the board and how many cycles failed: a single weak-signal event often clears on the next successful ignition, but repeated failures latch a lockout that holds until power is cycled.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or oxidized flame sensor rod.** A film of oxide, dust, or combustion residue insulates the rod and drops the microamp signal below the board's threshold. This is by far the most common cause, and a cleaning usually restores the signal.
2. **Sensor cracked or degraded beyond cleaning.** If the ceramic insulator is cracked or the rod is pitted and burned back, the signal stays low even after a careful cleaning and the sensor has to be replaced.
3. **Poor sensor position or bent rod.** If the rod isn't fully immersed in the flame, the signal reads low. This happens after burner service or if the sensor was bumped.
4. **Corroded or loose sensor wire / connection.** A poor connection at the sensor terminal or the control board weakens the returning signal.
5. **Bad ground.** Flame rectification needs a solid chassis/burner ground; a loose ground screw or corroded frame lowers the reading.
6. **Failing control board.** Less common — the flame-sense circuit on the board itself degrades and misreads a good flame.

Standard service practice is to clean and inspect the sensor, take a microamp reading, and verify the sensor wiring and ground before condemning the control board. The minimum flame signal for your specific furnace is listed on the control board label or in the unit's installation and service instructions.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps we recommend a homeowner do:

- **Replace a dirty air filter.** A clogged filter starves the burners of proper airflow and can disturb flame quality. A clean filter rules this out cheaply.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Heat and the temperature is above room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Cycle power once.** If the furnace is locked out, flip the furnace switch or breaker off, wait 30 seconds, and back on to allow **one** reset attempt. Do not repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.
- **Check visible supply and return registers.** Make sure they're open and unblocked so airflow isn't restricted.
- **Check the condensate drain (high-efficiency models).** A blocked drain backs water up until the pressure switch opens, which stops the furnace *before* the burners light — a different fault that's easy to mistake for a flame-signal problem. Make sure the line is clear and draining so you can tell the two apart.

If the furnace lights and shuts off within a few seconds after these checks, the sensor almost certainly needs professional cleaning or replacement. **Do not open the cabinet to clean the sensor yourself** — that's technician work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. Read the stored fault/flash code to confirm a flame-signal issue.
2. Watch a full ignition cycle to see burners light then drop out.
3. Put a meter in series with the sensor and measure the **microamp flame signal**, then compare it against the minimum printed on the control board or in that furnace's installation/service instructions. The threshold is board-dependent — on many Goodman/Amana boards it is well under 1 µA — while a healthy sensor generally reads in the low single-digit microamps.
4. Remove and inspect the sensor rod for oxide, cracks, or bending, then clean it with fine abrasive (never sandpaper that leaves grit).
5. Re-test the microamp reading after cleaning. If it stays low with a clean, properly positioned rod, they replace the sensor.
6. Verify the sensor wiring and the burner/chassis ground.
7. Only after the sensor, wiring, and ground check out will they suspect the control board.

If your quote jumps straight to a control board without a microamp reading and a cleaning attempt, ask why.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burners light, shut off in 2–7 seconds | Dirty flame sensor | Replace filter, cycle power once | Clean sensor, verify microamp reading |
| Repeated light-then-die, then lockout | Cracked or degraded sensor | One reset, then call a pro | Test signal, replace sensor |
| Ran fine, now short cycles on heat | Oxidized rod / poor position | Check filter and thermostat | Clean/reposition rod, re-test |
| Intermittent no-heat in cold snaps | Loose sensor wire or ground | None safe | Repair connection, tighten ground |
| Clean new sensor still reads low | Failing control board | None safe | Confirm with meter, replace board |
| No burner light at all | Not a sensor issue (igniter/gas) | Cycle power once | See "Won't ignite" / ignition lockout |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and service call fees):

- **Flame sensor cleaning (part of a service call or tune-up):** $0 if done during maintenance, or roughly **$90–$180** as a standalone diagnostic + cleaning visit.
- **Flame sensor replacement:** the sensor itself is cheap (**$10–$30**), but with labor and diagnosis expect **$120–$250**.
- **Sensor wiring / ground repair:** typically bundled into a service call, **$120–$220**.
- **Control board replacement (rare for this fault):** roughly **$400–$1,200** installed, depending on the model and whether the board is a proprietary or two-stage/variable-speed control.

Because the fix is usually a quick cleaning, this is one of the lower-cost furnace repairs — but the safe cleaning and microamp confirmation still call for a pro.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code. Your York air conditioner may be running, blowing air, or even completely silent — but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

York units don't display a single universal "not cooling" code. Depending on your model and control board, you may see a blinking LED fault pattern on the outdoor board, a communicating thermostat message, or nothing at all. Because the underlying cause ranges from a $20 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you from the cheapest, safest checks up to the point where a licensed technician is required.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops cooling output and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a miswired/failing thermostat that never calls for cooling.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood, the unit can't reject heat.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start, or the outdoor fan spins slowly.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces capacity; often shows as ice on the lines or weak, lukewarm airflow.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't close, so the compressor and fan never start.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — usually a downstream effect of low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks cooling entirely.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — unit overheats and shuts down on high-pressure protection.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit runs but produces no cooling, or trips repeatedly.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop at anything that requires opening sealed panels, touching capacitors, or handling refrigerant.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Set the fan to AUTO. If it's a battery model with a blank or dim screen, replace the batteries.
- **Air filter:** Pull it out and look at it. If it's gray and clogged, replace it. A dirty filter is the number-one cause of weak or no cooling.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs, and that the return grille is clear.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers in your panel. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for grass clippings, leaves, or debris smothering the coil fins. Gently clear obvious debris and trim back plants that crowd the unit. Never insert anything into the fan.
- **Condensate drain:** If the drain line is clogged, a float switch can shut the system off. Look for standing water in the drain pan near the indoor unit; clearing the pan and adding drain tablets can help.
- **Check for ice:** If you see frost or ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON is okay) and let it thaw fully — several hours — before running cooling again. Running a frozen system can damage the compressor.

If none of these restore cooling, the remaining causes involve refrigerant, high-voltage components, or the compressor — call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence. Knowing it helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirm the thermostat is signaling and 24V control voltage reaches the outdoor unit.
2. **Check line voltage and the contactor** — is power getting to the compressor and fan?
3. **Test the capacitor** — measure microfarads against the rated value; a weak cap is cheap and common.
4. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — read suction and head pressures, superheat, and subcooling to judge the charge. Low pressures point to a leak.
6. **Leak search if charge is low** — electronic detector or dye. A reputable tech won't just "top off" without finding the leak.
7. **Check the compressor** — amp draw, windings, and start behavior.

Ask for the actual measured readings (capacitor value, pressures, amp draw). A tech who can share numbers is diagnosing, not guessing.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower & duct static |
| No air at all, thermostat blank | Dead thermostat batteries | Replace batteries | Replace/rewire thermostat |
| Outdoor unit silent, breaker tripped | Electrical fault | Reset breaker once | Find short, replace component |
| Compressor hums, won't start; fan slow | Failed run capacitor | None — high voltage | Test & replace capacitor |
| Lukewarm air, ice on lines | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, thaw coil | Leak search & recharge |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed fan motor/cap | None | Replace motor or capacitor |
| Unit runs constantly, no cooling | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test/replace |
| Water near indoor unit, system off | Clogged condensate drain | Clear pan, add tablets | Clear line, check float switch |
| Outdoor coil caked with debris | Dirty condenser | Rinse gently with hose | Deep coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$650
- **Coil cleaning (deep):** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older units)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,800+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

Note: units using older R-22 refrigerant cost significantly more to recharge, since R-22 is phased out. On a 12+ year old system, a major repair is often a signal to consider replacement.

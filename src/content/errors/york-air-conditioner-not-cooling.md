## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single York fault code — it's a symptom. Your York air conditioner may be running (indoor blower on, outdoor fan spinning) but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or not getting your home to setpoint. On some York units with communicating controls or diagnostic LEDs, the control board may flash an error pattern; on older or non-communicating systems, there's no code at all, just poor performance.

Because "not cooling" points to many possible failures — from a $15 filter to a $2,500 compressor — this page walks you through ranking the likely cause, doing the safe checks yourself, and understanding what a technician should charge before you approve a repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (heat vs. cool), or a setpoint above room temperature.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs, so air blows but never cools.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — blocked by grass, leaves, or dryer lint; the system can't reject heat.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks cooling entirely.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a common ~$150–$350 failure that stops the compressor or outdoor fan from starting.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces or eliminates cooling; requires EPA-certified repair, not a top-off.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit sticks or burns out.
9. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive outcome; the unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — they're free and safe, and they solve a surprising number of "no cooling" calls.

- **Filter:** Pull your air filter. If you can't see light through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak or no cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is at least a few degrees below the current room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (both indoor and outdoor circuits). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear grass, leaves, and debris from around the condenser. Give it at least 2 feet of clearance on all sides.
- **Condensate:** Check the drain pan and line. A full pan can trip a safety float switch that shuts down cooling. You can clear a visible clog and empty a standing pan.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** (fan can stay ON) and let it thaw for a few hours. If it refreezes after restarting, you likely have low airflow or low refrigerant — call a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. Those are pro-only tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work systematically, not just guess. Expect them to:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat operation and call-for-cool signal.
2. **Measure temperature split** across the indoor coil (typical target is roughly 16–22°F difference between return and supply air).
3. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition.
4. **Check electrical components** — capacitor microfarad reading, contactor operation, and voltage at the outdoor unit.
5. **Attach gauges** to read refrigerant pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. This tells them whether the charge is correct or if there's a leak.
6. **Leak-search** if refrigerant is low — using electronic detectors, dye, or nitrogen pressure testing. A reputable tech will not just "top off" without finding the leak.
7. **Compressor test** — checking amp draw and windings if a compressor fault is suspected.

Ask for the measured numbers (temp split, capacitor µF, refrigerant pressures). A tech who can produce readings is diagnosing; one who quotes a compressor after a two-minute look is not.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower, coil |
| Blower runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Warm air, wrong thermostat behavior | Dead batteries / wrong mode | Set to COOL, replace batteries | Test thermostat wiring |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Frozen coil (low airflow/refrigerant) | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Check charge, airflow, leaks |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (electrical) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Cooling gradually got worse | Low refrigerant / leak | None (EPA-regulated) | Leak search & recharge |
| Outdoor coil caked with debris | Dirty condenser | Rinse gently with hose | Deep coil clean |
| Unit runs, no cooling at all | Compressor failure | None | Compressor test/replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual prices vary by region, unit size, and labor rates.

- **Air filter:** $15–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil deep cleaning:** $150–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher if using R-410A/R-454B and multiple pounds)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,000+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

A diagnostic service call typically runs **$90–$200**, often credited toward the repair.

- **York thermostat no power / blank screen** — control and wiring faults.

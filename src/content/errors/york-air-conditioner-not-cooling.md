## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on your York air conditioner — it's a symptom. Your system may be running, blowing air, and drawing power, but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

Most York residential units (Affinity, LX, Latitude series) don't display a plain-English "not cooling" message. Instead, the control board may store a fault code, flash an LED pattern, or communicating models may show a text alert on a compatible thermostat. What "not cooling" points to depends heavily on whether the compressor is running, whether the outdoor fan is spinning, and whether airflow indoors is normal.

Because the root causes range from a $10 filter to a failed compressor, this is rated **pro** — several of the likely causes (refrigerant, electrical, compressor) legally and safely require a licensed HVAC technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return air** — the single most common cause of weak cooling. Restricted airflow can also freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty or blocked outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bulged or dead capacitor prevents the compressor or condenser fan from starting; you may hear humming.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity and often freezes the indoor coil. Refrigerant does not "get used up," so low charge means a leak.
5. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; the unit runs but blows warm/weak air.
6. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a wiring/communication fault (especially on communicating systems).
7. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor fan not spinning causes the unit to overheat and trip.
8. **Contactor failure** — the outdoor unit won't energize the compressor at all.
9. **Compressor failure** — the least common but most expensive; the compressor won't start or has failed internally.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here if a step requires opening the electrical or refrigerant sections of the unit.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is well below room temperature. Try lowering it 5°F to force a call for cooling.
- **Thermostat batteries:** If it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank, replace the batteries.
- **Air filter:** Pull it out. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" complaints.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure no vents are closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs. Blocked airflow starves the system.
- **Breaker:** Check the breaker for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor unit. If a breaker is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets are unsafe.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for a completely blocked condenser (leaves, debris, tall grass). Gently clear obvious debris around it with the power off.
- **Condensate drain:** A full condensate pan or clogged drain line can trip a float switch and shut cooling down. Check for standing water near the indoor unit.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF and set the fan to ON to thaw it. Do not run cooling while frozen.

If cooling doesn't return after a fresh filter and a full thaw, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the process helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirms the thermostat is sending a signal and the low-voltage (24V) circuit is intact.
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Measure electrical** — tests the capacitor with a meter (not just by looking), checks the contactor, and confirms voltage to the compressor and fan.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to measure suction and liquid pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. This reveals low charge, overcharge, or a restriction.
5. **Leak search** — if charge is low, uses electronic detectors or dye to find the leak. A recharge without a leak repair is a red flag.
6. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw, windings, and start behavior if the compressor won't run.
7. **Read stored fault codes** — pulls board diagnostics or LED flash codes on Affinity/communicating systems.

A good tech explains what they measured before recommending a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents | Inspect blower, ductwork |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw with fan ON, replace filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or fan motor | Turn off, don't force blade | Test/replace capacitor or motor |
| Humming outdoor unit, no start | Failed run capacitor | Power off, call pro | Replace dual run capacitor |
| Unit runs but never cools enough | Low refrigerant / dirty coil | Clear debris around condenser | Leak search, recharge, coil clean |
| Outdoor unit totally silent | Contactor or breaker fault | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, control circuit |
| Blank thermostat | Dead batteries or power loss | Replace batteries, check breaker | Trace 24V wiring/board |
| Compressor won't start | Compressor or start components | None — call pro | Test windings, replace parts |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** under $10 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$600 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type (older R-410A vs newer refrigerants), and how accessible your equipment is.

## Related codes

- **York AC not turning on** — no power, tripped breaker, or contactor fault
- **York AC frozen coil / iced up** — airflow or refrigerant restriction
- **York outdoor fan not running** — capacitor or fan motor failure
- **York AC short cycling** — overcharge, sensor, or airflow issues
- **York communicating system fault** — thermostat-to-board communication loss on Affinity models

If your unit displays LED flash codes or a specific number on a communicating thermostat, note it and check your model's installation manual — code meanings differ between York board generations.

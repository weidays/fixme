## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Your York air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, thermostat calling for cool) but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or the house simply won't reach the set temperature.

York condensers and heat pumps generally do not display a plain-language "not cooling" message. Higher-end York equipment with communicating controls (such as some Affinity models) may flash LED fault codes on the control board that point to a root cause — but those codes vary by model and board revision. Always check your unit's specific service label or manual before matching a code.

This page walks through the real-world causes of lost cooling, what you can safely check yourself, and how a technician should diagnose it — so you can sanity-check any quote you're given.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the indoor coil, which can cause the coil to freeze and cooling to collapse. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat when its fins are packed with dirt, grass, and debris.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or fan motor won't start reliably, so no cooling is produced.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity and can freeze the coil. Refrigerant does not "run out" normally; low charge means a leak.
5. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a wiring/board fault that stops the cooling call.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by airflow or refrigerant problems; ice blocks heat transfer.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor fan still runs.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the outdoor unit runs but overheats and shuts down on high-pressure limit.
9. **Contactor failure** — the electrical relay that powers the compressor won't close.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive cause; often a last diagnosis after others are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are homeowner-safe and can save you a service call:

- **Air filter:** Pull it and inspect. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. Then wait for the system to run.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the fan is on **AUTO**, and the set temperature is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset once if tripped. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (unit off) if they look dirty.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch, which can shut down cooling. Empty standing water if present.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** to thaw it (can take several hours), then replace the filter.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping. Those steps require a licensed technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work in this order — use it to sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — confirm the thermostat call and measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Inspect airflow** — check filter, blower, and evaporator coil for dirt or ice.
3. **Check electrical** — test the capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and confirm voltage at the condenser.
4. **Read any board codes** — on communicating York systems, pull stored LED fault codes to guide the diagnosis.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — measure pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to confirm proper charge. Low readings point to a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if the charge is low, they should find and quote the leak repair, not just "top off" the refrigerant.
7. **Test major components** — compressor amperage draw, fan motor, and pressure switches.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to add refrigerant without first testing the capacitor, checking airflow, or looking for a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Clean blower/coil if fouled |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, test circuit |
| Outdoor fan hums but won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on refrigerant lines | Airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, new filter | Find airflow/leak cause |
| Cooling fades over days/weeks | Refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit trips high-limit | Dirty coil or bad fan motor | Rinse coil, clear debris | Replace fan motor |
| No cooling call at all | Thermostat fault | Replace batteries, check mode | Diagnose wiring/board |
| Rapid on/off cycling | Compressor or control fault | Shut off at breaker | Full diagnostic |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and contractor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$3,000+ (often worth comparing to full system replacement)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200 (often credited toward the repair)

Systems using older R-22 refrigerant cost significantly more to recharge, which often tips the decision toward replacement.

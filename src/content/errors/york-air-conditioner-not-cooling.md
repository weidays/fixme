## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on York air conditioners — it's a symptom that can point to a dozen different problems. Your system may be running (outdoor fan spinning, blower blowing) but producing no cold air, or it may not be running at all.

York's central AC units generally don't have a digital display with numeric error codes at the condenser. Instead, diagnostics live on the control board (in the air handler or furnace) and are often read through blink codes on some model years, or through your thermostat if you have a communicating York system like the Affinity or Acclimate series. Because this varies by model and installation, this page focuses on the underlying causes rather than a single code lookup.

The severity here is marked **pro** because the most damaging "not cooling" causes — low refrigerant, a failing compressor, or an electrical fault — require certified diagnosis and repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer so no cold air comes out.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the #1 pro-level cause; a system undercharged from a leak cannot pull heat out of the air.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a common part failure that stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting.
5. **Dirty or blocked condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling collapses on hot days.
6. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor fan not spinning causes high-pressure lockout and no cooling.
7. **Contactor failure** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit fails to close.
8. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive cause; the compressor hums, trips the breaker, or won't start.
9. **Thermostat or control-board fault** — miscommunication or a failed board, more likely on communicating York systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the single most common fix.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker** — check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker for both the air handler and the outdoor unit. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around and inside the condenser fins. Turn the unit off first.
- **Condensate drain** — if a full drain pan float switch has tripped, the system may shut down. Check for standing water and clear the drain line if you know how.
- **Check for ice** — if you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to FAN ONLY for a few hours to thaw it, then run cooling again. If it re-freezes, you have a refrigerant or airflow problem for a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work through a logical sequence so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — read suction and head pressures and measure superheat/subcooling to confirm proper charge. Low readings point to a leak.
4. **Leak search** — if charge is low, use an electronic detector or dye to find where refrigerant is escaping. Correct practice is to fix the leak, not just top off.
5. **Electrical checks** — test the capacitor with a meter, check the contactor, and measure amp draw on the compressor and fan motor.
6. **Component test** — confirm compressor and condenser fan operation, and read any board blink codes or communicating-thermostat fault data.

A good tech should show you their gauge readings and explain why a part failed — not just swap parts.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blowing but not cold | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace filter; check for ice | Measure charge, find and repair leak |
| Ice on copper lines / indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set to FAN, thaw, replace filter | Diagnose airflow vs. refrigerant cause |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or fan motor | Shut off unit, call pro | Test and replace capacitor or motor |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Weak capacitor or failing compressor | Shut off unit | Test capacitor; assess compressor |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Electrical fault or compressor short | Reset once only, then stop | Find fault, replace failed component |
| Warm air, unit won't start at all | Contactor, thermostat, or board | Check thermostat and breaker | Test contactor, control board, wiring |
| Weak cooling on hot days only | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse fins gently with hose | Deep-clean coil, verify charge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV / metering device:** $500–$900
- **Control board (communicating models):** $400–$800
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often makes full-unit replacement worth considering on older systems)

Note: older York units using R-22 refrigerant are especially expensive to recharge, since R-22 is phased out. A repeated leak on an R-22 system often tips the math toward replacement.

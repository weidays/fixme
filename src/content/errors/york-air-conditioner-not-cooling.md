## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a single York fault code — it is a symptom. Your York air conditioner may be running (blower and/or condenser fan spinning) but producing little or no cold air, or it may not be reducing indoor temperature to the thermostat setpoint.

Some York communicating systems (Affinity series with a communicating control board and matching thermostat) will display a specific fault or status code when a lockout occurs. Non-communicating and older Latitude/LX units generally give no on-board code — you diagnose by observation. **How faults are reported varies by model and whether your system is communicating; if you have a numeric code on your thermostat, look that specific code up rather than relying on this general page.**

Because refrigerant, compressor, and high-voltage electrical work are involved in most root causes, this is rated a **pro** repair. Homeowners can safely rule out simple issues first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops output and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil turns to ice and blocks air.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity. York AC does not "use up" refrigerant; low charge means a leak.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with dirt, grass, or cottonwood can't reject heat.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
6. **Failed contactor** — the outdoor unit doesn't energize at all.
7. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a broken C-wire.
8. **Failing compressor** — least common but most expensive; the outdoor unit runs but doesn't build pressure.
9. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor disconnect or a panel breaker has tripped.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Air filter:** Pull and inspect it. If it's gray or clogged, replace it with the correct size. A dirty filter alone can stop cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check the indoor and outdoor breakers and the outdoor disconnect. If one has tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the condenser and rinse the exterior fins gently with a garden hose (power off). Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut cooling down. Check for standing water in the pan and clear the drain line if you know how.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines, switch the system to **fan-only** and let it thaw fully (a few hours) before running cooling again.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or remove electrical panels on the outdoor unit.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed HVAC tech will typically:

1. Verify the thermostat call and confirm 24V control voltage reaching the outdoor contactor.
2. Check line-voltage supply, the run capacitor (with a meter), and the contactor for proper operation.
3. Measure temperature split across the evaporator (return vs. supply air) — a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F.
4. Connect gauges to read suction and head pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to determine refrigerant charge.
5. Inspect coils for dirt and airflow restriction, and check the blower motor.
6. If low on charge, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or bubble test) — a reputable tech repairs the leak, not just tops off refrigerant.
7. On communicating Affinity systems, pull stored fault history from the control board.

A quote that recommends "just adding refrigerant" with no leak search is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm-ish air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower & duct static |
| Ice on coil or line | Frozen evaporator | Fan-only to thaw, replace filter | Find airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Fan hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None — high voltage | Replace run capacitor |
| Runs constantly, low output | Low refrigerant (leak) | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor coil dirty | Blocked condenser | Rinse fins, clear debris | Deep coil cleaning |
| Runs but no pressure build | Failing compressor | None | Compressor diagnosis/replacement |
| Blank thermostat | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries | Check C-wire & transformer |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and unit age):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Service/diagnostic call:** $75–$200
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type; R-410A units cost more)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $600–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

If your unit is 12–15+ years old and needs a compressor or coil, ask your tech to price a full replacement — the repair may not be cost-effective.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it is a symptom. Your York air conditioner may power on, run the outdoor fan, and even push air through the vents, but the air isn't cold or the house never reaches the setpoint.

York systems handle this differently depending on the model. Basic Latitude and LX units have no display, so you diagnose by behavior and by checking the outdoor unit. Higher-end **Affinity** communicating systems (with a variable-speed inverter board and a Hx or Communicating thermostat) may log an actual fault code or show a status message at the thermostat. If your thermostat displays a specific code, look that up separately — this page covers the general "runs but won't cool" complaint.

Because cooling problems frequently involve refrigerant, sealed-system components, or high-voltage electrical parts, this is rated **pro-level**. Some causes are safe DIY checks; the rest require a licensed HVAC technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the indoor coil, dropping cooling capacity and eventually freezing the coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — dust, grass clippings, and cottonwood block heat rejection so the system can't cool.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "heat" or "fan on"), or a miswired/failing thermostat.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a common wear part; the compressor or fan struggles to start, so little or no cooling occurs.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the single most common reason for gradual loss of cooling on older systems.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks airflow entirely.
7. **Contactor failure** — the outdoor unit's electrical relay pits or sticks, so the condenser never engages.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor runs but overheats and shuts down without airflow.
9. **Tripped breaker or blown high-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
10. **Compressor failure or inverter board fault** — least common but most expensive; more likely on aging units or after electrical surges.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Everything else involves refrigerant or high voltage — leave it to a pro.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker (AC systems often have two breakers — indoor and outdoor). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or leaves choking the condenser. Clear the area and gently rinse the exterior coil with a garden hose (unit off) if it's visibly dirty.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a shut-off float switch — a clogged condensate line can stop cooling on many setups.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system OFF (fan can stay on Auto) and let it thaw for a few hours before running again.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good York tech follows a logical sequence, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint and airflow** — checks filter, blower operation, and temperature split (return vs. supply air, typically 15–20°F when healthy).
2. **Inspect the thermostat and control signal** — confirms a 24V cooling call is reaching the equipment.
3. **Check high-voltage components** — tests the capacitor with a meter, inspects the contactor, and confirms the condenser fan and compressor draw proper amps.
4. **Read any stored codes** — on Affinity communicating systems, pulls fault history from the inverter board or thermostat.
5. **Measure refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to read suction/head pressure and calculates superheat/subcooling to judge charge and airflow.
6. **Leak search if charge is low** — uses an electronic detector or dye; a proper repair finds and fixes the leak, not just tops off.
7. **Component verdict** — isolates the failed part (capacitor, motor, coil, compressor, board) and quotes the repair.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or to "replace the compressor" without pressure and electrical readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Verify airflow and coil condition |
| Outdoor unit dirty, poor cooling | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse exterior with hose (unit off) | Deep-clean coil, check pressures |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, find cause |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None | Test and replace capacitor |
| Gradual loss of cooling over weeks | Low refrigerant / leak | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Frozen coil (low airflow/charge) | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Diagnose airflow or refrigerant cause |
| Compressor runs, no outdoor airflow | Failed condenser fan motor | None | Replace fan motor and capacitor |
| Water around indoor unit, cooling stops | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear visible clog, empty pan | Clear drain line, service float switch |
| No cooling, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / power loss | Replace batteries, check breaker | Diagnose control wiring/transformer |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; regional pricing varies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Thermostat batteries:** under $10 (DIY)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (higher if the coil must be replaced)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often near the cost of replacing the whole condenser)
- **Inverter/control board (Affinity variable-speed):** $600–$1,400

If the unit is more than 12–15 years old and facing a compressor or coil replacement, ask your technician to compare the repair cost against a new system.

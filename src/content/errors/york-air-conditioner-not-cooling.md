## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on most York air conditioners — it's a symptom. Your system may be running (fan spinning, thermostat calling) but the air coming out of your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches setpoint. On York units with communicating controls (some Affinity models), you may see a fault indication at the thermostat or on the control board; on standard single-stage LX and Latitude condensers, there's often no code at all — just warm air.

Because "not cooling" can be caused by anything from a $20 filter to a failed compressor, the goal here is to help you rule out the cheap, safe stuff before you pay for a service call — and to understand what a legitimate diagnosis looks like.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the indoor coil, reducing cooling and sometimes freezing the coil.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat when coils are packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or condenser fan won't start, or hums and shuts off.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak or no cooling and often a frozen indoor coil. Always indicates a leak, never "normal" loss.
5. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit; pitted or stuck contacts prevent the compressor from running.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks all cooling.
7. **Thermostat or control wiring fault** — miscalibration, dead batteries, or a broken 24V wire.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — compressor runs but overheats and shuts down without airflow.
9. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive; the unit hums, trips the breaker, or does nothing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and lower the setpoint 5°F below room temperature. Confirm it's not in FAN-ONLY mode. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it out and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "weak cooling" complaints.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler/furnace and the outdoor unit. Reset **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets on a locked-out or shorted unit are unsafe.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser and gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (power off first). Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate:** If cooling stopped and you see a full drain pan or standing water, a clogged condensate line may have tripped a safety float switch. You can clear a visible clog, but sealed components are pro territory.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully before restarting. Persistent icing means low refrigerant or airflow problems — call a tech.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent York technician should:

1. Verify the thermostat call and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor unit.
2. Measure supply and return air temperature split (a healthy system typically shows a ~16–22°F drop).
3. Inspect and test the capacitor with a meter (against its rated microfarad).
4. Check the contactor for pitting and confirm it pulls in.
5. Inspect the condenser and evaporator coils for dirt and ice.
6. Attach gauges and measure refrigerant pressures/superheat and subcooling — this reveals a leak, overcharge, or restriction.
7. If low on refrigerant, perform a leak search (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen) rather than just topping off.
8. Test the condenser fan motor and, if needed, the compressor windings and amp draw.

If a quote jumps straight to "you need refrigerant" without a leak search, or "you need a new compressor" without electrical testing, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but not cold | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace filter; check for ice | Measure refrigerant, find/repair leak |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor fan runs | Failed capacitor or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Outdoor unit hums then quits | Bad capacitor or seized compressor | None | Meter capacitor; test compressor |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Weak airflow at vents | Clogged filter or blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents | Check blower and ductwork |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Short or failing compressor/motor | Stop resetting | Electrical fault diagnosis |
| Water near indoor unit, cooling stopped | Clogged condensate/float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $15 – $40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $0 DIY rinse – $150 professional cleaning
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150 – $400 replaced
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350 replaced
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350 – $700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500 – $1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000 – $2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500 – $2,800 (often worth weighing against a new system on older units)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80 – $180, often credited toward the repair

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type (R-410A vs older R-22), and unit age.

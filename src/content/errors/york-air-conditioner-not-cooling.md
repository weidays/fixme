## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Most York air conditioners are conventional split systems that don't display digital error codes at the thermostat the way high-end furnaces do. Instead, "not cooling" means your system is failing to remove heat from your home, even though it may still be running.

There are two broad scenarios:

- **The system runs but blows warm or room-temperature air** — usually a refrigerant, coil, or airflow problem.
- **The outdoor unit (condenser) won't start at all** — usually an electrical problem: breaker, capacitor, contactor, or a safety lockout.

Newer York communicating systems (Affinity/YXV with a Hx3 or similar thermostat) *can* store fault codes. If your thermostat shows a numbered code, note it — a technician can read it directly. For most homeowners, though, "not cooling" is diagnosed by symptom, not by a code number.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked airflow** — restricts airflow, drops efficiency, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
3. **Low refrigerant / refrigerant leak** — the system runs but can't cool. This is a repair, not a "top-off" — the leak must be found and fixed.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor and/or fan motor won't start; you may hear a hum or clicking outside.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil can't reject heat, so cooling capacity collapses on hot days.
6. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit burns or sticks, and the condenser won't energize.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor disconnect fuse or panel breaker has opened.
8. **Bad thermostat, wiring, or dead batteries** — no call for cooling reaches the equipment.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit runs but produces no cooling, often after a capacitor or hard-start problem is ignored.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here — don't open the sealed refrigerant system or the electrical panels inside the units.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the #1 cause of poor cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered and the display is dim or blank.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for tripped breakers on both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) circuits. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — don't keep resetting it.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure vents aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs. Confirm return grilles are unobstructed.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser so it can breathe. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** for a few hours to thaw it. Running a frozen system can damage the compressor.
- **Condensate line:** A clogged condensate drain can trigger a safety float switch that shuts down cooling. If you see standing water in the drain pan, clear the drain line.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the diagnostic path helps you sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirms the thermostat is sending 24V and the equipment responds.
2. **Check airflow and coil condition** — inspects the filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Measure electrical components** — tests the capacitor (microfarad reading), contactor, and voltage at the outdoor unit. A weak capacitor is a fast, common find.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures** — connects gauges to measure suction and liquid pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. This reveals a low charge or restriction.
5. **Leak search** — if the charge is low, a good tech finds the leak (electronic detector, dye, or soap) rather than just adding refrigerant.
6. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw and windings if the compressor is suspected.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search is a red flag — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so a low charge means a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blows warm air, indoor fan runs | Low refrigerant or dirty filter | Replace filter; check thermostat | Measure pressures, find/repair leak, recharge |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw, swap filter | Diagnose airflow vs. charge, fix root cause |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed capacitor | None — do not open the unit | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit fully dead | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, contactor | Reset breaker once | Test disconnect fuse, contactor, wiring |
| Weak cooling on hot days | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris, rinse fins gently | Deep coil clean, verify charge |
| System runs constantly, never cools | Undercharge or failing compressor | Verify filter and vents | Full refrigerant + compressor test |
| No response at all | Dead thermostat / bad wiring | Replace batteries | Test 24V control circuit |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Costs vary by region and unit age.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Blower motor:** $400–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 (higher if it's an older R-22 system, since R-22 is expensive and phased out)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $600–$2,000+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,200 (often the point where full replacement makes more sense on an old unit)

If your York system is 12–15+ years old and uses R-22, weigh a major repair against replacing the whole system — repeated repairs on aging equipment rarely pay off.

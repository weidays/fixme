## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on York air conditioners — it's a symptom homeowners report when the system runs but the house stays warm, or when the outdoor unit won't start at all. Depending on your York model (LX, Affinity, Latitude, or a packaged unit), the control board may or may not flash a diagnostic LED indicating a lockout or fault.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, the goal is to narrow it down. Some causes are safe for you to check and fix. Others — anything involving refrigerant, the compressor, or the sealed system — require a licensed HVAC technician. This is rated **pro** because the most likely paid repairs (capacitor, refrigerant, compressor) fall outside safe DIY territory.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, reduces cooling, and can freeze the coil. Most common and cheapest fix.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setpoint, or a failed thermostat not calling for cooling.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit (condenser) or air handler has lost power.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the fan or compressor hums but won't start, or the outdoor fan won't spin.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer, so warm air blows.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — long cool-down times, warm air, and often ice on the lines. Requires leak repair, not just a recharge.
7. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil is caked with dirt/grass, so the unit can't reject heat.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the compressor is pitted or stuck.
9. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive outcome, sometimes preceded by a failed capacitor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner. Turn the thermostat to OFF before touching filters.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 cause of poor cooling and frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it uses them.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker serving the AC or air handler. Flip it fully OFF, then back ON — **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow throughout the house.
- **Frozen coil / lines:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan can stay ON) and let it thaw for a few hours before running again.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trigger a safety float switch that shuts down cooling. Check for standing water near the indoor unit.
- **Outdoor unit:** Gently clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser and off the top grille. Do **not** open the unit or spray water into the electrical box.

If cooling doesn't return after these checks, it's time for a technician. Do not attempt anything involving refrigerant, wiring, or opening the sealed system.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the workflow helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the call for cooling** — verifies the thermostat sends a signal and 24V control power is present.
2. **Checks LED fault codes** — reads any diagnostic flash codes on the York control board (varies by model).
3. **Measures electrical components** — tests the capacitor with a multimeter, inspects the contactor for pitting, and checks amp draw on the compressor and fan motor.
4. **Inspects airflow** — examines the filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
5. **Attaches refrigerant gauges** — reads suction and head pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to determine if refrigerant is low or the system is overcharged.
6. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before any recharge.
7. **Cleans the condenser coil** if it's restricting heat rejection.

A good tech won't just "add a pound of Freon" without finding the leak. Recharging a leaking system is a temporary, non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower & coil if unresolved |
| Indoor blower runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, test circuit |
| Outdoor fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None (electrical) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, change filter | Diagnose refrigerant/airflow issue |
| Long run times, house never cools | Low refrigerant (leak) | None | Leak search & repair, recharge |
| Warm air, hot outdoor unit | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris outside | Deep coil cleaning |
| Nothing runs, thermostat blank | Dead batteries or no power | Replace batteries, check breaker | Trace control power fault |
| Unit trips breaker repeatedly | Compressor or wiring fault | Stop resetting | Test compressor, wiring, capacitor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY, free labor)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$300
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$2,000 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Fan motor replacement:** $300–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing to a full system replacement on older units)

Diagnostic/service call fees typically run $75–$200 and are sometimes credited toward the repair. Costs vary by region, refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older systems), and unit age.

## Related codes

- **York flashing LED fault codes** — check your unit's board legend; patterns indicate high/low pressure lockouts.
- **AC frozen evaporator coil** — closely tied to "not cooling" from airflow or refrigerant issues.
- **AC compressor won't start** — often a capacitor or contactor problem.
- **AC leaking water / condensate float switch trip** — can shut down cooling entirely.

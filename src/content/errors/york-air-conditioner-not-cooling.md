## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom homeowners describe when a York air conditioner runs but the house won't reach the set temperature, or the air from the vents isn't cold. On most York split systems, the outdoor unit itself has no display; diagnostics live on the indoor board and your thermostat.

Depending on your setup, "not cooling" can mean the outdoor unit never starts, it starts but shuts off quickly, or everything runs continuously yet the air stays warm. Each of those points to a different set of causes, so pin down *which* behavior you're seeing before troubleshooting.

Note: exact fault reporting varies by model and by the thermostat installed. Communicating York/Affinity systems with a compatible thermostat may show a specific alert code, while a basic single-stage LX or Latitude unit will show nothing at all.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode/setpoint, dead batteries, or a wiring/calibration fault.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant line.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — blocked by dust, grass, or debris so the unit can't reject heat.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or fan hums or won't start; a very common failure part.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
7. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — long run times, warm air, ice, hissing near coils.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Failing compressor or fan motor** — outdoor unit trips breakers or won't run at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, remove electrical panels, or bypass any safety switch.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the #1 cause of weak or no cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering the setpoint 3–5°F to force a call for cooling.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the indoor and outdoor units. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clear space.
- **Condensate drain:** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a safety float switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water near the indoor unit and clear the drain line if you're comfortable doing so.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the coil or copper lines, turn the system to OFF (fan ON is fine) and let it thaw fully for a few hours before running again. If it re-freezes, call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech should work systematically. Use this to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — confirm setpoint, mode, and measure actual supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Electrical checks** — test the run/start capacitor, contactor, and voltage at the outdoor unit; confirm the compressor and fan draw normal amps.
4. **Refrigerant readings** — connect gauges to read pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. Abnormal readings point to under- or overcharge or a restriction.
5. **Leak search** — if charge is low, a proper tech finds and repairs the leak *before* adding refrigerant, using electronic detectors or dye.
6. **Component confirmation** — verify a failed compressor or motor with resistance and amp-draw tests, not guesswork.

Be wary of anyone who wants to "just add refrigerant" without leak-testing, or who condemns a compressor without electrical readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter; open vents | Clean coil, check blower |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find cause, leak-test, recharge |
| Outdoor unit won't start | Capacitor, contactor, or power | Reset breaker once | Test and replace capacitor/contactor |
| Runs constantly, still warm | Low refrigerant / dirty condenser | Rinse condenser fins gently | Leak repair, recharge to spec |
| Buzzing/humming outside | Failed capacitor | None | Replace capacitor |
| Breaker keeps tripping | Compressor/motor fault or short | Stop resetting; call pro | Diagnose electrical fault |
| No cooling, water near unit | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain line | Clear/repair drain, reset float |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Regional pricing varies.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$300
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often worth comparing to full unit replacement)

If your York unit uses older R-22 refrigerant, recharge costs are significantly higher and replacement may be the better value.

## Related codes

- **York AC frozen evaporator coil** — the most common escalation of a "not cooling" complaint.
- **York AC compressor not starting** — humming outdoor unit, often a capacitor.
- **York heat pump not cooling in summer** — reversing valve or thermostat mode issues.
- **York AC blowing warm air** — overlaps heavily with low refrigerant and airflow faults.

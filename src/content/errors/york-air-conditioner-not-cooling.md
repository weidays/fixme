## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on York equipment — it's the symptom homeowners report most often. Your York air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, outdoor unit humming) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

York AC units are electro-mechanical systems with limited onboard diagnostics compared to furnaces. Basic single-stage units may show nothing on a display. Higher-end **Affinity communicating systems** can log fault codes at the thermostat or control board that point toward the real cause — but the exact codes and behavior vary by model and board revision. If your system has a communicating thermostat, check it for stored faults before assuming a mechanical failure.

Because "not cooling" can stem from anything from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you from cheapest to most serious causes.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with grass, dust, or debris so the unit can't reject heat.
4. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a wiring/calibration fault.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor won't start or runs weakly.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system can't absorb heat; usually shows as long run times and warm air.
7. **Tripped breaker or disconnect** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
8. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that energizes the compressor won't close.
9. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive; the heart of the system is dead or seized.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform. Everything else requires a licensed tech.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the #1 cause of poor cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the fan is on **AUTO**, and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker/disconnect:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Flip it fully OFF, then back ON — **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro; repeated trips signal an electrical fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes. Blocked returns starve the system of airflow.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float safety switch and shut down cooling. If you can see standing water in the drain pan, you may have a blockage — drain tablets help, but a fully clogged line needs professional clearing.
- **Ice check:** Look at the copper lines and indoor coil. If you see ice, **turn the system OFF at the thermostat, set the fan to ON**, and let it thaw for several hours before running again. Running a frozen system can damage the compressor.

Do **not** open sealed refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good York tech follows a logical sequence — knowing it helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verifies the complaint** — checks supply/return temperature split (a healthy system typically shows a 16–22°F drop). A low split confirms poor cooling.
2. **Inspects airflow** — filter, blower operation, coil condition, and duct issues.
3. **Checks electrical** — measures voltage at the disconnect, tests the **capacitor** with a meter, and inspects the **contactor** for pitting.
4. **Reads control board / communicating faults** — on Affinity and other communicating systems, pulls stored codes.
5. **Takes refrigerant readings** — gauges pressures and superheat/subcooling to confirm proper charge or diagnose a leak. Low charge points to a leak search (dye or electronic detector).
6. **Tests the compressor** — checks amp draw and windings if it won't start.

Be wary of any tech who "just adds refrigerant" without finding the leak — refrigerant doesn't get used up. A recharge without a leak repair is a temporary fix, and the EPA requires leaks be addressed.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm-ish air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower & ducts if it persists |
| Ice on lines/indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Check charge, find leak, clean coil |
| Outdoor unit not running | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, capacitor, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Long run times, house never cools | Low refrigerant / dirty condenser | Rinse debris off outdoor coil (power off) | Leak search, recharge, coil clean |
| No cold air, thermostat blank | Dead batteries or power loss | Replace batteries, check breaker | Diagnose thermostat/wiring |
| System short-cycles or shuts off | Float switch / drain clog / safety fault | Check condensate pan | Clear drain, diagnose safety lockout |
| Warm air, compressor won't run at all | Compressor failure | None — call pro | Test compressor, quote replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0–$10 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$450
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $300–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,900+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

If your York unit is 12+ years old and facing a compressor or coil failure, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison, especially if it still uses R-22 refrigerant, which is expensive and phased out.

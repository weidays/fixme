## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on most York systems — it's a symptom you're describing, not something flashing on a board. Your York condenser may be running normally, running but blowing warm air, short-cycling, or not turning on at all.

Some newer York communicating systems (YXV, YZV with S1-series controls and a compatible communicating thermostat) will store fault or status codes on the thermostat or control board. If you have one of those, note any code shown — it narrows the diagnosis considerably. Older single-stage York units have no display and give no code, so diagnosis relies on measurements a technician takes.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a dirty filter to a dead compressor, this page walks you through the likely causes from cheapest to most serious.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, freezes the evaporator coil, and kills cooling. The #1 cause and the first thing to check.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a wiring/call issue means the system never gets told to cool.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser has its own breaker; if it's tripped, the indoor fan may still run and blow warm air.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat when caked with dirt, grass and cottonwood, drastically cutting capacity.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or fan motor hums, buzzes, or won't start.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes warm air, ice on the lines, and poor cooling. Refrigerant is never "used up," so low charge always means a leak.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil turns to a block of ice and blocks all cooling.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the condenser is pitted or stuck, so the outdoor unit never energizes.
9. **Bad compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive failure. Compressor won't start or trips the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner. Do not open the electrical panel of the condenser or touch refrigerant lines.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the fan to **Auto**, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for the indoor **and** outdoor unit breakers. If one has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop — call a tech (a repeat trip signals a real fault; don't keep resetting).
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs or closed registers aren't blocking airflow in the house.
- **Condensate drain:** If the indoor unit has shut off and you see a full drain pan or a float switch, a clogged condensate line may have tripped a safety. Clearing the drain line and pan is safe to attempt.
- **Ice check:** Look at the copper lines and the indoor coil. If you see ice, turn the system to **Off** and set the fan to **On** to let it thaw before calling — running a frozen unit makes things worse.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — confirms modes, checks the thermostat call, and measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Inspect airflow** — checks the filter, blower, and evaporator coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Check electrical** — tests the capacitor with a meter, inspects the contactor, and confirms the condenser is getting 240V.
4. **Read any codes** — on communicating York systems, pulls stored faults from the board or thermostat.
5. **Take refrigerant readings** — connects gauges to measure pressures and temperatures (superheat/subcooling) to determine if the charge is correct.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before recharging.
7. **Test the compressor** — checks amp draw, windings, and start behavior.

A good tech explains *why* they reached a conclusion — not just "it needs refrigerant." Low charge without finding the leak is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor fan runs, air is warm | Dirty filter, low refrigerant, or tripped outdoor breaker | Replace filter; reset outdoor breaker once | Measure charge, find/repair leak, recharge |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor blows warm | Tripped breaker, bad contactor or capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Ice on copper lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn to Off, fan On to thaw; replace filter | Diagnose airflow or charge, repair cause |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None — high voltage | Test and replace capacitor |
| Weak cooling, high energy bills | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse outer fins gently with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean, verify charge |
| Indoor unit shut off, water near air handler | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear drain line and pan | Clear blockage, test safety switch |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted compressor or motor | Stop resetting; call a pro | Test compressor/motor, replace if failed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 service call
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often worth weighing against a full system replacement on older units)

Note: units using older R-22 refrigerant cost far more to recharge, since R-22 is phased out and expensive. On aging R-22 systems, a major repair often points toward replacement.

## Related codes

- **York frozen evaporator coil** — low airflow or low charge causing ice.
- **York AC not turning on** — no power, bad contactor, or thermostat fault.
- **York condenser fan not spinning** — capacitor or fan motor failure.

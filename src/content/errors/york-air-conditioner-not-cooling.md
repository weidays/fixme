## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Your York air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, thermostat calling for cool) but the air coming from your vents is warm, room temperature, or only slightly cool. On systems with a diagnostic control board or communicating thermostat, you may see a related fault or status light, but many York single-stage units give no code at all — they simply fail to cool.

The root problem is almost always one of three things: **airflow is restricted**, **the refrigerant charge is wrong**, or a **major electrical component (capacitor, contactor, compressor) has failed**. Some of these you can rule out safely; the rest require a licensed HVAC technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can lead to a frozen evaporator coil that blocks cooling entirely.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" instead of "cool"), or incorrect programming.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice on the indoor coil or the copper line at the outdoor unit.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dirt insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan motor hums or won't start.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** to the outdoor unit — indoor blower runs but the condenser is dead.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cooling gets progressively worse; ice or hissing may appear. Requires professional leak repair.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck open.
9. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive outcome; the outdoor unit hums, trips the breaker, or does nothing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform. If they don't solve it, stop and call a pro.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of poor cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the temperature is set several degrees below room temp, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel and the outdoor disconnect for a tripped breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop — that signals an electrical fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow. Open closed vents.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch that shuts down cooling. Check for standing water in the drain pan; clearing a visible clog is safe.
- **Ice check:** Look at the indoor coil and the large copper line at the outdoor unit. If you see ice, **turn the system to OFF (fan ON)** to let it thaw fully — running an iced unit makes it worse. Then replace the filter.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work through a logical sequence — you can sanity-check a quote against this:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirm 24V control voltage reaches the outdoor unit.
2. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower operation, coil condition, and static pressure.
3. **Check the capacitor** with a meter (microfarad rating within spec) and inspect the contactor for pitting.
4. **Measure electrical draw** on the compressor and condenser fan motor.
5. **Attach gauges** to read suction and liquid pressures, then calculate **superheat/subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct.
6. **Leak search** if the charge is low — dye, electronic detector, or soap bubbles — because topping off refrigerant without fixing the leak is a code violation and a waste of money.
7. **Compressor test** — checking windings and amp draw if all else points to it.

A trustworthy diagnosis identifies the *why*, not just "you're low on Freon."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, dirty filter | Clogged filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Check coil, static pressure |
| Ice on indoor coil or line | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Leak search, recharge |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Failed capacitor or compressor | None — call pro | Test/replace capacitor or compressor |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Cooling slowly worsens | Refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Find and repair leak, recharge |
| Dirty outdoor coil | Debris blocking heat rejection | Gently rinse coil, clear brush | Deep coil clean, verify charge |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries | Replace batteries | Replace/rewire thermostat |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included where noted:

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$200
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$400
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type; R-410A pricing varies)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,000+ (often worth comparing against a new system if the unit is 10+ years old)

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type, and whether your unit is under warranty — many York units carry a 10-year parts warranty if registered, which can drastically cut part costs (you'll still pay labor).

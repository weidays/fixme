## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your York air conditioner runs (or tries to run) but the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On York units with a diagnostic board, you may or may not see a fault indicated depending on the cause; airflow and refrigerant problems often produce **no error at all**, which is why homeowners describe it simply as "not cooling."

The behavior varies by model. On basic **Latitude** and single-stage **LX** units, there's minimal onboard diagnostics, so you're troubleshooting by observation. On higher-end **Affinity** communicating systems, the thermostat or control board may log a fault or show a status message — but the meaning of those codes differs by board generation, so always check the label inside your air handler or condenser access panel.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, freezes the coil, kills cooling. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection.
3. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a bad thermostat.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the condenser is pitted or stuck.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks all cooling.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cooling fades over days or weeks; requires a pro.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — unit overheats and shuts down on high-pressure.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the unit runs but produces no cold air at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe and often solve the problem:

- **Air filter** — Pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Do this first.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker** — Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (both the indoor air handler and outdoor condenser may have their own). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal an electrical fault.
- **Vents and registers** — Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (power off).
- **Condensate drain** — If the indoor unit has stopped and there's water near it, the drain may be clogged and triggered a safety float switch. You can clear a visible clog or run a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain line end.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. If the outdoor breaker trips repeatedly, leave it off and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap to expensive, and you can sanity-check their process:

1. **Confirm the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or restriction.
3. **Verifies power and controls** — tests the thermostat call, transformer (24V), contactor, and capacitor with a multimeter.
4. **Reads any board status** — on Affinity/communicating units, pulls fault history from the board or thermostat.
5. **Gauges the refrigerant** — connects gauges to read pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before recharging (recharging without fixing the leak is a red flag).
7. **Tests the compressor and fan motor** — checks amp draw and windings if the outdoor unit won't start.

Ask for the measured temperature split and refrigerant readings — a legitimate diagnosis includes numbers, not just "it needs freon."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Inspect coil/blower if unresolved |
| Indoor coil iced over | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, let thaw, replace filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Low refrigerant leak | None — call a pro | Locate leak, seal, recharge |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None | Replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit hot, shuts off | Failed condenser fan motor | Clear debris | Replace fan motor |
| Runs constantly, no cooling | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test/replacement |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or transformer | Replace batteries | Test transformer/wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Breaker reset:** $0 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $650–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,800+ (often worth weighing against a new unit)

Prices vary by region, unit size (tonnage), and refrigerant type. Units using older R-22 cost more to recharge because that refrigerant is phased out.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific York fault code — it is a symptom. Your York air conditioner may be running, blowing air, and drawing power, but the air coming from your vents is not cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

Because this is a symptom rather than a single stored code, the root cause can be anything from a dirty filter to a failed compressor. Some newer York systems with communicating control boards (Affinity series) may store diagnostic fault codes or flash LED patterns on the board — check your specific board's legend, as **the codes and behavior vary by control board generation and by whether your unit is single-stage or two-stage.**

This page walks you through the most likely causes, what you can safely check yourself, and what a technician should do so you can sanity-check any quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricts airflow, reduces cooling, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Ice on the indoor coil blocks airflow and heat exchange. Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — A grass-, dust-, or lint-caked outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling drops off.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — A weak or blown capacitor prevents the compressor or condenser fan from starting. Very common failure part.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Reduces capacity and freezes coils. Always a leak — refrigerant is not "used up." Requires a certified tech.
6. **Thermostat problem** — Dead batteries, wrong mode, or a miscalibrated thermostat.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — The outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
8. **Failed contactor** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
9. **Failed compressor** — The most expensive cause; the AC hums or trips but won't cool.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or bypassing a safety device.

- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL, the fan is on AUTO, and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Inspect and replace the air filter.** A clogged filter is the #1 cause. Swap in a clean one and give the system time to recover.
- **Check the breakers.** Look for a tripped breaker for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset it **once.** If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Look for ice.** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully — this can take several hours. Then replace the filter.
- **Clear the outdoor unit.** Remove leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance. Gently rinse the coil fins with a garden hose (power off).
- **Check the condensate drain.** A clogged drain can trip a float switch that shuts down cooling. Look for standing water in the drain pan.
- **Confirm vents are open** and no return grilles are blocked by furniture or rugs.

Do **not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or repeatedly reset a unit that locks out or trips a breaker.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work methodically. Use this to sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat call for cooling with a meter.
2. **Read any board fault codes / LED flashes** on communicating York boards.
3. **Measure supply and return air temperature split** (typically 16–22°F on a healthy system).
4. **Check capacitor microfarads** against the rated value and inspect the contactor.
5. **Verify voltage and amp draw** at the compressor and condenser fan motor.
6. **Attach gauges to read refrigerant pressures** and calculate superheat/subcooling to determine charge and detect a leak.
7. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector or dye) if the charge is low.
8. **Inspect the coils** indoor and out for airflow and cleanliness.

Be wary of any tech who wants to "just add refrigerant" without finding the leak — that's a temporary, non-compliant fix that will fail again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, low airflow | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Verify airflow, check blower |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, recharge, check coil |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or fan motor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or motor |
| Indoor blower runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, wiring, board |
| Unit hums, won't start | Weak capacitor or seized compressor | None | Test capacitor, compressor |
| House won't cool on hot days | Dirty condenser or low charge | Rinse outdoor coil | Check pressures, subcooling |
| System cycles then stops | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain, check pan | Clear line, test float switch |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or no power | Replace batteries | Check transformer, wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Prices vary by region, model, and labor rates.

- **Air filter:** $0–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $100–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often worth comparing to a full system replacement on older units)

If your York system is over 12–15 years old and facing a compressor or coil replacement, ask your contractor for a repair-vs-replace comparison, especially given the phase-out of older refrigerants.

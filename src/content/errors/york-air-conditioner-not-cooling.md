## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint York homeowners report. Your air conditioner may be running (the outdoor fan spins, the indoor blower moves air), but the air coming from the vents is warm, lukewarm, or just not cold enough to hold your set temperature.

Because this is a symptom rather than a stored error, there's no single part to blame. The root cause could be as simple as a clogged filter or as serious as a failed compressor. Newer York units with communicating or inverter controls (some Affinity models) may show an actual fault code on the thermostat or control board — if you have one, note it, since it narrows the diagnosis considerably.

The goal of this page is to help you rule out the cheap, safe fixes first, then understand what a technician should be checking so you can sanity-check any quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so the system runs but barely cools.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "on" instead of "auto"), or a miscalibrated setting.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — refrigerant doesn't get "used up"; if it's low, there's a leak. Causes weak cooling and coil icing. Requires a pro.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a bulged or dead capacitor keeps the compressor or condenser fan from starting. Common and relatively inexpensive.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit can pit or stick, cutting power to the compressor.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — a result of low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil turns to ice and blocks cooling entirely.
8. **Clogged condensate drain / safety switch trip** — a full drain pan trips a float switch that shuts the system down on some installs.
9. **Failed compressor** — the least common but most expensive. The unit hums or trips the breaker but won't cool.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Stop here and call a pro for anything else.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the temperature is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto** (not On). Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the AC. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop — that's an electrical fault for a pro.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure the condenser isn't buried in leaves, grass clippings, or debris. Clear at least two feet of space around it. Gently rinse the fins with a garden hose from the outside if they look dirty — never pressure-wash.
- **Vents and registers:** Confirm supply and return vents inside the house are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water pooling near the indoor unit or the system shut off suddenly, the drain may be clogged and tripped a float switch. You can clear a visible clog, but persistent overflow needs a pro.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system **Off** and set the fan to **On** to let it thaw for a few hours before restarting. Don't chip at the ice.

If none of these solve it, call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — knowing it helps you spot a quote that skips steps or jumps straight to the most expensive repair:

1. **Verifies the complaint** and checks the thermostat call and control board (reads any stored fault codes on communicating York models).
2. **Measures the temperature split** — the difference between return and supply air. A healthy split is roughly 16–22°F; a low split points to airflow or refrigerant issues.
3. **Inspects airflow** — filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
4. **Checks electrical components** — tests the run capacitor with a meter, inspects the contactor for pitting, and confirms proper voltage at the compressor and fan.
5. **Takes refrigerant readings** — connects gauges to measure pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling. Low readings indicate a leak, which they'll then locate.
6. **Tests the compressor** — checks amp draw and windings if the unit hums, trips, or won't start.

Ask the tech what the temperature split and refrigerant readings were. Concrete numbers separate a real diagnosis from a guess.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower and coil if it recurs |
| Warm air, good airflow | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris, rinse fins | Deep-clean coil, check charge |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, recharge, verify airflow |
| Outdoor unit humming, not spinning | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| No power to outdoor unit | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| System shut off, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear visible clog | Clear drain, reset float switch |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Diagnose thermostat wiring/board |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Electrical fault or compressor | Stop — do not reset again | Diagnose compressor/electrical |

## Repair costs

Ranges below are typical US figures including parts and labor. Your area, unit age, and refrigerant type affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil deep cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ (older R-22 units cost significantly more)
- **Fan motor replacement:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,200+ — often it's worth comparing against a full system replacement, especially on units over 10 years old.

A note on refrigerant: if a tech just "tops off" your system without finding the leak, you'll be back to warm air within a season. Insist on leak detection.

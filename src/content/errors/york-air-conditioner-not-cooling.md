## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code on your York air conditioner — it's a symptom. Your York outdoor unit and indoor air handler or furnace coil work together to move heat out of your home. When cooling fails, it usually means one part of that chain has broken: airflow, refrigerant, electrical, or the compressor itself.

Some York units, especially communicating **Affinity** systems with the S1 control board, will store diagnostic fault codes you can retrieve at the thermostat or control board. Standard **LX** and **Latitude** models may only flash an LED code on the furnace or air-handler board. Whether you get a stored code depends entirely on your model and board — check your unit's service label and manual before assuming.

This is rated **pro** because the most likely root causes — refrigerant leaks, failed compressors, and electrical faults inside the condenser — require certification, tools, and safety training to diagnose and repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Dirty or blocked condenser coil** — the outdoor coil covered in grass, leaves, or dirt can't reject heat, so cooling drops off.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. Ice on the indoor coil or copper lines blocks cooling entirely.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure. The compressor or fan hums but won't start, or the outdoor fan doesn't spin.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system runs constantly but blows warm or barely cool air.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
7. **Thermostat fault or dead batteries** — the system never gets the call for cooling.
8. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that energizes the compressor is pitted or stuck.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome. The unit runs but produces no cooling at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform. Do **not** open the electrical panel of the outdoor unit, handle refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

- **Check the air filter.** A clogged filter is the #1 cause of poor cooling. Replace it if it's dirty and see if performance improves over a few hours.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. If it's battery-powered, replace the batteries.
- **Check the breakers.** Look for a tripped breaker for both the indoor and outdoor units. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal a real fault.
- **Check for ice.** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper refrigerant lines, turn the system to OFF (fan can stay ON) and let it thaw fully for a few hours before running it again.
- **Check the supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking airflow.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a safety float switch and stop the system. Clearing a visible clog and emptying the pan is safe.
- **Rinse the outdoor coil.** With the unit OFF, gently rinse leaves and debris off the outdoor condenser with a garden hose from the inside out. Never use a pressure washer.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the process helps you sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the complaint and airflow.** They'll confirm the thermostat call, check the filter and blower operation, and measure temperature split across the coil.
2. **Retrieve any stored codes.** On Affinity/communicating systems they'll read the S1 board or thermostat history; on others they'll read the LED flash code.
3. **Check electrical components.** They'll test the capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and verify voltage to the compressor and fan motor.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges.** A certified tech reads suction and liquid pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct or if there's a leak.
5. **Leak search if charge is low.** Using electronic detectors or dye to locate the leak — a proper repair fixes the leak, not just tops off refrigerant.
6. **Compressor testing.** If everything else checks out, they'll test compressor windings and amp draw to confirm or rule out compressor failure.

A trustworthy quote names the specific failed part and its measured value (e.g., "capacitor reads 32 µF, rated 45 µF").

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower & duct static |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or motor | None — call pro | Test/replace capacitor or motor |
| Unit hums, won't start | Bad capacitor or contactor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor/contactor |
| Runs constantly, barely cools | Low refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Leak search & recharge |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| No cooling at all, unit runs | Failed compressor | None — call pro | Test & replace compressor |
| System never turns on | Thermostat / dead batteries | Replace batteries, check settings | Test thermostat wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $5 – $40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 professional
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150 – $400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300 – $650 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500 – $1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000 – $2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500 – $2,500+ (often makes replacing an older unit worth considering)

If your York unit is under its parts warranty (typically 10 years with registration), you may pay only labor on major components — always ask the tech to check warranty status by serial number.

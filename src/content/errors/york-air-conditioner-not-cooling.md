## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code on most York air conditioners. Standard single-stage York units (LX and Latitude series) do not display error codes at the outdoor unit — they rely on thermostat behavior and physical symptoms. Communicating systems (some Affinity series with a compatible York communicating thermostat) may show fault messages at the thermostat, but the exact codes vary by control board and thermostat model.

When your York AC is "not cooling," the system is calling for cooling but the air at your supply vents is warm, weak, or the house never reaches setpoint. This points to a problem with airflow, refrigerant charge, electrical components, or the compressor itself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked airflow** — restricts air across the evaporator coil, reducing cooling and sometimes freezing the coil.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — a coil packed with grass, dust, or debris can't reject heat, so cooling drops off, especially on hot days.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a miswired/failing thermostat that isn't sending the cooling call.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks the coil and no cold air reaches the house.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure that stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting; you may hear humming or clicking.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system runs but can't cool; requires EPA-certified diagnosis and repair.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler has no power.
8. **Contactor failure** — the electrical switch that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive failure; the unit runs but produces no cooling at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. Do not open the outdoor disconnect, touch capacitors, or handle refrigerant.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it uses them.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Flip it fully OFF, then ON once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting it.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear grass, leaves, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil fins with a garden hose (unit off) if they look dirty.
- **Condensate drain:** Check for standing water near the indoor unit. A clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut down cooling.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF and run only the FAN for a few hours to thaw it before restarting.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. Verify the thermostat is calling for cooling and confirm 24V signal at the equipment.
2. Check power at the disconnect, contactor, and capacitor with a meter.
3. Test the run capacitor and contactor for failure.
4. Inspect and measure airflow, filter, and blower operation.
5. Attach refrigerant gauges to read pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to confirm proper charge.
6. If low, perform a leak search (electronic detector, dye, or bubbles) rather than just topping off.
7. Test compressor windings and amp draw if the compressor is suspect.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without mentioning a leak search, ask why — topping off a leaking system is a temporary fix that violates good practice.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Verify airflow, clean coil |
| Warm air, normal airflow | Low refrigerant | None — check for ice | Leak search, seal, recharge |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker / contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, capacitor, wiring |
| Humming, no fan/compressor | Failed run capacitor | None | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or refrigerant | Fan-only to thaw | Find root cause, recharge if needed |
| No cooling, no thermostat response | Dead batteries / thermostat fault | Replace batteries | Test/replace thermostat, check wiring |
| Water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, tablets | Clear line, test float switch |
| Unit runs constantly, no cooling | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test / replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $100–$350
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$1,800+ (often worth weighing against replacing an older unit)

Older York units using R‑22 refrigerant cost significantly more to recharge because R‑22 is phased out — factor this into repair-vs-replace decisions.

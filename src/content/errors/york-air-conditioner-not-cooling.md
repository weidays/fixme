## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on a York system — it's a symptom. Your York air conditioner may be running, blowing air, and showing no error at the thermostat, yet the air coming from your vents is warm or only mildly cool. On some setups the equipment appears to run normally while the house temperature keeps climbing.

York units span a wide range of designs. Basic **LX** and **Latitude** series units use simple control boards or none at all in the condenser, so there's often no diagnostic display. Higher-end **Affinity** communicating systems may log a fault or show a status code at the thermostat or control board. Because behavior varies significantly by model and board revision, treat any on-screen code as a starting point and confirm the actual condition at the equipment.

This is rated **pro** because the leading real causes — refrigerant loss, electrical component failure, and compressor problems — require test equipment, EPA certification, or both.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked indoor coil** — restricted airflow is the single most common reason a system runs without cooling well. Easy to check.
2. **Dirty or blocked outdoor condenser coil** — grass, leaves, and dryer lint insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — a weak or dead capacitor stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting. Very common and relatively cheap to replace (pro job).
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes poor cooling, ice on the lines, or long run times. Requires leak detection and EPA-certified handling.
5. **Failed or stuck contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit. Pitted contacts prevent the compressor from energizing.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — usually a downstream effect of low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks cooling entirely.
7. **Clogged condensate drain safety switch** — a full drain pan can trip a float switch that stops cooling to prevent water damage.
8. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit hums, trips the breaker, or won't start.
9. **Thermostat, wiring, or control board fault** — miscalibration, dead batteries, or a bad board can leave the outdoor unit unpowered.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model. Try lowering it several degrees.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling.
- **Breaker:** Check the panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler or outdoor unit. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't choking airflow.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the condenser. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (power off).
- **Condensate drain:** If you see standing water in the pan or a float switch, the drain may be clogged. Drain tablets and clearing the line can help.

Do **not** open the electrical compartment, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will typically:

1. Verify thermostat call and confirm 24V signal is reaching the contactor.
2. Measure voltage and capacitor microfarads (µF) against the rating printed on the capacitor.
3. Inspect the contactor for pitting and confirm it pulls in.
4. Attach gauges to read suction and head pressures, then calculate **superheat and subcooling** to judge the refrigerant charge — this is where low charge or a leak shows up.
5. Check temperature split across the indoor coil (typically 16–22°F on a healthy system).
6. Test compressor windings and amp draw if it won't start.
7. If refrigerant is low, perform a leak search rather than simply refilling.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without mentioning a leak search, ask questions — refrigerant doesn't get used up, so low charge means it leaked out.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter or coil | Replace filter, clear vents | Deep-clean coils, verify airflow |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Failed run capacitor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn off, replace filter, let thaw | Leak search, recharge, coil check |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Contactor or capacitor failure | Check breaker once | Test contactor/capacitor, board |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted compressor or wiring | Stop resetting, call pro | Amp draw, winding test |
| Water near air handler, no cooling | Tripped condensate float switch | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, test float switch |
| Nothing runs at all | Thermostat, board, or power | Check batteries and breaker | Verify 24V, test control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and model differences apply):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Run/dual capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Control board (Affinity communicating):** $400–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ — often makes replacing the whole outdoor unit worthwhile on older systems

If your York unit is 12+ years old and facing a compressor or coil failure, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint homeowners bring to a York air conditioner. The unit may be running (fan spinning, compressor humming) but the air coming from the vents is warm or only slightly cool, or the system runs constantly without reaching the thermostat setpoint.

Some York systems paired with a communicating thermostat or control board **will** display a specific fault (low pressure, high pressure, or a lockout). If you see a code on your thermostat or an LED flash pattern on the outdoor or indoor control board, note it — it narrows the diagnosis considerably. The exact codes and flash patterns **vary by model and board generation**, so check the wiring diagram label inside your unit's access panel rather than assuming.

This page covers the general "warm air / won't reach setpoint" situation on York split-system and package units.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a miswired/failing stat calling for the wrong stage.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — ice on the indoor coil from low airflow or low refrigerant; blows warm air until it thaws.
4. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood, so the unit can't reject heat.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** to the outdoor unit — indoor fan runs, outdoor unit is dead.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure; compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit won't close.
8. **Low refrigerant / leak** — reduces cooling capacity; almost always indicates a leak that must be found and repaired.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive cause; unit runs but produces little or no cooling.
10. **Blocked condensate drain triggering a safety float switch** — some setups shut down cooling to prevent overflow.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a locked-out unit.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak cooling and frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering the setpoint 3–5°F to force a call for cooling.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the indoor and outdoor units. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping means a fault.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure it's not buried in leaves, grass clippings, or debris. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance. Gently rinse the outside of the coil fins with a garden hose (low pressure, no power tools).
- **Vents and registers:** Confirm supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF but leave the fan set to ON to help it thaw. Do not run cooling until it's fully thawed.
- **Condensate drain:** If there's water around the indoor unit or a full drain pan, the drain line may be clogged and a float switch may have shut the system down. A wet/dry vac on the drain line outlet can clear a simple clog.

If cooling doesn't return after these checks, the problem is in the sealed system, electrical, or mechanical components — call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good York tech works from cheapest and most likely to most expensive. Expect them to:

1. Verify the thermostat call and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor unit.
2. Check the run/start capacitor with a meter — a bulging or out-of-spec capacitor is a fast, cheap fix.
3. Test the contactor for pitted contacts or a coil that won't pull in.
4. Inspect the evaporator and condenser coils for dirt or ice.
5. Connect **refrigerant gauges** and take temperature readings (superheat/subcooling) to judge the charge and system performance.
6. If low on charge, perform a **leak search** — because a properly installed system doesn't lose refrigerant. Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.
7. Test compressor windings and amp draw if capacitor and charge check out.

Sanity check: if a quote says "just needs a recharge" with no mention of a leak search, ask where the refrigerant went. A legitimate diagnosis explains the root cause, not just the symptom.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Check coil for ice/dirt |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Verify airflow, test charge, find leak |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor dead | Tripped breaker or capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test capacitor, contactor, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant / leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Warm air, high energy bills | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse fins with hose | Deep coil clean, check charge |
| System shut off, water near unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain with wet/dry vac | Clear line, test float switch |
| Little/no cooling, high amps | Failing compressor | None — call pro | Compressor test / replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Regional rates and model complexity (two-stage and variable-speed units cost more) will move these numbers.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 professional
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$300
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,200 (more if the leak is in the evaporator coil)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (on an older unit, replacement may be more economical)

If the system is more than 12–15 years old and facing a compressor or coil replacement, ask your tech for a repair-vs-replace comparison. R-410A phase-down and refrigerant costs can tip the math toward a new system.

## Related codes

- **York high-pressure lockout** — often a dirty condenser or overcharge; presents as a shutdown, not warm air.
- **York low-pressure lockout** — commonly low refrigerant or a frozen coil.
- **Frozen evaporator coil** — overlaps heavily with this "not cooling" complaint.
- **Outdoor fan not running** — leads to overheating and loss of cooling.
- Check your specific board's LED flash pattern or communicating thermostat message; York fault codes **vary by model and board**, so match against the label inside your unit's panel.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single error code — it's a symptom your York air conditioner is running (or trying to run) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On York systems this can show up in a few ways: the outdoor condenser hums but the fan won't spin, the indoor blower runs with warm air, or the unit short-cycles on and off.

York's higher-end **Affinity** communicating systems may display fault codes on the control board or a smart thermostat, while **LX** and **Latitude** series units are simpler and give you no code at all — just weak or no cooling. Because behavior varies by model and board, the checks below focus on the physical causes rather than a specific display code.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor coil, which then blocks cooling entirely.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — a clogged outdoor coil can't reject heat, so cooling capacity drops sharply.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes warm air, ice on the copper lines, and long run times. Requires a licensed tech.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bad capacitor stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting; you may hear a hum or click.
5. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a wiring/calibration fault.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from airflow or refrigerant problems; blocks all cold air.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the compressor is pitted or stuck.
9. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive cause; the unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or electrical panels beyond the breaker.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the AC (there are usually two — indoor air handler and outdoor unit). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal a real fault.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance on all sides.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full condensate drain pan or a clogged drain line — some units have a safety float switch that shuts off cooling when the pan overflows.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and run the fan only to thaw it (can take several hours), then check the filter.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work through the system methodically so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and coils for restriction or ice.
3. **Test electrical** — measure the capacitor's microfarads against the rating, check the contactor, and confirm voltage at the compressor.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures** — gauges on the service ports show whether the charge is correct and whether the metering device (fixed orifice vs. TXV) is behaving.
5. **Leak search** — if low on charge, they'll use an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before any recharge.
6. **Compressor test** — check windings and amp draw if pressures and electrical point to the compressor.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search — that's a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, unit running | Dirty filter/coil or low refrigerant | Replace filter, clear condenser | Clean coils, leak search + recharge |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan won't spin | Failed capacitor | None (capacitors hold charge) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Filter, thaw with fan-only | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant |
| Nothing runs outside | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Short-cycling | Thermostat, low charge, or overheating | Check thermostat settings | Diagnose control/refrigerant fault |
| No cooling, compressor runs | Compressor failure | None | Test windings, replace compressor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional prices vary):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 DIY
- **Thermostat replacement:** $20–$300 depending on model
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,800, often near the cost of a new unit

If your York system is 12+ years old and facing a compressor or coil failure, ask your tech to compare repair cost against replacement.

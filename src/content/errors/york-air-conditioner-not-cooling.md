## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint homeowners report with a York air conditioner. Your system may be running (the outdoor unit hums, the indoor blower moves air) but the air coming from your vents is warm, lukewarm, or not cold enough to keep up on a hot day.

York units vary widely by model and control board. Higher-end **Affinity** systems with communicating controls may display an actual fault code at the thermostat or outdoor board, while basic **Latitude** and older single-stage units give you no code at all — just poor performance. If your thermostat or outdoor board shows a specific number or blinking-LED pattern, note it; it narrows the diagnosis considerably.

The causes range from trivial (a clogged filter) to serious (a failed compressor or a refrigerant leak). Because refrigerant and compressor work requires licensed, EPA-certified tools and training, this is rated a **pro** repair once the easy checks are ruled out.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode/setting, dead batteries, or a miscalibrated or failing thermostat.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with dirt, grass, or cottonwood, the unit can't reject heat.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cooling fades over weeks or seasons; requires a licensed tech to find and fix the leak.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a common electrical failure that stops the compressor or fan from starting.
7. **Failing compressor or contactor** — the outdoor unit tries to start but hums, clicks, or won't run.
8. **Blocked condensate drain / safety switch trip** — a full drain pan can trip a float switch and shut the system down.
9. **Control board or wiring fault** — varies significantly by York model and vintage.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening a sealed system, touching refrigerant lines, or bypassing a safety switch.

- **Filter:** Pull your air filter. If it's gray or clogged, replace it and give the system time to recover. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the fan to **Auto**, and the setpoint several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Breaker:** Check the breaker panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Return and supply vents:** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked by furniture, rugs, or closed doors.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance on all sides. Do **not** spray a running unit or remove panels.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see standing water near the indoor unit, the drain may be clogged and a float switch may have shut the system off. Clearing the drain line is DIY-safe; the water should flow freely.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system **off** (fan can stay on) and let it thaw fully before restarting. Then replace the filter.

If none of these restores cooling, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the workflow helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Read any codes** — checks the thermostat and outdoor control board for fault codes or LED flash patterns, which vary by York model.
3. **Airflow check** — inspects filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
4. **Electrical test** — measures the run capacitor, contactor, and compressor amp draw with a meter.
5. **Refrigerant test** — connects gauges to read pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak, which triggers a leak search.
6. **Component isolation** — confirms whether the fault is the compressor, fan motor, board, or refrigerant circuit.

A good tech shows you readings, not just a verdict. Be cautious of anyone who quotes a compressor or "recharge" without connecting gauges or measuring the temperature split.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Dirty filter / blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents | Inspect blower and ductwork |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off to thaw, replace filter | Check refrigerant charge, find leak |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — electrical | Test and replace capacitor |
| Cooling fades over weeks | Refrigerant leak | None — EPA regulated | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| System shut off, water present | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear drain line | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker / control fault | Reset breaker once | Diagnose board and wiring |
| Cools but never keeps up | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris outside | Deep-clean coil, verify charge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Actual pricing varies by region and unit.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries:** under $10 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 for a pro clearing.
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,200.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ — often the point where replacing the unit makes more sense on older systems.

If your York unit is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, ask the tech to compare repair cost against a full replacement.

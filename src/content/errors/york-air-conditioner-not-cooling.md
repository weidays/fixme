## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on the display — it's the symptom homeowners report when a York air conditioner runs but the house won't get cold, or the unit won't start at all. York central AC systems and heat pumps rarely show a numeric error for this; instead you'll notice warm air at the vents, a compressor that won't start, ice on the lines, or a system that short-cycles.

Because York builds many model families (Affinity, LX, Latitude and older lines) across decades, the way a control board reports faults varies widely. Newer communicating systems may flash an LED code or show a message at the thermostat; older single-stage units give you no code at all. This page covers the physical causes behind "not cooling" regardless of what — if anything — your board displays.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a failed thermostat that isn't calling for cooling.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler loses power.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cooling fades over weeks; the system runs constantly but never satisfies.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan won't start; you may hear humming.
7. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling collapses on hot days.
8. **Contactor failure** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't close.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — compressor runs but overheats and shuts down.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive cause; the unit hums, trips, or does nothing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — none involve refrigerant, electrical panels beyond a breaker, or safety switches.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the #1 cause of poor cooling and frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the temperature is below room temp. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Make sure the fan is on AUTO.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the AC. Flip it fully OFF then ON once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan can stay ON) and let it thaw fully — this can take several hours. Then replace the filter and restart.
- **Condensate drain:** Check for a full drain pan or a clogged drain line, which can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Clear standing water and consider drain tablets.

If cooling doesn't return after these steps, the problem is likely electrical, refrigerant, or mechanical — call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence, and knowing it helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verifies the call for cooling** at the thermostat and checks control voltage (24V) to the outdoor unit.
2. **Inspects airflow** — filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil condition.
3. **Checks capacitors and contactor** with a meter; a weak capacitor is a common, cheap find.
4. **Measures refrigerant pressures** with gauges and takes superheat/subcooling readings to judge charge and airflow.
5. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low — using electronic detectors, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure testing. A reputable tech won't just top off a leaking system.
6. **Tests the compressor and condenser fan motor** for proper amp draw and startup.
7. **Reads any stored fault codes** on communicating York boards to confirm findings.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without a documented leak search or electrical test.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, house won't cool | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents | Check blower, coil, airflow |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Diagnose refrigerant/airflow |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Runs constantly, cools poorly | Low refrigerant leak | None — call a pro | Leak search and repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air, warm coil | Dirty condenser coil | Gently rinse coil if accessible | Deep clean, check charge |
| System trips breaker repeatedly | Electrical short or compressor fault | Do not keep resetting | Diagnose short/compressor |
| Nothing happens at all | Blown fuse, contactor, or thermostat | Check breaker and thermostat | Test low-voltage circuit |
| Loud hum then shutoff | Failing compressor | None — call a pro | Compressor test/replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and unit size.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $120–$350
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$300
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often makes full replacement worth considering on older units)

If your York unit uses R-22 refrigerant (phased out), leak and recharge costs can be much higher, and replacement may be more economical.

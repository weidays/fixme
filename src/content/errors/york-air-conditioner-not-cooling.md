## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a single stored fault code on York equipment — it's a symptom. Depending on your model, the outdoor unit's control board may flash a diagnostic LED pattern, or the system may show no fault at all while simply blowing warm or room-temperature air.

York uses several different control boards across its **LX, Affinity, and Latitude** product families, and the meaning of any LED flash code varies by board. Because of that, the first job is to figure out *how* it's failing:

- **Nothing runs at all** — no fan, no compressor (power or control issue).
- **Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit doesn't** — often a capacitor, contactor, or safety lockout.
- **Both run but air isn't cold** — usually airflow restriction or a refrigerant problem.

Each of those points to a different set of causes below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked return** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature until it ices over and stops cooling.
2. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — a coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so cooling capacity collapses.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure. The outdoor fan or compressor hums or won't start.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — refrigerant is never consumed, so low charge means a leak that must be found and repaired.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — kills power to the outdoor unit or control board.
6. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, loose wiring, or a bad "call for cooling" signal.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that energizes the outdoor unit sticks or burns out.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — a downstream result of low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil becomes a block of ice.
9. **Failed compressor** — the worst case. Expensive, and often a replace-the-system decision.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here — do not open the electrical compartment or touch refrigerant lines.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets on a locked-out unit are unsafe.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit airflow:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water pooling or a full drain pan, the system may have shut off on a float switch. You can clear the visible drain line and add a drain tablet.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to **OFF** (fan **ON** is okay) and let it fully thaw for a few hours before running again.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the steps helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — confirms warm air and checks the thermostat is calling for cooling.
2. **Read the board** — reads any York LED flash code or fault, noting the model's specific code chart.
3. **Airflow check** — inspects filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
4. **Electrical test** — measures the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, and verifies 24V control voltage and line voltage.
5. **Refrigerant measurement** — connects gauges, reads suction/liquid pressures, and checks superheat/subcooling to determine if charge is correct.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, uses electronic detector or dye to locate the leak. A good tech will *not* just top off and leave.
7. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw and windings if the compressor won't start.

A trustworthy quote names the specific failed part and its measured value — not just "needs Freon."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Clogged filter / dirty coil | Replace filter; clear return vents | Deep-clean coils, verify airflow |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Failed capacitor or contactor | Check breaker once | Test and replace capacitor/contactor |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn to OFF, let thaw | Diagnose cause; clean or recharge |
| Cools poorly, hisses | Refrigerant leak | None — call a pro | Locate leak, repair, recharge |
| Nothing runs at all | Breaker, fuse, or thermostat | Reset breaker once; replace batteries | Test transformer, board, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Weak/failed capacitor | None — capacitors hold charge | Replace capacitor; test compressor |
| Compressor won't start, high amps | Failing compressor | None | Test windings; recommend repair/replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 professional.
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$400.
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A costs more than older systems).
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ — often the point where replacing the whole condenser or system makes more financial sense.

Prices vary by region, unit age, and whether your system is still under York's parts warranty (typically 10 years with registration — labor is usually separate).

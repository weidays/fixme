## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific numeric fault code on most York air conditioners — it's the symptom homeowners describe when the system runs but the house won't drop to the set temperature, or the air from the vents isn't cold. Depending on your York model, you may see no error indication at all (older LX and Latitude units), or a diagnostic LED flash pattern on the control board of newer Affinity variable-speed systems.

Because "not cooling" can be caused by anything from a $5 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you through the likely causes in order, the checks you can safely do yourself, and how a technician will confirm the real problem so you can sanity-check any quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a failed thermostat not calling for cooling.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser has its own breaker; if it trips, the indoor fan runs but no cold air is produced.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common outdoor failure. The compressor and/or fan won't start, so no cooling occurs.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; requires a licensed tech to find and fix.
7. **Dirty condenser coil** — a caked outdoor coil can't reject heat, killing efficiency.
8. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the compressor doesn't close.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor fan not spinning causes high-pressure trips and no cooling.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive and least common; often the diagnosis of last resort.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Everything else involves electrical, refrigerant, or sealed-system work that requires a pro.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first — it fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel. You typically have **two** breakers — one for the indoor air handler/furnace and one for the outdoor condenser. Reset any that are tripped **once**. If a breaker trips again, stop and call a pro — that signals an electrical fault.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs, and that return grilles aren't clogged with dust or blocked.
- **Condensate drain:** If your unit has a float switch and the drain line is clogged, the system may refuse to run. You can clear a visible clog and pour a cup of water/vinegar down an accessible drain line.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** (leave the fan on to help it thaw) and let it fully melt — this can take several hours — before running it again.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, discharge capacitors, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work systematically. Knowing the steps helps you tell a thorough diagnosis from a guess:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check the thermostat call for cooling with a meter.
2. **Confirm 24V control signal** reaching the outdoor contactor.
3. **Check the capacitor** with a multimeter for the correct microfarad (MFD) rating — a bulged or out-of-spec cap is a common, cheap find.
4. **Test the contactor** to confirm it's pulling in and passing voltage.
5. **Measure amp draw** on the compressor and fan motor to spot a locked or failing motor.
6. **Inspect airflow** — filter, coil, and blower — before condemning refrigerant.
7. **Attach gauges and take temperatures** (superheat/subcooling) to determine if the charge is correct. Low readings point to a leak.
8. **Leak search** with electronic detector or dye if refrigerant is low.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add refrigerant" without finding the leak, or who condemns the compressor without measuring amp draw and checking the capacitor first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower & ductwork |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped condenser breaker | Reset breaker once | Trace electrical fault if it re-trips |
| Outdoor unit humms but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test & replace capacitor |
| Ice on refrigerant lines/coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, let thaw, replace filter | Check charge & find leak |
| Cooling weak on hot days only | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Gently rinse outdoor coil | Coil clean, charge check |
| Blank/dim thermostat | Dead batteries | Replace batteries | Replace thermostat if faulty |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed fan motor or capacitor | None — call pro | Test & replace motor/cap |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant or failing compressor | None — call pro | Leak search, compressor test |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Prices vary by region and unit size.

- **Air filter:** $10 – $40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 – $15 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150 – $400
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400 – $700
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150 – $300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500 – $1,500 (depends on leak location)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000 – $2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500 – $2,500+ (often worth comparing to full system replacement on older units)

A basic diagnostic/service call typically runs **$75 – $200**, often credited toward the repair.

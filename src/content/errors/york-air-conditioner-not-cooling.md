## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on your York air conditioner — it's a symptom. Your system may be running, blowing air, and consuming power, but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

Because York central ACs span several generations (LX, Affinity, Latitude and older lines), the way a fault shows up varies. Some newer communicating systems display diagnostic codes at the thermostat or on a control board LED; many standard single- and two-stage units give you **no code at all** and simply fail to cool. When there's no code, diagnosis comes down to checking airflow, electrical components, and the refrigerant circuit in order.

This page walks through the safe checks you can do yourself, then explains how a technician diagnoses the deeper causes so you can sanity-check any quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, freezes the indoor coil, and kills cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty or blocked outdoor condenser coil** — leaves, grass, and dirt insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common electrical failure; the compressor and/or fan hum but won't start, or the outdoor fan spins but the compressor doesn't.
4. **Low refrigerant due to a leak** — reduces cooling and often ices the indoor coil or the copper line at the outdoor unit.
5. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit; pitted or stuck contacts leave the condenser dead.
6. **Frozen indoor evaporator coil** — a downstream result of low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks cooling entirely.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown control fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
8. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a wiring/board fault.
9. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the unit tries to start but the compressor is seized or electrically dead.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the checks any homeowner can safely do:

- **Air filter** — Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Set the fan to AUTO.
- **Breakers** — Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Outdoor unit** — Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (never a pressure washer) with the power off at the disconnect.
- **Supply and return vents** — Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't choking airflow. Open all vents.
- **Condensate** — Check for standing water at the indoor unit or a full drain pan. A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and shut cooling down. Clear a visible clog or add drain tablets.
- **Ice** — If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper line at the outdoor unit, turn the system to **FAN ONLY** for a few hours to thaw it, then run cooling again. If it re-freezes, you need a technician.

**Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass safety switches, or attempt any electrical work inside the units.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified HVAC tech will typically:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply and return air temperatures (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect the filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Test electrical components** — measure the run capacitor's microfarads against its rating, inspect the contactor for pitting, and confirm the compressor and fan motor are getting proper voltage.
4. **Read any board or thermostat codes** — on communicating York systems, they'll pull diagnostic data; on standard units they rely on measurements.
5. **Check refrigerant charge** — connect gauges to read pressures and measure superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak, which they'll locate with an electronic detector or dye.
6. **Assess the compressor** — test windings and amp draw if it won't start.

A trustworthy diagnosis names a specific failed part with a measurement behind it — not just "you're low on refrigerant" with no leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, house won't cool | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower & duct static |
| Outdoor unit dirty, poor cooling | Blocked condenser coil | Rinse coil, clear debris | Deep coil cleaning |
| Humming, fan/compressor won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — leave power off | Test & replace capacitor |
| Ice on coil or copper line | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Fan-only to thaw; check filter | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit completely dead | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor / trace power |
| Indoor water or shutdown | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, test float switch |
| No response at all | Thermostat or wiring fault | Replace batteries, check mode | Test thermostat & control board |
| Loud start attempt, then nothing | Failed compressor | None | Compressor/system evaluation |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor):

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Coil cleaning (condenser or evaporator):** $150–$400
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+, often approaching the cost of a new unit

If your York unit is 12+ years old and facing a compressor or coil failure, ask your technician to compare repair cost against replacement.

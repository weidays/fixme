## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single fault code — it's a symptom. Your York air conditioner may be running, blowing air, or completely idle, but the air coming from your vents isn't cold enough to bring your home to the thermostat setpoint.

York central air systems don't display a universal "not cooling" code the way a furnace flashes a fault sequence. Depending on your model and its control board, the unit may simply run continuously without satisfying the thermostat, short-cycle (turn on and off quickly), trip a breaker, or lock out on an internal protection circuit. Newer York communicating systems (such as some Affinity models with a Hx3 or similar touchscreen thermostat) may show an equipment fault or alert; older units give you no code at all.

Because the causes span everything from a $20 filter to a $2,500 compressor, the goal of this page is to help you rule out the easy stuff and understand what a fair professional diagnosis looks like.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the indoor coil, reducing cooling and eventually freezing the coil.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass, leaves, and dirt block heat rejection so the system can't remove heat from your home.
3. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a wiring/setpoint issue.
4. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks cooling entirely.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan motor hums or won't start.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cooling gets progressively weaker; requires leak repair, not just a "top off."
7. **Contactor or relay failure** — the outdoor unit doesn't energize.
8. **Condensate safety switch tripped** — a full drain pan shuts the system off to prevent water damage.
9. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit is completely dead.
10. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive outcome, usually the last thing confirmed.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner. Stop at anything that requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or electrical panels beyond the breaker.

- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak cooling and frozen coils.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check your breakers.** Look for a tripped breaker for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Look at the vents.** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Check the outdoor unit.** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around and on top of the condenser. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (never a pressure washer) with the power off.
- **Look for ice.** If you see ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** and set the fan to **On** to thaw it (this can take a few hours). Persistent freezing means low airflow or low refrigerant — call a pro.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A full drain pan or clogged drain line can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Clearing a visible clog is fine; anything beyond that is a service call.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified HVAC tech should work methodically, not just guess. Here's what a proper diagnosis looks like so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the complaint** — measure the temperature split between return and supply air (a healthy split is typically 15–20°F).
2. **Verify airflow** — inspect the filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Check electrical** — test the capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and confirm voltage at the condenser.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures** — connect gauges to measure suction and liquid pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. This tells them if the charge is correct.
5. **Leak search if charge is low** — with dye, an electronic detector, or nitrogen pressure test. A reputable tech won't just add refrigerant without finding the leak.
6. **Test the compressor and fan motor** — for correct amp draw and operation.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without measured readings. Ask to see the numbers.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm-ish air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower, ductwork |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker, contactor, capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor, contactor |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed capacitor or motor | None | Replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Cooling fades over days/weeks | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search and repair, recharge |
| Runs constantly, never satisfies | Dirty condenser, low charge, undersized | Clean condenser fins | Measure pressures, check charge |
| System off, water near indoor unit | Condensate float switch tripped | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, replace switch |
| Short-cycling on/off | Capacitor, control, or refrigerant issue | Check filter | Full electrical and charge diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor). Your area, model, and unit age will move these.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$450
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Condensate float switch:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher if the leak is in the coil)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often the point where replacing the whole unit is worth pricing)

If your York system is more than 12–15 years old and uses R-22 refrigerant, a major repair like a compressor or coil is a good time to get a full-system replacement quote for comparison.

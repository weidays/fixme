## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code on most York systems. Your York air conditioner may power on, the thermostat may call for cooling, but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or barely cool.

Because standard York condensers (LX, Latitude, and many Affinity units) don't have an alphanumeric display, the "code" you're chasing is really a set of behaviors: the outdoor unit hums but the fan won't spin, the compressor short-cycles, or the whole system runs continuously without dropping the indoor temperature.

Communicating Affinity systems with a compatible thermostat **can** display fault codes for specific failures (pressure, sensor, or communication faults). If your setup shows a numbered code, note it — the diagnosis below still applies, but the code narrows the cause.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or restricted airflow** — the most common and most overlooked cause. A clogged filter starves the indoor coil, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil into a block of ice.
2. **Dirty or blocked condenser coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust on the outdoor coil prevent heat from escaping, so the system runs but won't cool.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — a weak or bulged capacitor stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting. You'll often hear a hum or click without the fan spinning.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — refrigerant doesn't get "used up." If it's low, there's a leak. Symptoms include ice on the copper line, warm air, and long run times.
5. **Bad contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit can pit or stick, leaving the condenser dead or chattering.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil ices over and blocks cooling entirely.
7. **Thermostat or control issue** — dead batteries, wrong settings, or a communication fault on Affinity systems.
8. **Failed compressor** — the least common but most expensive. The unit may trip the breaker, hum and shut off, or run without producing cold air.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner:

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal an electrical fault.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser and gently hose the coil from the outside with the power off. Keep at least 18–24 inches of clearance.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a safety float switch that may have shut the system down. Clear a clogged drain line if you can access it.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the outdoor copper lines or the indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully — several hours — before restarting.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech should work systematically — this list helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat operation and settings.
2. **Measure temperature split** across the indoor coil (typically 16–22°F difference between return and supply air).
3. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower, and coil condition.
4. **Check electrical components** — capacitor microfarad reading, contactor condition, and voltage/amp draw on the compressor and fan.
5. **Attach gauges** to read system pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling — this reveals a refrigerant charge problem or leak.
6. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low, using electronic detector or dye.
7. **Read fault codes** on communicating Affinity boards if applicable.

A good tech tells you the **root cause**, not just "add refrigerant." If they top off refrigerant without finding a leak, ask why.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Dirty filter / airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Check blower, coil, static pressure |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper line or coil | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Condenser totally dead | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Rinse outdoor coil | Verify charge, clean coil, leak check |
| Unit clicks/hums then shuts off | Failing compressor or capacitor | None — call a pro | Test compressor, capacitor, hard-start |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries / float switch | Replace batteries, check drain | Diagnose control wiring/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$50 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Hard-start kit:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far pricier)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$1,800+ (often worth weighing against full system replacement on older units)

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type, and unit age. Get a written diagnosis before authorizing major work.

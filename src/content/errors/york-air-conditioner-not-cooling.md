## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom York homeowners report most often. Your York air conditioner may be running (the outdoor fan spins, air moves from vents) but the air isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

Because York builds several product lines — from basic single-stage **LX** condensers to two-stage **Affinity** and variable-speed **Acclimate** systems — how the system reports and reacts to a cooling problem differs. Communicating York systems with an **S1 / Hx** control board may log fault codes at the board or thermostat, while older single-stage units give you no code at all. If your model has a diagnostic display, note any codes shown; they narrow the diagnosis considerably.

This is rated a **pro-level** problem because the two most damaging root causes — low refrigerant and compressor faults — cannot be legally or safely handled by a homeowner in the US.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from a dirty filter, blocked return, or low refrigerant. Ice blocks airflow, so vents blow little or warm air.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection, so the system can't dump heat.
4. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "on" vs "auto"), or a wiring/setpoint issue.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler has lost power.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common electrical failure; the compressor or fan hums but won't start.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling and can freeze the coil. Requires a pro to find and repair.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the outdoor unit runs but produces no cold refrigerant.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and fan is on AUTO. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker** — check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler or condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain** — if the drain pan is full or overflowing, a clogged line may have tripped a safety float switch that shuts cooling off. You can inspect and clear the visible drain outlet.
- **Ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines** — if you see frost, turn the system to OFF but leave the fan ON to thaw it, then run cooling again after a few hours with a clean filter. If it re-freezes, call a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass the float switch, or repeatedly reset a breaker that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work through this sequence — use it to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat call, then confirm power at the disconnect and low-voltage signal at the contactor.
2. **Measure temperature split** across the indoor coil (typically ~15–22°F drop between return and supply air).
3. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition.
4. **Test electrical components** — capacitor microfarads, contactor, and compressor windings with a meter.
5. **Attach gauges and measure refrigerant** — subcooling and superheat readings reveal a low charge, restriction, or overcharge.
6. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low — with electronic detector or dye, because simply adding refrigerant without repair is a temporary and illegal fix under EPA rules.
7. **Check compressor amperage and start behavior** if the outdoor unit runs but produces no cooling.

A thorough diagnostic usually runs **$90–$200** and should be applied toward the repair if you proceed.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with fan on | Clean coil, find airflow restriction |
| Ice on indoor coil / lines | Low refrigerant or airflow loss | Thaw and swap filter | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, trace fault |
| Fan hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor coil packed with debris | Dirty condenser | Gently rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean, straighten fins |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low charge or compressor fault | Verify filter/thermostat | Gauge readings, compressor test |
| No power to thermostat | Dead batteries / blown fuse | Replace batteries | Check control board, transformer, wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor), varying by region and York model:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condensate line clearing / float switch:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. legacy R-22 is far pricier)
- **Blower motor:** $450–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,200+ — often the point at which replacing the whole condenser is worth pricing

Two-stage Affinity and variable-speed Acclimate systems can carry higher parts and diagnostic costs because of their communicating boards and specialized components.

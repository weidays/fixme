## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single fault code — it's a symptom. Your York air conditioner may run, blow air, and show no error at all while the house keeps getting warmer. On some York systems with a communicating thermostat or control board, you may see a fault indication (like a blinking LED or a thermostat message), but many older or single-stage York units give no code and simply fail to cool.

Because York builds several platforms (LX, Affinity, Latitude and heat-pump variants), the exact diagnostics vary by model and board. This page focuses on the most common real-world causes when a York AC runs but won't lower the temperature. Since diagnosing a no-cool condition often involves electrical testing and refrigerant work, this is rated **pro** — most root causes need a licensed technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked airflow** — the single most common cause; restricts airflow enough to ice the coil or cripple cooling.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — leaves, grass and dirt block heat rejection, so the system can't dump heat.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" not "cool"), or wiring/setting issues.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — you'll feel weak or no cold air; requires a licensed tech and leak repair, never just a "top-off."
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start; a common, affordable repair.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low charge; the coil ices over and cooling stops.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown contactor** — outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor fan still runs.
8. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive cause; the outdoor unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should attempt. If none fix it, call a pro.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 no-cool cause.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered and the display is dim or blank.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker feeding the AC. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings and debris from around and on top of the condenser. Leave at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** Check for standing water in the drain pan. A clogged drain can trigger a safety float switch that shuts off cooling. You can clear a visible clog and add drain tablets, but leave wiring alone.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or open the electrical compartments.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work through a logical sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply and return air temperatures. A healthy split (delta-T) is roughly 16–22°F; a small split points to a charge or airflow problem.
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and both coils for ice or dirt.
3. **Test electrical** — measure the capacitor's microfarad rating, check the contactor, and confirm voltage at the compressor and fan.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — read pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to confirm proper charge. Low charge means a leak search, not a top-off.
5. **Leak detection** — if charge is low, they should locate the leak with electronic detectors, dye, or a nitrogen pressure test.
6. **Compressor test** — measure amp draw and windings if all else checks out.

Be cautious of any tech who adds refrigerant without finding the leak first, or recommends a full system replacement before basic electrical and airflow checks.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace the filter | Inspect blower, ductwork |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, test circuit |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on refrigerant lines or coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, let it thaw, change filter | Find airflow/charge fault |
| Air barely cool, long run times | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Dirty, matted outdoor coil | Blocked condenser | Rinse gently, clear debris | Deep coil cleaning |
| No cooling, standing water inside | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, verify switch |
| Outdoor unit runs, zero cooling | Failed compressor | None — call pro | Compressor test/replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Regional rates and model differences apply.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 professional
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

If your York system is more than 12–15 years old and uses R-22 refrigerant, weigh major repairs against replacement — R-22 is phased out and expensive.

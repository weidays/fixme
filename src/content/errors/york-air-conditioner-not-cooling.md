## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code on your York air conditioner — it is a symptom. Your system may be running (the outdoor unit hums, the indoor blower moves air) yet the air coming from your vents is not cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

York units, depending on model and control board, may or may not display an actual diagnostic code for this. Older Latitude and LX single-stage units typically have no display and rely on the outdoor board's LED flash patterns (if equipped). Newer Affinity communicating systems can log fault codes at the thermostat or control board. Because behavior varies widely by model and board generation, always check your unit's specific service label or manual for LED/fault-code meaning rather than assuming.

The underlying issue is almost always one of three categories: **restricted airflow**, **an electrical/component failure**, or **a refrigerant problem**. This page helps you sort out which one you're facing.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — a coil caked in grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so the system runs but barely cools.
3. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks airflow and cooling stops.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure that can stop the compressor and/or condenser fan.
5. **Low refrigerant due to a leak** — cooling weakens gradually; requires a pro to find, repair, and recharge.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse to the outdoor unit** — the indoor blower runs but the outdoor unit is dead.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/setting issue.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit fails to close.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the outdoor unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If none resolve the issue, call a licensed HVAC technician.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the temperature is set below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it uses them.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and inspect. If it's gray with dust or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" complaints.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop — do not keep resetting it. Call a pro.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris (leaves, grass clippings, cottonwood) matted against the condenser coil. You can gently rinse the outside with a garden hose on low pressure after turning off power at the disconnect.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and shut the system down. Check for standing water in the drain pan.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully before calling — this helps the technician diagnose.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass safety switches, or open the outdoor electrical compartment.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work through the system methodically. Knowing this helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and evaporator coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Test electrical components** — measure the capacitor's microfarad rating, check the contactor, and confirm proper voltage to the compressor and fan.
4. **Read control board diagnostics** — on Affinity communicating units, pull fault history at the board or thermostat. On simpler units, interpret LED flash codes if present.
5. **Take refrigerant readings** — connect gauges to measure pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to confirm proper charge or diagnose a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, use electronic detectors or dye to locate the leak before recharging.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or "replace the compressor" without ruling out the capacitor and contactor first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Inspect blower, coil, ductwork |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, check wiring |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Check charge, find leak, verify airflow |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or fan motor | None — power is present | Test/replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Cooling weakened over weeks | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search, repair, EPA-certified recharge |
| Outdoor coil packed with debris | Dirty condenser | Rinse gently with hose (power off) | Deep coil cleaning, fin repair |
| Unit hums but won't start | Bad capacitor or seized compressor | None | Diagnose capacitor vs. compressor |
| Thermostat blank/unresponsive | Dead batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Check thermostat wiring and control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual price depends on your region, unit age, and labor rates.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$250
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $650–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$1,800+ (often worth comparing against full unit replacement on older systems)

If your York AC is 12–15+ years old and facing a compressor or coil failure, ask your technician for a repair-vs-replace comparison — the economics often favor replacement at that age.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific York fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your air conditioner runs (or tries to) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On York units with a diagnostic control board (common on Affinity and newer LX systems), the board may flash an LED fault pattern that points to a specific cause. Older Diamond-series and basic single-stage units often have no on-board diagnostics at all.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, the goal below is to help you rule out the cheap, safe stuff before you pay for a service call — and to help you sanity-check a technician's quote.

**Behavior varies by model.** Check your outdoor unit's control board for a blinking LED and match it to the label inside the service panel or your owner's manual. If there's no board, diagnosis is entirely by measurement.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with dirt, grass, or cottonwood, the unit can't reject heat.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the fan or compressor hums but won't start; very common failure.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, longer run times, possible icing. Requires a licensed tech.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
7. **Contactor failure** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
8. **Failed compressor** — the unit runs but produces no cooling; the most expensive outcome.
9. **Thermostat or control-wiring fault** — no call for cooling reaching the equipment.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers in your panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** Check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch (common on attic units). A clogged drain can shut the system off. You can pour condensate tablets down an accessible drain line, but don't disassemble plumbing.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or ice on the refrigerant lines. Turn the system off at the thermostat and let ice fully melt before running again.

Do **not** open the electrical service panel on the outdoor unit, touch the capacitor, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. Those are pro-only tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work methodically — this list helps you tell a thorough diagnosis from a guess:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirm 24V reaching the contactor.
2. **Check incoming power** at the disconnect and the contactor.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter (microfarads must be within spec).
4. **Measure amp draw** on the compressor and condenser fan motor.
5. **Read the control board's LED fault code** if the model has one.
6. **Attach gauges** to read suction and head pressures, then calculate superheat/subcooling to judge the refrigerant charge and find restrictions.
7. **Inspect coils** (indoor and outdoor) for dirt and ice.
8. **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add refrigerant" without leak-testing — a low charge nearly always means a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, filter looks gray/black | Clogged filter | Replace filter, restart | — |
| Ice on indoor coil or line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, let ice melt, replace filter | Find airflow/refrigerant fault |
| Outdoor unit humming, not spinning | Failed capacitor | Turn off system | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor coil caked in dirt | Dirty condenser | Gently rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil cleaning |
| Runs constantly, weak cooling | Low refrigerant / leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Breaker, fuse, or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace fuse/contactor, find fault |
| Runs but zero cooling, coils clean | Failed compressor | None — call pro | Test and replace compressor |
| No cooling call at all | Thermostat/wiring | Check settings & batteries | Trace 24V control circuit |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and unit.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$450
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Low-voltage fuse:** $75–$150
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 (more if the coil is leaking)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against a new unit)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400

If your York unit is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, get a quote on a full system replacement before repairing — the repair can approach half the cost of a new unit.

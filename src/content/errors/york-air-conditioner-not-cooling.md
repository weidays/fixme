## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code — most York air conditioners don't display a numeric error for this. Instead, you'll notice the thermostat calling for cool while the air stays warm, the temperature never reaches setpoint, or the outdoor unit isn't running.

York air conditioners cool by moving heat from inside your home to the outdoors using refrigerant, a compressor, and two coils (indoor evaporator, outdoor condenser). When any link in that chain fails — airflow, electrical, or refrigerant — the system runs but delivers little or no cooling. Because several causes are electrical or refrigerant-related, this is rated a **pro** problem: a few checks are safe, but most repairs need a licensed technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the most common and cheapest cause; restricts airflow and can freeze the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer and the unit blows warm.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power, so the compressor and fan don't run.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — very common; the compressor or condenser fan hums or won't start.
5. **Dirty or blocked condenser coil** — outdoor coil caked with dirt/grass can't reject heat.
6. **Bad contactor** — worn or pitted contacts stop power from reaching the compressor.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; requires leak repair and recharge (pro only).
8. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive failure; unit runs but produces no cooling.
9. **Thermostat or control board fault** — no cooling call is sent, or wiring/board issues vary by model.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do:

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If it's gray or clogged, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" complaints.
- **Thermostat:** Set to COOL, temperature several degrees below room temp. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the condenser so it can breathe. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** If the unit shut off and the drain pan is full, a clogged drain or float switch may have stopped it. You can clear a visible clog, but don't disable the float switch.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw for a few hours before calling.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed HVAC tech will typically:

1. **Confirm the complaint** — verify thermostat call, indoor and outdoor operation, and measure supply/return temperature split (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check electrical** — test the capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and verify voltage to the compressor and fan motor.
3. **Inspect airflow** — check the filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil for dirt or ice.
4. **Measure refrigerant** — connect gauges to read pressures and check superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, they'll use electronic detectors or dye to find the leak before recharging.
6. **Test the compressor** — check windings and amp draw if it won't start or runs without cooling.

A trustworthy quote will name the specific failed part and a test result — "capacitor reads 22 µF on a 45/5 rating" — not just "it needs refrigerant" with no leak found.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but stays warm | Low refrigerant or bad compressor | Check filter; verify thermostat set to COOL | Measure pressures, find leak, recharge or replace compressor |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker, bad capacitor or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Ice on coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter, set fan ON to thaw | Diagnose airflow or refrigerant cause |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None — call a pro | Replace capacitor, verify compressor |
| Unit short-cycles or trips breaker | Electrical fault or failing compressor | Stop resetting; call a pro | Diagnose overload, wiring, or compressor |
| Never reaches setpoint on hot days | Dirty condenser coil or undersized charge | Clean debris around outdoor unit | Clean coil, check charge |
| Water/overflow, unit shut off | Clogged condensate drain, float switch tripped | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts and labor, varies by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0–$150
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often near the cost of a new unit)

If your system uses R-22 refrigerant (older units), recharge costs are much higher due to phase-out — replacing the system may be more economical.

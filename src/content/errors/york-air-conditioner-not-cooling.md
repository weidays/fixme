## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code on York air conditioners — it's a symptom. Depending on your model, the outdoor unit may run normally, short-cycle, hum without starting, or sit dead while the indoor blower still moves warm air.

York equipment varies widely in how it reports problems. Basic **Latitude** and older split systems have no diagnostic display at all — you diagnose by behavior. Higher-end **Affinity** communicating systems can show error codes on the thermostat or control board LED. If your thermostat displays a specific code, look that up separately; this page covers the general "AC runs but the house won't cool" situation.

The core issue is almost always one of three things: **not enough refrigerant, not enough airflow, or an electrical component that won't start the cooling cycle.**

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat set wrong** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," wrong temperature, or dead batteries on a battery-powered stat.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power, indoor blower still runs warm air.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — very common. The compressor or fan motor hums but won't start, or the outdoor fan won't spin.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling drops off, especially on hot days.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — gradual loss of cooling, ice on the copper lines, longer run times.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer completely.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit sticks or burns, so the compressor never engages.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause. Unit hums, trips the breaker, or runs but produces no cooling.
10. **Clogged condensate drain triggering a safety float switch** — some systems shut down cooling when the drain backs up.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here and call a pro for anything else.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the single most common fix.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the target temperature is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. York systems usually have two: one for the indoor air handler/furnace and one for the outdoor condenser. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — that's a fault, not a nuisance trip.
- **Return and supply vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't starving the system of airflow.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for leaves, grass clippings, or debris packed against the coil fins. Gently clear the area and pull weeds back at least two feet. Do **not** spray high-pressure water into the fins.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see a full drain pan or a float switch near the indoor unit, a clogged drain may have shut cooling off. You can clear a visible pan of standing water, but don't disassemble drain lines.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, **turn the system off** and set the fan to "on" to thaw it. Running an iced unit damages the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — confirms the unit runs but doesn't cool, and checks thermostat calls.
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Measure electrical** — tests the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, and confirms voltage to the compressor and fan motor.
4. **Gauge the refrigerant** — connects manifold gauges and takes superheat/subcooling readings to determine if the charge is correct.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to locate the leak. A reputable tech won't just "top off" without finding the leak.
6. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw and windings if the compressor won't start or trips the breaker.
7. **Report** — should tell you the specific failed component and a repair-vs-replace recommendation, especially on older R-22 systems.

Be cautious of any quote that recommends adding refrigerant without a leak search, or jumps straight to a full system replacement without diagnostics.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air from vents, blower runs | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace filter; check thermostat | Gauge refrigerant, find leak, recharge |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None (capacitors hold charge) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit completely dead | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw with fan on | Fix airflow or refrigerant charge |
| AC short-cycles on/off | Low charge, bad capacitor, dirty coil | Clean/clear condenser area | Diagnose electrical and charge |
| Cooling weak on hot days only | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris around unit | Deep-clean coil, verify charge |
| Blower off, water near indoor unit | Clogged drain, float switch tripped | Clear visible standing water | Clear drain line, add tablets |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Failed compressor or short | Stop resetting; call pro | Test compressor, amp draw, windings |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges. Prices vary by region and by whether your unit uses phased-out R-22 refrigerant.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ (higher for R-22 systems)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,800; often not worth it on an older unit
- **Full system replacement:** $5,000–$12,000+ (considered when the compressor fails on a 12+ year old unit)

If your unit is 10+ years old and uses R-22, weigh a major repair against replacement — R-22 is expensive and largely phased out.

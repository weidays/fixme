## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific flashing fault code — it's the symptom most York air-conditioner owners describe when the system runs but the house never gets comfortable, or the outdoor unit won't start at all. Because York central AC units generally don't display alphanumeric error codes at the equipment the way furnaces or heat pumps with communicating controls do, diagnosis depends on **what the system is physically doing**: is the outdoor fan spinning, is the compressor humming, is the indoor blower moving air, is the coil frozen?

This page walks you through the likely causes in order, the checks that are genuinely safe for a homeowner, and how a technician should approach the diagnosis — so you can tell a fair quote from an inflated one. Behavior varies by model and age (older R-22 Diamond/Sunline units vs newer R-410A LX and Affinity systems), so where it matters, we'll say so.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, loose wiring, or a failed thermostat not calling for cooling.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow (dirty filter/coil) or low refrigerant. The unit runs but blows warm or weak air.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common electrical failure. The compressor or outdoor fan hums but won't start.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — long cooling cycles, warm air, ice on the copper lines. Requires a licensed tech; refrigerant is never "topped off" without finding the leak.
6. **Dirty or blocked condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling capacity drops on hot days.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the compressor is pitted or stuck, so the outdoor unit won't engage.
9. **Failed compressor** — the worst case. The unit trips breakers, hums and shuts off, or does nothing. Repair or replace decision.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here — do not open the sealed refrigerant system or the electrical compartment of the outdoor unit.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model. Make sure the fan is on **AUTO**, not off.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers in your panel. Flip a tripped breaker fully OFF, then ON — **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro; repeated resets can damage the compressor.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Closing too many rooms off restricts airflow.
- **Condensate drain:** If your indoor unit has a float safety switch and the drain is clogged, the system may shut off cooling. Check for water in or around the drain pan and clear a visible clog if you can reach it. Drain tablets help prevent recurrence.
- **Frozen coil / ice on lines:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper lines outside, turn the system to **OFF** (fan can stay ON to help it thaw), let it fully melt for a few hours, then replace the filter before restarting. If it freezes again, you have an airflow or refrigerant problem for a tech.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent York technician should:

1. **Verify the thermostat call** and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor unit's contactor.
2. **Measure the capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads (µF printed on the label) — a weak capacitor is a cheap, common fix.
3. **Check the contactor** for pitted contacts and confirm it pulls in when the thermostat calls.
4. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower, and both coils — and confirm the evaporator isn't frozen or fouled.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and take superheat/subcooling readings to judge the charge. Correct diagnosis requires readings, not guesswork.
6. **Leak-search** if the charge is low — with electronic detector or dye — because adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a temporary patch and, for R-410A/R-22, wasteful and non-compliant.
7. **Test the compressor** windings and amp draw if the unit hums, trips, or won't start.

If a tech quotes a compressor or "recharge" without taking pressure readings or leak-searching, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, house won't cool | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with system off | Check coil, airflow, refrigerant charge |
| Indoor blows but outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker, capacitor, contactor | Reset breaker once | Test capacitor, contactor, 24V signal |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — pro repair | Replace capacitor, verify µF rating |
| Ice on copper lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw, replace filter | Leak-search, measure charge |
| Long cycles, warm air on hot days | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Gently rinse outdoor coil exterior | Clean coil, check subcooling |
| Breaker trips repeatedly on cooling call | Failed compressor or short | Stop resetting; shut off | Test compressor windings/amps |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Check wiring, transformer, board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Your area and unit age affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY).
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed. Common and quick.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500, higher for older **R-22** systems where refrigerant is expensive and increasingly scarce.
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800–$2,500+ — often the point where replacing the whole condenser or system makes more sense, especially on units over 10–12 years old.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180, often credited toward the repair.

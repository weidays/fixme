## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you're most likely searching when your York air conditioner is running but the house stays warm. Unlike a furnace flash code, most residential York AC units don't display an error for poor cooling; you diagnose it by working through the airflow and refrigeration system.

Cooling depends on three things working together: enough airflow across the indoor coil, a sealed refrigerant charge, and a compressor/outdoor fan that actually run. If any one fails, the air coming from your vents will feel weak, warm, or the system will run non-stop without lowering the temperature.

Some newer York communicating systems (Affinity with the S1 control) *will* log fault codes for pressure, sensor or compressor issues — check the indoor control board or thermostat for stored codes if your model supports it.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a bad setting so the system never calls for cooling correctly.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, dust and debris block heat rejection so the unit runs but can't cool.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; often causes ice on the lines. Requires a pro (never a DIY "top-off").
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or outdoor fan hums but won't start.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — result of low airflow or low charge; blocks cooling entirely.
8. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive failure; unit runs the fan but produces no cold air.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do not open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 cause of weak cooling and frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler and the outdoor unit. Reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs or closed registers aren't blocking airflow.
- **Outdoor unit:** Visually confirm the fan on top is spinning and that grass, leaves or a hedge aren't smothering the coil. Keep 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch — a clogged condensate line can shut the system off.
- **Ice check:** If you see frost or ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system OFF (fan can stay ON) and let it thaw fully before running again.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech should work methodically — this is how you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and coil for restrictions or ice.
2. **Check the call for cooling** — confirm 24V is reaching the contactor and control board.
3. **Test electrical components** — measure the capacitor's microfarad rating and inspect the contactor for pitting.
4. **Gauge the refrigerant** — connect manifold gauges to read suction and head pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. This tells them if the charge is correct or if there's a leak.
5. **Leak search** — if the charge is low, they should find the leak (electronic detector or dye), not just add refrigerant.
6. **Compressor test** — measure amperage draw and windings if the compressor won't start or run.

Be wary of any tech who just "adds a pound of Freon" without finding the leak — that's a temporary fix on a system that will lose charge again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower & ductwork |
| System off, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / tripped breaker | Replace batteries, reset breaker once | Trace 24V control fault |
| Runs constantly, house won't cool | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Clean visible debris, clear coil area | Gauge charge, clean coil, find leak |
| Ice on refrigerant lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Diagnose charge & airflow |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — call a pro | Test & replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Bad contactor or capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor/capacitor, replace |
| Fan runs, zero cold air | Failed compressor | None — call a pro | Test compressor, quote replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250 (service call)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $300–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing to a full system replacement on older units)

If your York unit is out of warranty and over 12–15 years old with a failed compressor, ask for a replacement quote alongside the repair — the math often favors a new system.

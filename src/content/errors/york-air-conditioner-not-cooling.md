## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific York fault code. It means the air conditioner is running (or trying to run) but the air coming from your vents is not cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On York units with a diagnostic board, you may or may not see a stored fault — many cooling problems are mechanical or refrigerant-related and do not trigger a flashing code at all.

Because this can range from a $10 filter to a $2,500 compressor, the goal below is to help you rule out the cheap, safe causes yourself and understand what a technician should be checking before you approve any expensive repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, reduces cooling, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "heat" or "fan"), or a miswired/failed thermostat.
3. **Tripped breaker or disconnect** — outdoor condenser has no power while the indoor fan still runs.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a common failure; the fan or compressor hums but won't start.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling drops off in hot weather.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — weak cooling, ice on the copper line, longer run times. Requires a certified tech.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks all cooling until thawed.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the compressor doesn't engage.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most serious and expensive cause; often the last thing diagnosed.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or bypassing a safety device.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak cooling and coil freeze-ups.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again immediately, stop and call a pro — repeated resets can be dangerous.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The box near the condenser should be fully seated/on.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged condensate line can trip a float switch and shut cooling down. Check for standing water at the indoor unit.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the outdoor copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system **OFF** (fan can stay on) and let it thaw completely — several hours — before running it again.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is typically 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition.
3. **Confirm power** — voltage at the disconnect, contactor, and capacitor readings with a meter.
4. **Test the capacitor** — a quick microfarad reading; out-of-spec caps get replaced.
5. **Inspect the contactor** — for pitting or failure to pull in.
6. **Gauge the refrigerant** — connect manifold gauges, read pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low charge means a **leak search**, not just a top-off.
7. **Evaluate the compressor** — amp draw, winding resistance, and whether it starts under load.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add a pound of Freon" without looking for a leak — that's a temporary and non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm air, low airflow | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with system off | Verify airflow, inspect blower and coil |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or disconnect off | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test contactor, wiring, and outdoor power |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan/compressor won't start | Failed capacitor | None (leave the panel closed) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines | Low refrigerant or airflow restriction | Change filter, thaw unit | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Cools poorly on hot days only | Dirty condenser coil | Gently rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean, check charge |
| No cooling, thermostat blank | Dead batteries or thermostat fault | Replace batteries | Test thermostat and control wiring |
| Long run times, high bills | Low charge or failing compressor | Note symptoms for the tech | Measure pressures, amp-test compressor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,800
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

If your York unit is 12+ years old and needs a compressor or coil, ask your contractor for a repair-vs-replace comparison before spending big.

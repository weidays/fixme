## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom rather than a specific York fault code. It describes an air conditioner that is powered on and running (or trying to run) but failing to lower the indoor temperature. On many York systems there is no error-code display at all on the outdoor unit — the diagnosis comes from what the equipment is *doing*: the fan spins but no cold air, ice on the lines, the outdoor unit hums but won't start, or the system short cycles.

Some York communicating systems with a compatible thermostat (Affinity/Hx series) will report fault or comfort alerts on the wall control. Behavior varies significantly by model and by the installed thermostat, so treat any on-screen message as brand-specific and note the exact wording when you call.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, this page is rated **pro** — the safe checks are DIY, but most true causes require a licensed HVAC technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks heat exchange until it thaws.
3. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — dust, grass, and cottonwood insulate the coil so heat can't escape.
4. **Failed dual run capacitor** — a very common failure; the fan or compressor hums but won't start.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, ice, and hissing; requires leak repair, not just a top-off.
6. **Thermostat problem or wrong setting** — dead batteries, set to "fan on" instead of "cool," or a wiring/config fault.
7. **Contactor failure** — the outdoor unit gets no power to start because the contactor won't pull in.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — no power to the condenser or control board.
9. **Failed condenser fan or blower motor** — airflow loss on one side of the system.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the unit runs but produces little or no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. If a step doesn't restore cooling, stop and call a pro.

- **Filter:** Pull the return-air filter. If it's gray and clogged, replace it and let the system run 30–60 minutes.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace batteries if it's battery powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel and the outdoor disconnect for a tripped breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for a coil buried in grass, leaves, or cottonwood. Gently clear debris from around the cabinet (power off).
- **Condensate:** Check that the drain line isn't clogged and the pan isn't full — a full pan can trip a safety float switch and stop cooling.
- **Ice check:** If you see frost or ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, **turn the system OFF at the thermostat, set the fan to ON**, and let it thaw fully (several hours) before running cooling again.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good York technician follows a logical sequence you can sanity-check against the quote:

1. **Confirm the symptom** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or restriction.
3. **Inspects the condenser** — cleans coil if needed, checks the fan motor.
4. **Tests electrical components** — measures the capacitor's microfarads against its rating, tests the contactor, and checks voltage to the compressor.
5. **Gauges the refrigerant** — connects manifold gauges to read pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low charge points to a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before recharging.
7. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw and windings if everything upstream is good.

Ask for the measured temperature split, the capacitor reading, and refrigerant pressures. A quote that jumps straight to "recharge" or "new compressor" without these numbers deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter, run 1 hr | Inspect blower/duct if unresolved |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, fan ON, thaw | Find leak, recharge, fix airflow |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (high voltage) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, no fan | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Warm air, hissing sound | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak repair + EPA-certified recharge |
| Dirty, matted outdoor coil | Debris insulation | Clear surrounding debris | Deep coil clean, fin combing |
| Blank thermostat | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries | Check low-voltage fuse & C-wire |
| Full drain pan, unit stops | Clogged condensate drain | Clear line, add tablets | Clear blockage, test float switch |
| Runs constantly, poor cooling | Failing compressor | None | Amp/winding test, compressor replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor unless noted:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries:** under $10 (DIY).
- **Dual run capacitor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Condenser or blower fan motor:** $300–$700 installed.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far pricier).
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+; if the unit is old and out of warranty, a full system replacement is often the better value.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200, often credited toward the repair.

Check your York warranty — many systems carry a 10-year parts warranty (registration usually required), which can significantly cut compressor and coil part costs, though you still pay labor.

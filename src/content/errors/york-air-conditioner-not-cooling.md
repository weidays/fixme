## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on most York air conditioners — it's a symptom. York central AC systems generally don't display alphanumeric error codes on the equipment itself the way a furnace does. Instead, the diagnosis lives in the behavior: the thermostat calls for cooling, but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or the house never reaches the setpoint.

What actually happens inside a York AC when it's working: the thermostat closes a circuit, the outdoor condenser (compressor and fan) starts, and the indoor blower moves warm house air across a cold evaporator coil. If any link in that chain breaks — power, airflow, refrigerant, or a control component — you get "not cooling."

Because this covers many possible failures, some are simple homeowner fixes and some require a licensed HVAC technician with gauges and EPA certification. This page is marked **pro** because the most likely non-trivial causes (refrigerant, compressor, capacitor, contactor) are not safe or legal DIY repairs.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the coil. Most common and cheapest.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to Heat or Fan instead of Cool), or setpoint above room temp.
3. **Tripped breaker or disconnect to the outdoor unit** — the indoor blower runs (warm air blows) but the condenser is dead.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer and airflow.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common outdoor-unit failure; the compressor or fan hums but won't start.
6. **Failed or pitted contactor** — the relay that powers the condenser doesn't close, so the outdoor unit stays off.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, ice, or a hard-working system that never satisfies. Requires a pro.
8. **Condensate safety switch tripped** — a clogged drain fills the pan and a float switch shuts down cooling (common on units with this option).
9. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil can't reject heat, so cooling fades on hot days.
10. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — least common but most expensive; the heart of the system.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner. Do these first — they resolve a large share of "not cooling" calls.

- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause and can freeze your coil.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. If it's a battery model, install fresh AA batteries.
- **Check the breaker.** Look in your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (handle sitting between on and off). Reset it once. There's usually a separate breaker for the outdoor unit. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the outdoor disconnect.** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated. Don't open the unit or touch internal wiring.
- **Look at the vents and returns.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed doors.
- **Check for ice.** If the indoor coil, copper lines, or outdoor unit are iced up, switch the thermostat to **Off** or **Fan Only** and let it thaw completely (a few hours) before running cooling again.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A wet or overflowing drain pan near the indoor unit can trip a safety switch. Clearing a visible clog is fine; anything requiring wet-vac or disassembly is a pro job.

**Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a breaker that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the workflow helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Confirm the call for cooling** — checks that the thermostat is sending 24V to the equipment.
3. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower operation, coil condition, and any ice.
4. **Check outdoor power** — voltage at the disconnect and contactor.
5. **Test electricals** — reads the run capacitor's microfarads against the rating, checks the contactor, and inspects the compressor/fan motor windings.
6. **Connect refrigerant gauges** — measures pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge charge. Low charge points to a leak, which they'll then locate.
7. **Leak search if needed** — dye, electronic sniffer, or bubble test to find the source before repairing.

A good tech shows you the failed reading (a capacitor testing 5μF instead of 45μF, or pressures well below spec) rather than just naming a part. Be cautious of anyone who wants to "just add a pound of refrigerant" without finding the leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker/disconnect, bad contactor or capacitor | Reset breaker once; check disconnect is seated | Test contactor/capacitor, replace as needed |
| Weak cooling, house never reaches setpoint | Dirty filter, dirty condenser coil, low refrigerant | Replace filter; gently rinse outdoor coil | Check charge, clean coils, find leak |
| Ice on coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set to Fan Only to thaw; replace filter | Verify airflow, test refrigerant charge |
| System won't turn on at all | Dead thermostat, blown low-voltage fuse | Replace thermostat batteries | Check 24V control circuit and fuse |
| Cooling stops, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain tripped float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear drain line, test/replace float switch |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor or compressor | None — power hazard | Test capacitor; assess compressor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor for a professional visit (regional pricing varies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY, $0 labor)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200 (often credited toward repair)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate float switch / drain clearing:** $100–$300
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+, often making full-unit replacement worth considering
- **Full outdoor unit / system replacement:** $4,500–$12,000+

If your system is under 10 years old, check the York warranty — many parts (including compressors) carry a 10-year registered warranty, which can dramatically cut the parts cost, though labor is usually still yours.

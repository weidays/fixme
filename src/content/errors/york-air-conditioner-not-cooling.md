## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint homeowners report about a York air conditioner. Your system may be running, blowing air, or completely silent, but the house isn't getting cold. Because York units span many series (LX, Affinity, and packaged TCGD/TCJD models) and different ages use different controls, there's no single blinking-light code that covers "not cooling."

Instead, this points to one of several underlying problems: airflow restriction, a refrigerant issue, an electrical component failure, or a control/thermostat fault. This page walks you through what's safe to check yourself and what genuinely needs a licensed HVAC technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops efficiency, and can freeze the indoor coil so no cold air moves.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" not "cool"), or a miswired/failing thermostat.
3. **Frozen indoor (evaporator) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure that stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting.
5. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — caked with dirt, grass, or debris, so heat can't be rejected.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system can't absorb heat; almost always indicates a leak that needs repair.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor unit runs hot and shuts down on high-pressure limit.
9. **Failed contactor or capacitor in the disconnect** — the compressor never energizes.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive; the unit hums or trips but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter** — Pull it out. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the temperature is set below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model. Try lowering the setpoint 3–5°F to trigger a call.
- **Breaker** — Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler and outdoor unit. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals a real fault.
- **Supply and return vents** — Make sure vents aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs. Blocked airflow can freeze the coil.
- **Condensate drain** — A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and shut the system down. Check for standing water near the indoor unit; a wet-shop-vac on the drain line outlet sometimes clears it.
- **Ice check** — If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **FAN ONLY** for a few hours to thaw it before restarting in COOL. If it re-freezes, you have an airflow or refrigerant problem needing a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirms the thermostat sends 24V to the contactor.
2. **Check the outdoor unit** — measures voltage at the contactor, tests the capacitor with a meter, and inspects the fan motor and contactor.
3. **Measure electrical draw** — checks compressor amperage against the data-plate rating to spot a seizing or failing compressor.
4. **Connect refrigerant gauges** — reads suction/head pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge charge and airflow (R-410A and R-22 have very different target pressures).
5. **Inspect coils and airflow** — looks for dirty condenser/evaporator coils, a stuck blower, or a frozen coil.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, uses electronic detectors or dye to find the leak rather than just topping off.

Be cautious of any tech who adds refrigerant without discussing where it leaked out.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but isn't cold | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter, thaw coil on FAN | Check charge, airflow, coil |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | No power / bad contactor / capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Bad fan motor or capacitor | None safe | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw, replace filter | Diagnose airflow or leak |
| Warm air, hissing/oily spot | Refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Locate/repair leak, recharge |
| Unit hums then trips breaker | Failing compressor | Stop resetting | Test compressor, quote replacement |
| Water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, vac line | Clear drain, check float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and unit age):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** under $10 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Coil cleaning (condenser/evaporator):** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher for R-22 systems due to refrigerant cost)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,800+ (often worth weighing against full-system replacement on older units)

Note: R-22 refrigerant is phased out and very expensive. On older R-22 York units, a major leak or compressor failure often makes replacement more economical than repair.

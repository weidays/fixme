## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on your York air conditioner — it's the symptom homeowners report most. Your York system may be doing one of several things: the outdoor condenser runs but the air isn't cold, nothing runs at all, the indoor blower works but the compressor doesn't, or the unit cools weakly and can't keep up.

Most York residential AC units don't display alphanumeric fault codes on the equipment itself the way a furnace board does. Diagnosis relies on what's running, what isn't, and electrical and refrigerant readings taken by a technician. Communicating systems paired with certain York thermostats may show a message, but behavior varies by model and thermostat — don't assume a specific code exists on your unit.

This is a **pro-level** problem overall because the most common serious causes (low refrigerant, compressor and capacitor failure) require tools and certification you shouldn't improvise with. That said, several safe checks below can fix it for free.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks cooling entirely once iced over.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a failed thermostat not calling for cooling.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler loses power.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — very common; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
6. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with dust, grass, and debris, it can't reject heat.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system runs but can't cool; needs leak repair and recharge.
8. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the compressor won't close.
9. **Bad condensate drain / float switch** — a full drain pan trips a safety switch and shuts the system off.
10. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive outcome, usually a last conclusion after other causes are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all safe for a homeowner. If none of them fix it, call a licensed HVAC tech.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the single most common fix.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the temperature is set below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering it 3–5 degrees to force a call for cooling.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler and the outdoor unit. Reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow throughout the house.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (power off first). Never bend the fins or use a pressure washer.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water pooling near the indoor unit or a full drain pan, the float safety switch may have shut the system down. Clearing the clog can restore cooling.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, set the thermostat to OFF (or fan-only) and let it thaw for several hours before running it again.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good York tech follows a logical sequence. Knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — confirms airflow, thermostat call, and what's running versus not.
2. **Check electrical** — tests voltage at the disconnect, contactor, and capacitor with a multimeter. A weak or dead capacitor is a fast, cheap find.
3. **Inspect airflow and coils** — checks the filter, blower, and both coils for dirt or ice.
4. **Take refrigerant readings** — connects gauges to measure pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak, not "topping off."
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to locate the leak before recharging. Recharging without finding the leak is a red flag.
6. **Compressor test** — measures amp draw and windings if the compressor won't start after capacitor and contactor are ruled out.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without documenting pressures, electrical readings, or a leak location.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but isn't cold | Low refrigerant, dirty coil, or frozen coil | Check/replace filter; thaw coil in fan mode | Measure pressures, find leak, clean coil |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker, thermostat, or contactor | Reset breaker once; check thermostat settings/batteries | Test contactor, transformer, control wiring |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit doesn't | Failed capacitor or contactor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor/contactor |
| Ice on the lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter; run fan-only to thaw | Diagnose airflow vs. refrigerant cause |
| Weak cooling, can't keep up | Dirty condenser coil or low refrigerant | Rinse outdoor coil; clear debris | Check charge, coil condition, airflow |
| System shuts off after starting | Full drain pan / float switch tripped | Clear condensate drain line | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Loud hum then nothing | Failed capacitor or seized compressor | None (electrical) | Test capacitor first, then compressor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,800 (often makes replacing an older unit more sensible)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400

Prices vary by region, unit age, and refrigerant type (older R-410A vs. newer refrigerants affect recharge cost). If your York unit is 12+ years old and needs a compressor or coil, ask your tech to compare repair cost against a full replacement.

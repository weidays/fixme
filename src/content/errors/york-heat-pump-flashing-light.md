## What this code means

A flashing light on a York heat pump is the diagnostic LED on the outdoor unit's control board, which on many models is also the defrost board. The board uses blink patterns to report its status.

- **Normal operation:** On most York and JCI boards, a slow, regular heartbeat blink means the board has power and sees no fault.
- **Fault:** A fast blink, a specific flash count, or a repeating code group means the board has logged a problem.

The faults these boards most commonly report are:

- High-pressure switch trips
- Low-pressure switch trips
- Soft or hard lockouts caused by repeated pressure trips
- Defrost or ambient temperature sensor faults
- Communication errors, on communicating models only

**Flash meanings are not universal across York models.** A "3 flashes" on one board revision can mean something different on another. Your unit's code legend is printed on the wiring diagram, usually inside the outdoor unit's control access panel. Reading it means removing a panel near live 240V wiring, so leave that to a technician. If you can see the LED without opening anything, count the flashes and note the pattern before you call.

**Reset behavior:** On typical York boards:

- A single pressure switch trip is a soft lockout. The unit waits out a delay and retries automatically.
- Several trips within a short window cause a hard lockout. The unit stays off until power is removed and restored.

Thresholds and timing vary by board. While the heat pump is locked out, your auxiliary or backup heat may carry the house in heating mode, which raises your electric bill.

*Not covered here:* York furnace flash codes (2 through 10 flashes) come from a different control and are covered on their own pages.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Restricted airflow causing high head pressure.** In heating mode, the indoor coil is the condenser. A clogged filter, closed registers, or a weak blower drives pressure up until the high-pressure switch trips. This is the most common trigger and the one homeowners can sometimes fix.
2. **Dirty or blocked outdoor coil, or a failed outdoor fan, in cooling mode.** Heat can't leave the outdoor coil, so head pressure climbs and the high-pressure switch trips. Typical culprits are cottonwood, grass clippings, leaves, or a fan motor or capacitor failure.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Low charge drops suction pressure and opens the low-pressure switch. This often shows up in cold weather or right after defrost.
4. **Failed or out-of-range temperature sensor.** The defrost coil sensor or the outdoor ambient thermistor can fail or read out of range. The board flags the sensor fault and may fall back to timed defrost or stop running the heat pump.
5. **Faulty pressure switch or loose switch wiring.** The switch or its wiring reports a trip that isn't really happening.
6. **Overcharge or restriction in the refrigerant circuit.** Examples include a stuck metering device or a clogged filter-drier. These cause pressure trips that keep coming back.
7. **Low or unstable 24V control voltage, or a failing control board.** Brownouts or wiring faults can also trigger codes.
8. **Communication fault** between the thermostat, air handler, and outdoor unit. This applies to communicating models only.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm the system mode (Heat or Cool) and setpoint are correct. Replace the thermostat batteries if it has them.
- **Air filter:** Check the filter at the air handler or return grille. Replace it if it's dirty, which is a common fix for heating-mode high-pressure trips.
- **Registers and returns:** Open the supply registers and clear any return grilles that are blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit, viewed from outside only:**
  - Clear leaves, snow, or debris at least 2 feet from all sides.
  - Make sure nothing is blocking the top fan discharge.
  - Gently hose the outside of the coil fins with the power off at the disconnect. Use no pressure washer.
- **Condensate:** Check that the indoor condensate drain or pan isn't overflowing, and that any float or safety switch hasn't shut the system down.
- **Panels:** Make sure the indoor access panels are fully seated.
- **One reset:** Turn the system off at the thermostat. Switch off both the outdoor disconnect and the breaker for about 5 minutes, then restore power. Do this **once**. If the flashing fault returns, stop and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should do the following, roughly in this order:

1. **Read the code.** They read the LED code against the legend on your unit's wiring diagram. On some boards they also pull the stored fault history using the board's test or diagnostic button.
2. **Check airflow.** They inspect the filter, blower operation, and indoor coil cleanliness, and may measure static pressure.
3. **Inspect the outdoor unit.** They check coil condition, fan motor operation, and the fan capacitor.
4. **Measure pressures and temperatures.** Using gauges or probes, they measure superheat and subcooling to tell low charge, overcharge, and restrictions apart. If the charge is low, a leak search should follow, not just a top-off.
5. **Test electrical components.** They test the pressure switches for continuity and check the sensors' resistance against the manufacturer's temperature chart.
6. **Verify voltages and the board.** They check 24V control voltage and line voltage, and confirm the board itself before condemning it.

**Red flags in a quote:** Be wary if the tech recommends replacing the control board without reading the code or checking pressures. The same goes for adding refrigerant with no leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light flashes, heat pump off in winter, aux heat running | High-pressure trip from poor indoor airflow | Replace filter, open registers, one reset | Check blower, indoor coil, static pressure |
| Flashing in summer, outdoor fan not spinning | Fan motor or capacitor failure causing high pressure | Clear debris around unit | Replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Flashing after defrost or in cold weather, weak heat | Low charge opening low-pressure switch | None beyond one reset | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Defrost acting erratic, frost buildup, code shown | Coil or ambient sensor fault | Clear snow and ice from around the base | Test and replace sensor |
| Unit locked out until breaker cycled | Hard lockout after repeated pressure trips | One power reset only | Find the root pressure problem |
| Code with clean filter and clear coil | Faulty switch, wiring, or board | Note flash pattern | Test switch, wiring, 24V, board |
| Thermostat shows communication error | Communication fault (communicating models) | Check thermostat batteries and power | Check communication wiring and board configuration |

Typical US price ranges, including labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|

| Outdoor coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Run or fan capacitor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Defrost or ambient sensor replacement | $150–$350 |
| High- or low-pressure switch replacement | $200–$500 (requires refrigerant recovery on some designs) |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350–$800 |
| Defrost or control board | $350–$800 |

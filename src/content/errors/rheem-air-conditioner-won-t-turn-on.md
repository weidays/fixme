## What this code means

"Won't turn on" is not a stored fault code. It describes an air conditioner that shows no response at all when the thermostat calls for cooling. The outdoor unit stays silent, and usually the indoor blower does too.

On a Rheem split system, the outdoor condenser has no "brain" of its own for starting. It waits for a 24-volt signal (the Y wire) sent from the thermostat through the indoor furnace or air handler. That means a "dead" AC is very often an indoor power or control problem rather than an outdoor one.

How to read the symptoms:

- **Indoor fan works but the outdoor unit is silent:** see the related pages on compressor and outdoor fan problems.
- **Fan runs but the air is warm:** see the related page on fan running without cold air.
- **Nothing runs at all:** start here.

**Does it reset itself?**

- Standard Rheem condensers have no lockout. They restart once power and the call for cooling return, after any built-in anti-short-cycle delay (typically up to about 5 minutes).
- Some newer communicating (EcoNet) or variable-speed systems can hold a fault until power is cycled. Check the thermostat display and your model's manual for details.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat issue.** The thermostat may be blank, have dead batteries, be set to Heat or Off, have its setpoint above room temperature, or still be inside its startup delay.
2. **Tripped breaker or switched-off disconnect.** Check all three possible power points: the AC breaker, the furnace/air handler breaker, and the service switch near the furnace that looks like a light switch.
3. **Tripped condensate overflow (float) switch.** A clogged drain line or full pan cuts the cooling signal. This is very common in attic air handlers.
4. **Blower door or panel not seated.** The door safety switch keeps the indoor unit from powering up when the panel is loose.
5. **Blown low-voltage fuse on the furnace/air handler control board.** The fuse is typically 3A on furnace boards. The rating varies by air handler model, and some air handlers use a resettable breaker instead of a fuse. A blown fuse is often caused by a shorted thermostat wire, for example one chewed or pinched outdoors.
6. **Failed 24V transformer or indoor control board.** These can fail from age or after power surges.
7. **Outdoor unit failures (when indoor power is fine).** Examples are a failed contactor coil, an open high/low-pressure switch on equipped models, or a damaged thermostat wire to the condenser. These overlap with the "compressor not running" diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Thermostat.** Set it to Cool, with the setpoint 3–5°F below room temperature and the fan on Auto. Replace the batteries if the screen is blank or dim. Then wait 5 minutes for the built-in delay to pass.
2. **Breakers.** At the main panel, find the AC (condenser) breaker and the furnace/air handler breaker. If either has tripped, switch it fully Off, then On, **one time only**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
3. **Indoor service switch.** Make sure the switch on or near the furnace or air handler is On. People often mistake it for a light switch.
4. **Outdoor disconnect.** Check that the pull-out block or lever box near the condenser is fully inserted or switched On. Don't open any electrical box beyond operating it as designed.
5. **Condensate drain.** Turn the system off at the thermostat first. Look for water in the drain pan or a float switch on the drain line. If the line is clogged, attach a wet/dry vacuum to the outdoor end where the drain line terminates and draw out the clog. Then use the access port (cleanout) on the line near the indoor unit to flush it with vinegar or water. Wait a few minutes for the float to drop before turning the system back on.
6. **Front panels.** Make sure the furnace or air handler doors are fully seated and latched.
7. **Air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter doesn't usually cause a total no-start, but it can lead to coil freezing once the system runs again.
8. **Single reset.** If you've done all of the above, cycle power off for 1 minute and back on **once**. If the AC still won't start, call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A technician will typically work through these checks in order:

- **Line voltage:** confirms 120V or 240V at the indoor unit and 240V at the outdoor disconnect.
- **24V transformer:** checks the transformer's output at the R and C terminals of the indoor control board.
- **Control fuse:** inspects the low-voltage board fuse (typically 3A on furnace boards; varies by air handler model, and some use a resettable breaker). If it's blown or tripped, they hunt for the short in the thermostat wiring or condenser wire before replacing or resetting it.
- **Control board LED:** reads any diagnostic flash code on the furnace or air handler board. Codes are model-specific.
- **Cooling signal path:** traces the Y signal from the thermostat, through the float switch, to the contactor coil at the condenser.
- **Outdoor safeties and contactor:** tests pressure switches on equipped models and checks the contactor.
- **Communicating systems:** reviews EcoNet fault history.

**Red flags in a quote:** being sold a new condenser or compressor before anyone has verified 24V control voltage. Most "won't turn on" cases are control-side problems.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thermostat screen blank | Dead batteries or no 24V power | Replace batteries; check furnace breaker and switch | Test transformer and fuse; repair wiring |
| Nothing runs; water in drain pan | Float switch tripped | Turn system off; clear condensate line | Clean pan and trap; verify switch |
| Nothing runs; breaker found tripped | Electrical fault or one-off overload | Reset once | Find short or failing component |
| Thermostat on but no response; board LED off | Blown low-voltage fuse or failed transformer | Check panel seating; reset once | Locate short; replace fuse or transformer |
| Indoor runs, outdoor silent | Contactor, wiring, or pressure switch | Check outdoor disconnect | Diagnose condenser controls |
| Fault shown on EcoNet thermostat | Communicating fault | Note the code; cycle power once | Read fault history; repair |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Batteries, filter, condensate line clearing (DIY) | $0–$40 |
| Service call / diagnostic | $90–$200 |
| Control fuse replacement plus short repair | $100–$300 |
| Condensate float switch replacement or drain cleaning | $100–$275 |
| Thermostat replacement (installed) | $150–$450 |
| 24V transformer replacement | $150–$350 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Thermostat wire repair or replacement | $150–$500 |
| Furnace/air handler control board | $400–$1,200 |

Prices vary by region and by board type. Communicating (EcoNet) control boards can cost more than the top of the board range shown above.

These figures cover the control-side repairs behind most "won't turn on" cases. Outdoor unit failures beyond the contactor, such as a pressure switch or a compressor-side problem, are not included in this table and can cost far more. See the Rheem AC Compressor Not Running page for those repairs.

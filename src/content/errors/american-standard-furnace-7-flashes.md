## What this code means

Seven flashes on your American Standard furnace's diagnostic LED indicates a **gas valve circuit error** — the control board is not seeing the gas valve circuit energized correctly. In plain terms, when the board commands the gas valve to open (or verifies it is closed), the electrical signal on that circuit does not match what the board expects.

This is a **control-circuit** fault, not a gas-supply or flame problem. The board is watching the valve wiring, the valve coil, and its own valve driver. If any of those is open, shorted, miswired, or failed, the furnace posts 7 flashes and refuses to fire for safety.

On most American Standard (and sister-brand Trane) integrated controls, this code **holds until the problem is corrected and power is cycled** — it does not quietly clear itself, because the board cannot confirm the valve circuit is healthy. Flash-count legends differ slightly between board revisions, so confirm 7 flashes against the legend printed inside your blower-compartment door before acting.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

**The ranking below assumes the furnace has not been worked on recently.** If 7 flashes appeared in the hours or days right after a service call, tune-up, or parts replacement, jump straight to cause 4 — miswiring is the leading suspect in that scenario, ahead of everything else on this list.

1. **Loose, corroded, or damaged gas valve wiring/connector** — the wires or plug between the control board and the gas valve have backed out, corroded, or chafed.
2. **Failed or degraded gas valve** — an open or shorted coil winding, or a valve that is electrically worn to the point it no longer draws the correct current, so the board flags the circuit.
3. **Faulty valve driver on the control board** — the board's own output that energizes the valve has failed.
4. **Miswired valve after a recent repair** — a polarity or terminal swap during a prior service call leaves the circuit incorrect. Statistically uncommon overall, but **the number-one cause whenever the code followed recent work on the furnace.**

Note the theme: everything here is on the **valve control circuit**. Gas-supply pressure, ignitor, and flame-sensing problems are separate codes and do not cause 7 flashes on this brand.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do — none require opening the sealed cabinet:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it is set to Heat and the setpoint is above room temperature. Replace the thermostat batteries if it uses them.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter won't cause this code, but replace it if it's dirty so the tech starts with a clean system.
- **Breaker / switch:** Find the furnace's dedicated switch (looks like a light switch near the unit) and the breaker. Turn the unit off, wait 30 seconds, and turn it back on **once** to attempt a single reset.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return registers are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate line (high-efficiency units):** Check that the drain isn't obviously clogged or overflowing.

If 7 flashes returns after your single reset, stop. The fault is inside the cabinet — that's technician territory.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. Confirm the flash code against the board legend and note whether it's latching.
2. Check for **miswiring** first if the unit was recently serviced.
3. Power down and inspect the **gas valve wiring harness and connector** for looseness, corrosion, or damage.
4. Use a multimeter to measure the **gas valve coil resistance** against spec, checking for an open or shorted winding.
5. Verify the **board's valve-driver output voltage** when heat is called, isolating whether the board or the valve is at fault.
6. Replace the failed component — connector, valve, or control board — and cycle power to clear the latched code, then confirm a full heat cycle.

Sanity check: a good tech isolates *which side* of the circuit failed (board vs. valve vs. wiring) before quoting a part. Beware anyone who jumps straight to "replace the whole furnace."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 flashes, no ignition, blower may run | Loose/corroded valve connector | One power-cycle reset | Inspect and repair harness/connector |
| Code returns immediately after reset | Failed gas valve coil | None — call a pro | Measure coil resistance, replace valve |
| Code appeared after a recent repair | Miswired valve | None — call the servicer back | Correct wiring per diagram |
| No response to heat call at all | Failed board valve driver | Confirm thermostat & breaker first | Test driver output, replace control board |
| Intermittent 7-flash, sometimes runs | Marginal valve or chafed wire | One reset, then log occurrences | Locate intermittent fault, replace part |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90 – $180 (often applied to the repair).
- **Wiring or connector repair:** $150 – $300.
- **Gas valve replacement:** $300 – $900. Single-stage valves with good access sit near the bottom; two-stage and modulating valves, or a valve buried behind other components, commonly land in the $400 – $900 band.
- **Control board replacement:** $400 – $1,200 for an integrated furnace control. Communicating and modulating boards — the kind used on Platinum-series equipment — can exceed $1,200 installed, especially where the board has to be ordered through a dealer.
- **DIY portion:** $0 — the only homeowner steps (filter, batteries, one reset) are free; anything inside the cabinet is professional work.

Because the failed part could be inexpensive (a connector) or genuinely costly (a communicating board), insist on a diagnosis that identifies the specific component before approving a big-ticket replacement — and get the board price in writing before the part is ordered.

## What this code means

"Breaker keeps tripping" is not a Carrier error code. It is a symptom: the circuit breaker in your main panel that feeds the outdoor condenser shuts off, sometimes again and again. In rarer cases the trip is on the furnace or air-handler breaker that powers the indoor side.

A breaker trips for one of three reasons:

- **Sustained overcurrent:** the unit is drawing more amps than the circuit is rated for.
- **A short or ground fault:** a wire or motor winding is touching metal.
- **A failing breaker:** the breaker itself has worn out.

There is no automatic reset. The breaker stays off until someone resets it by hand.

Single-stage Carrier units have no outdoor control board, so they record nothing. Two-stage units and Infinity/Greenspeed variable-speed units can store fault history on the outdoor board or inverter. That history can help a technician pinpoint the problem, but what is stored varies by model.

If your Carrier is a heat pump rather than a straight AC, see the separate heat pump breaker page listed below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** Without proper capacitor support, the compressor or fan motor struggles to start. It pulls locked-rotor amps until the breaker trips. This is the most common and cheapest cause.
2. **Compressor drawing excessive current or grounded.** Causes include worn bearings, a seized or "locked" compressor, or a winding shorted to the shell. If the breaker trips instantly every time, a grounded compressor is a serious possibility.
3. **High head pressure from a dirty condenser coil or blocked airflow.** Debris, cottonwood, grass clippings or shrubs against the coil force the compressor to work harder and draw more amps. The breaker trips after the unit has run for a while, typically on hot afternoons.
4. **Failing condenser fan motor.** A fan motor with shorted windings or seized bearings can trip the breaker. A fan that stops spinning also causes high pressure, which leads back to cause 3.
5. **Damaged or loose wiring.** Possible culprits include chewed or chafed wire, a burnt contactor, loose lugs at the disconnect, or corroded connections. These create shorts or heat buildup.
6. **Worn, undersized or overheated breaker.** Breakers weaken with age and repeated trips. Loose panel terminals also cause nuisance trips. Check whether the breaker matches the MOCP on the unit's rating plate.
7. **Refrigerant system problems.** Overcharge or non-condensables raise head pressure and amp draw. A technician will rule these out after the electrical checks.
8. **Inverter or drive fault (Infinity/Greenspeed variable-speed models only).** An internal fault in the inverter can trip the breaker. These models are diagnosed through the board's fault codes.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Reset the breaker once.** Set the thermostat to OFF first. Push the breaker fully to OFF, then back to ON, and wait 5 minutes. Then set the thermostat to COOL. If the breaker trips again, stop and leave it off.
- **Look for warning signs at the panel.** Note any burning smell, scorch marks or a hot breaker. If you find any, leave it off and call an electrician or HVAC technician.
- **Replace a dirty air filter.** A clogged filter alone rarely trips a breaker, but it adds load and can lead to coil freeze-ups.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it is set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and replace its batteries if it uses them.
- **Clear around the outdoor unit.** Remove leaves, grass and debris. Trim plants back to about 2 feet of clearance. Gently rinse the outside of the coil fins with a garden hose (not a pressure washer) only with power OFF at the breaker. Do not remove the top or any panels.
- **Make sure indoor airflow is open.** Supply registers and return grilles should be open and unblocked.
- **Note when the trip happens.** Is it instant on startup, after a few minutes, or only on the hottest days? Did you hear a hum, buzz or clank? Write it down and pass it to the technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Verify the circuit.** Check the breaker size and wire gauge against the rating plate's MCA/MOCP. Inspect the panel and disconnect for loose or heat-damaged terminals.
- **Test the compressor with power off.**
  - Megohm or ohm test of the windings (C-S, C-R, S-R) and each winding to ground. A grounded or open winding usually means compressor replacement.
- **Test the run capacitor.** Measure microfarads against the label (±6% is typical). Also look for bulging or leaking.
- **Inspect the contactor.** Look for pitted or welded contacts and burned wiring.
- **Check the condenser fan motor.** Test the windings and bearings, then measure the motor's amp draw against its nameplate.
- **Measure startup and running current.** A clamp meter shows locked-rotor amps (LRA) and running amps (RLA) compared with the nameplate. An inrush that never settles points to a hard-start or locked compressor.
- **Check refrigerant pressures, if the unit will run.** Gauges and line temperatures reveal high head pressure or a refrigerant overcharge.
- **Read stored faults (communicating models only).** On Infinity/Greenspeed systems, the technician reads stored faults at the outdoor board or wall control.

**Red flags in a quote:** being told "you need a new compressor" without a winding or ground test, or a suggestion to upsize the breaker.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trips instantly when AC starts | Grounded or locked compressor, shorted wire | Reset once, then leave off | Winding/megohm test, compressor or wiring repair |
| Hums, then trips within seconds | Failed run capacitor or hard-starting compressor | Leave breaker off | Test/replace capacitor, consider hard-start kit |
| Trips after running 15+ minutes on hot days | Dirty condenser coil, weak fan, high head pressure | Clear debris, hose off coil with power off | Coil cleaning, fan motor test, pressure check |
| Outdoor fan not spinning before trip | Fan motor or fan capacitor failure | Leave off | Replace fan motor or capacitor |
| Burning smell or scorch marks at panel/disconnect | Loose or overheated connection, failing breaker | Leave off, call promptly | Repair terminals, replace breaker or disconnect |
| Trips randomly, even when AC is idle | Worn breaker or chafed wire shorting intermittently | Leave off | Inspect wiring, replace breaker |
| Variable-speed unit trips with fault code | Inverter or drive fault | Note the code from the wall control | Read the fault history, repair inverter |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices, parts plus labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $75 – $200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120 – $350 |
| Hard-start kit | $150 – $400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Condenser fan motor | $300 – $700 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100 – $250 |
| Wiring, lug or disconnect repair | $150 – $500 |
| Breaker replacement (electrician) | $150 – $350 |
| Inverter board (variable-speed models) | $800 – $2,000+ |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $3,500+ (lower if parts are under warranty, labor and refrigerant still extra) |

If an older unit, often 12+ years and especially one running R-22, needs a compressor, many owners replace the condenser instead. That typically costs $4,000 – $8,000+ installed.

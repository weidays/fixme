## What this code means

**If you smell gas right now, stop reading and do this first:**

1. **Do not** flip light switches, use the thermostat, unplug anything, start a car in an attached garage, or light anything. Any of these can spark.
2. **Get everyone, including pets, out of the house.** Leave doors open behind you if that's quick and easy.
3. **From outside and well away from the house**, call your gas utility's emergency line or **911**.
4. **Do not go back inside** until the utility or fire department says it is safe.
5. Do not try to find the leak yourself, and do not operate any gas valve. Utility crews will shut off gas at the meter if needed.

"Gas smell" is not a code on the Rheem control board. No Rheem or Ruud furnace has a fault code or LED flash pattern for a gas leak. The board's flame-sensing circuit only confirms that a flame lit during a call for heat. It cannot detect gas escaping from piping, fittings or the valve. That is why a smell is always an emergency, even if the furnace seems to run normally and shows no fault.

Gas suppliers add an odorant (mercaptan) to natural gas and propane, which gives it a rotten-egg or sulfur smell. Propane is heavier than air and collects near the floor, so LP homes may notice the smell lowest in basements and closets.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

Ranked by how often leaks are found on gas furnace installations. Rheem does not publish a ranked troubleshooting order for gas odor. Its manuals direct you to leave the building and call the gas supplier.

1. **Loose or failed fitting on the gas supply line at the furnace.** The flex connector, union, sediment trap ("drip leg") or black-iron joints near the furnace are the most common leak points. Vibration, age, or a fitting disturbed during service can cause the leak.
2. **Leak at the furnace gas valve.** Possible leak points are the inlet/outlet connections, pressure-tap plugs left loose after a pressure check, or, less often, the valve body itself.
3. **Delayed ignition releasing unburned gas.** Gas flows but lights late, so a puff of odor escapes at startup. This is often heard as a bang. See the related Loud bang on startup page. On Rheem furnaces this is usually tied to dirty burners, misaligned crossover ports or weak ignition, all of which need a technician. A gas smell at startup still means leave the house and call the utility first. Call the technician after the all-clear.
4. **Leak elsewhere in the house gas piping.** The odor may be near the furnace but come from the water heater, range, dryer, a fireplace, or piping in the walls or crawlspace.
5. **Leak at the meter or outdoor service line.** The smell can be drawn in through the furnace combustion-air intake or nearby openings. Utilities handle leaks at the meter and outdoor line.
6. **Vent or combustion problems mistaken for a gas smell.** A blocked or disconnected flue, a cracked heat exchanger or poor combustion can create sharp, acrid exhaust odors. These are a carbon monoxide risk. CO itself is odorless, so a CO alarm going off is a separate emergency: get out and call 911.
7. **Sewer gas or dried-out floor drain trap.** This is a non-gas source of sulfur smell near basement furnaces. Only rule it out *after* the utility confirms there is no gas leak.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

With a gas smell, there are **no DIY checks before calling**. Leaving the house and calling the gas utility or 911 from outside *is* the first step. The filter, breaker and condensate line have nothing to do with a gas leak, so don't spend time on them.

Once the utility has visited and declared the home safe, you can do the following:

- **Leave the furnace off if the utility tagged or shut it off.** A red tag means the furnace must not be used until a licensed contractor repairs it. Do not relight it or turn the gas back on yourself.
- **Check your CO alarms.** Make sure there is a working carbon monoxide alarm on each level and near sleeping areas. Replace the batteries if they are due.
- **Look at the outside vent terminals** for 90%+ furnaces with PVC pipes through the wall or roof. Check from the outside only for snow, ice, nests or debris blocking them. Do not take the pipes apart.
- **Write down what you noticed:** when the smell happens (constant, only at startup, only while running), where it is strongest, and any bang or rumble at ignition. This helps the technician find the cause quickly.
- **Do not** bring the furnace back by resetting it repeatedly, cycling power, or opening gas valves to "test" it.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed HVAC or gas technician should do most of the following:

- **Sweep with a combustible gas detector** along the supply line, sediment trap, flex connector, shutoff valve and gas valve. Then check the rest of the house piping and appliances.
- **Confirm the leak with leak-detection solution** on suspect joints. The tech will tighten or remake fittings, typically with new approved sealant or a new flex connector.
- **Check the gas valve.** This includes the pressure-tap plugs and inlet/outlet connections. If the valve body leaks or fails to close fully, it will be replaced, not repaired.
- **Run a pressure test** on house piping if the leak location is unclear or piping was repaired. Many jurisdictions require this before the gas is turned back on.
- **Measure manifold gas pressure** against the rating plate for natural gas or LP and correct it if needed. An LP conversion kit must match the model.
- **Watch the ignition sequence** for delayed ignition, and inspect burners, crossover alignment and the igniter if a startup puff or bang is reported.
- **Perform combustion and venting checks** with a combustion analyzer. These check CO in the flue, draft and vent integrity, and heat exchanger condition when exhaust odors are suspected.
- **Check the board's fault history** with the LED flash codes. Codes for ignition retries or flame loss support a delayed-ignition diagnosis. Remember that no code indicates a leak.

If a quote says "replace the furnace" for a simple fitting leak, ask exactly where the leak was found and how it was confirmed. A cracked heat exchanger or a failed valve on an old unit can make replacement reasonable. A leaking flex connector does not.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rotten-egg smell near furnace, constant | Leaking fitting, flex connector or sediment trap | Evacuate; touch nothing; call gas utility/911 from outside | Locate the leak with a detector, remake fittings, replace the flex connector, pressure test |
| Smell strongest at the furnace gas valve | Loose pressure-tap plug or valve connection; failed valve | Evacuate; touch nothing; call gas utility/911 from outside | Tighten or reseal; replace gas valve; check manifold pressure |
| Brief gas whiff at startup, sometimes with a bang | Delayed ignition | Evacuate; touch nothing; call gas utility/911 from outside. After the all-clear, call an HVAC technician and do not run the furnace until it is repaired | Clean and align burners, check igniter and gas pressure, verify ignition timing |
| Gas smell throughout house, not just at furnace | House piping or another appliance leaking | Evacuate; touch nothing; call gas utility/911 from outside | Utility isolates the leak; plumber or gas fitter repairs piping |
| Smell outdoors near meter, drawn inside | Meter or service line leak | Leave the area; touch nothing; call gas utility from a safe distance | Utility repairs meter or service line |
| Sharp exhaust smell, headaches, CO alarm | Vent blockage, disconnected flue, cracked heat exchanger | Get out; call 911; seek medical attention if anyone has symptoms; check outside vent terminals only after the all-clear | Combustion analysis, vent repair, heat exchanger inspection or replacement |
| Sulfur smell after utility found no gas leak | Dry floor drain trap or sewer gas | Pour water into floor drains | Plumber if the smell persists |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including labor. Emergency, after-hours and weekend rates often add $100–$300.

| Item | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Gas utility emergency odor response | $0 (most utilities) |
| HVAC/gas technician diagnostic visit | $90 – $250 |
| Tighten or remake leaking fitting / sediment trap | $100 – $300 |
| Replace appliance flex gas connector | $150 – $350 |
| Replace furnace gas valve (single- or two-stage) | $300 – $750; modulating valves can be higher |
| Burner cleaning and ignition adjustment (delayed ignition) | $150 – $400 |
| House gas piping leak repair and pressure test | $200 – $1,200+, depending on access |
| Vent pipe repair or reconnection | $150 – $600 |
| Heat exchanger replacement (if cracked; check warranty) | $1,500 – $3,500 |
| Furnace replacement (if the heat exchanger fails on an old unit) | $3,500 – $8,000+ installed |

Rheem parts warranties, often 10 years with registration, and lifetime or 20-year heat exchanger coverage on many models may cover the gas valve or heat exchanger part. Warranty terms vary by series, install date and whether the unit was registered. Heat exchanger coverage also differs between tiers such as Classic and Prestige, so check the warranty for your exact model. Labor usually isn't covered unless you have an extended plan.

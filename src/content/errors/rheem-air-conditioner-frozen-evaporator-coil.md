## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is a condition, not a numbered fault code on most Rheem air conditioners. The evaporator coil sits above the furnace or inside the air handler. When the coil surface drops below freezing, moisture pulled from your indoor air freezes on it instead of draining away.

Signs you'll notice:
- Weak or warm airflow from the vents.
- Ice on the copper suction line (the larger, insulated pipe) at the indoor or outdoor unit.
- Water around the furnace or air handler as the ice melts.

The system doesn't "reset" from this condition. It stays iced until the airflow or refrigerant problem is fixed and the coil fully thaws.

On EcoNet-communicating Rheem systems, the thermostat may log related alerts, such as low airflow or blower faults. Exact alert text varies by model and control board.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Restricted airflow from a dirty air filter.** This is the most common cause by far. Less warm air crossing the coil lets its temperature fall below 32°F.
2. **Closed or blocked supply registers and return grilles.** Furniture, rugs, or many closed vents can starve the coil of air.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil.** Dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate the coil and restrict airflow, especially when filters have been skipped.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Low pressure means a colder coil. Rheem coils, like those from other manufacturers, can develop leaks at joints or tubing over time.
5. **Blower problems.** Possible faults include:
   - a failing blower motor or capacitor
   - a dirty blower wheel
   - an incorrect blower speed setting
   - a variable-speed motor fault that reduces airflow
6. **Running the AC in cool outdoor temperatures.** Operating below roughly 60–65°F outdoors can drop coil pressure enough to ice up.
7. **Metering device problems.** A restricted or failing TXV, or a clogged piston, underfeeds the coil.
8. **Thermostat set to fan "On" with very low setpoints for long run times.** Combined with any of the above, this can tip a marginal system into freezing. It is rarely the sole cause.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thaw first.** Set the thermostat to **Off** (cooling off) and the fan to **On**. Let the ice melt completely, usually 2–4 hours or longer.
- **Handle the meltwater.** Put towels near the furnace or air handler. Watch the condensate drain and the overflow pan.
- **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. Make sure the airflow arrow points toward the blower and the filter is the correct size.
- **Open supply registers.** Clear any obstructions from return grilles throughout the house.
- **Check the thermostat.**
  - Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint (around 72–78°F).
  - Replace weak batteries.
  - Avoid running the AC on cool days.
- **Check the condensate line.** If water is backing up, clear the visible drain line opening or use a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain end. Make sure the line isn't kinked.
- **Seat the panels.** Confirm the furnace or air handler access panels are properly closed.
- **Restart and watch.** After a full thaw, restart cooling. If ice returns within a day with a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a technician. That pattern points to refrigerant, coil, or blower problems.

Do not chip ice, use heat guns, open the cabinet, or add refrigerant yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically work through these steps:

- **Confirm a full thaw.** They should make sure the coil is completely thawed before measuring. Readings on an iced coil are misleading.
- **Measure static pressure and airflow.** This checks blower speed taps or ECM settings against Rheem's installation data for your coil tonnage.
- **Inspect the blower.** They'll check the wheel, motor amperage, and run capacitor. On ECM motors, they'll check motor and board signals.
- **Inspect the evaporator coil.** This means looking for dirt, matted fins, or corrosion, usually through an access panel or by removing the coil.
- **Read refrigerant pressures.** They'll measure superheat and subcooling and compare them to the charging chart on the outdoor unit's data plate.
- **Leak-search if the charge is low.** Methods include an electronic detector, bubble solution, UV dye, or a nitrogen pressure test. Topping off without finding the leak is a red flag on a quote.
- **Check the metering device.** They'll look for TXV or piston restriction and a plugged filter-drier.
- **Review EcoNet history.** On communicating systems, they'll check the thermostat's fault history.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, weak airflow, filter clogged | Dirty filter | Thaw, replace filter | Usually none needed |
| Ice after vents were closed or blocked | Restricted airflow | Open registers, clear returns | Duct or static pressure check if it recurs |
| Ices up repeatedly with a clean filter | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw, keep filters on schedule | Coil cleaning |
| Ice on suction line, hissing, gradual cooling loss | Refrigerant leak / low charge | Thaw and shut off cooling | Leak search, repair, evacuate and recharge |
| Little or no air from vents, humming at the furnace | Blower motor or capacitor fault | Check breaker and thermostat fan setting | Test and replace capacitor or motor; check ECM settings |
| Freezes mainly on cool nights or mild days | Low ambient operation | Don't run AC below about 60–65°F outdoors | Check charge and controls |
| Freezes with a correct charge and good airflow | TXV or piston restriction | Thaw | Replace metering device and filter-drier |
| Water on floor during thaw | Meltwater overwhelming the drain | Towels; clear condensate line | Inspect pan, drain, float switch |

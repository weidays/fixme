## What this code means

"Compressor not running" is a symptom, not a stored fault code. You will usually notice one of these:

- The outdoor unit hums but nothing starts.
- The outdoor fan spins but the air from your vents is not cold.
- The outdoor unit is completely silent while the indoor blower runs.

The compressor is the pump that moves refrigerant. Without it, the system cannot cool.

How this shows up depends on the model. Control type varies by model, so check your unit's service manual:

- **Basic single-stage units (GSX13, GSX14, GSX16, GSXN, older CKL/CPLE models):** These have no diagnostic display. The compressor is switched by a contactor and started with a run capacitor, and the diagnosis is purely electrical.
- **Communicating and inverter units:** The GSXC16 is single-stage and communicating-capable. The GSXC18 is two-stage with ComfortNet. The GSXV9 is the inverter model. On these units, the outdoor control may show LED flash codes or report faults to a communicating thermostat. A technician should read these codes first, using the service manual for that model.

**Reset behavior:**

- A tripped internal compressor overload resets automatically once the compressor cools.
- An inverter or board lockout may hold until power is cycled.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** This is the most common cause by far. The compressor hums or clicks off, and the fan may still run on its half of a dual capacitor.
2. **Failed or pitted contactor, or no 24V signal reaching it.** The outdoor unit stays silent or only the fan runs. The signal can be lost to a thermostat issue, a broken low-voltage wire or a tripped safety switch in the control circuit, such as a high- or low-pressure switch, if equipped. Many basic single-stage units do not have both switches.
3. **Tripped internal thermal overload.** This is often triggered by a dirty condenser coil, a failed outdoor fan, low refrigerant charge or a bad capacitor. The compressor restarts after cooling, then trips again.
4. **Power supply problem.** Examples include a tripped breaker, a blown fuse in the outdoor disconnect, or one leg of the 240V supply lost.
5. **Locked rotor or hard-start condition.** The compressor draws high amps and hums without starting. A technician may try a hard-start kit.
6. **Shorted, open or grounded compressor windings.** This means the compressor itself has failed and needs replacement.
7. **Inverter drive or control board fault (inverter and communicating models only).** This is diagnosed from the board's LED flash codes or thermostat alerts.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL, at least 3°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Breakers:** Check both the AC breaker and the furnace or air handler breaker in your panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and see our breaker article.
- **Outdoor disconnect switch:** Confirm it is in the ON position. Do not open the fuse block.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it is dirty. A clogged filter can freeze the coil and, on units equipped with a low-pressure switch, trip that switch.
- **Around the condenser:** Clear leaves, grass clippings and debris. Keep 2 feet of clearance on all sides.
- **Vents and condensate:** Make sure supply registers are open. Check that the condensate line is not backed up, since a float switch can shut down cooling.
- **Exterior panels:** Make sure they are fully seated.
- **Overload cool-down:** If the outdoor unit hummed and then shut off, turn the system off at the thermostat for 1 to 2 hours. Then try once. If it fails again, call a technician.

Never push the contactor in by hand. Never open the electrical panel on the outdoor unit.

## How a technician will diagnose it

1. Read any board LED codes or thermostat alerts (communicating and inverter models).
2. Check for 240V at the disconnect and contactor line side, and 24V at the contactor coil.
3. Inspect the contactor for pitting, then measure voltage across it under load.
4. Discharge the capacitor and test it with a capacitance meter. A reading more than about 6% below the rated µF means it fails.
5. Measure compressor winding resistance across C-S, C-R and S-R, and test for a short to ground. An open winding when the compressor is hot can simply mean the overload is tripped.
6. Measure amp draw at startup. Locked-rotor amps with no start points to a seized compressor.
7. Inspect the condenser coil and fan motor. If the compressor runs, connect gauges to check the refrigerant charge and any pressure switches the unit is equipped with.

**Sanity-checking a quote:** A capacitor quote should come with a µF reading. A compressor replacement quote should come with winding and ground test results.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor unit hums, no start | Bad capacitor or locked rotor | Turn the system off to protect the compressor | Test or replace the capacitor; hard-start kit; amp draw test |
| Fan runs, compressor silent | Capacitor (compressor side), overload trip | Let the unit cool 1–2 hours, try once | Capacitor test, winding resistance test |
| Outdoor unit completely silent | No power, contactor, no 24V signal | Check breaker, disconnect and thermostat | Voltage checks, contactor replacement, wiring repair |
| Compressor starts then stops after minutes | Overheating: dirty coil, low charge | Clear debris around the unit, replace the filter | Coil cleaning, leak search, recharge |
| Board LED flashing (GSXC/GSXV) | Inverter, pressure or board fault | Note the flash pattern, cycle power once. If it locks out again, do not reset again | Read the code, repair per the service manual |
| Breaker trips when the compressor tries to start | Grounded winding, shorted capacitor | Do not reset again | Ground test, possible compressor replacement |

## Repair costs

These are typical US prices, including parts and labor.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnosis | $80–$200 |
| Run capacitor | $120–$300 |

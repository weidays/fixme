## What this code means

**If you smell gas, your CO alarm is sounding, or anyone feels dizzy, nauseous or headachy: get everyone out of the house now. Don't flip switches on your way out. Once you're outside, call 911 or your gas utility's emergency line.**

**If there are no gas or CO warning signs, still turn the furnace off.** Use the thermostat first, then the furnace power switch or breaker. Open a few windows and call a licensed HVAC technician. Don't keep running the furnace.

"Flame rollout" means flame or very hot combustion gases came out of the front of the burner box instead of being drawn through the heat exchanger and out the vent. Lennox furnaces have one or more **flame rollout switches** mounted on or near the burner box. These are heat-sensing safety switches. When one opens, the control board shuts off the gas valve.

Here's how it shows up on your furnace:

- **Most Lennox rollout switches are manual reset.** The furnace stays off until someone physically resets the switch. Turning the power off and on won't clear it.
- **On many models the rollout switches are wired in series with the limit circuit.** So the board may show a limit-type code instead of the word "rollout." On boards with 7-segment displays that's often E250. On older boards it's a limit flash pattern on the LED. What displays depends on your control board, so check the chart on the furnace door.
- **A rollout trip is not like an ordinary limit trip.** Normal limit trips usually come from overheating caused by poor airflow, and they reset automatically. A rollout trip almost always points to a combustion or venting problem.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Blocked or restricted vent or flue.** Bird nests, snow, ice or debris can block the exhaust. So can a crushed or disconnected PVC pipe, or a blocked chimney on 80% models. Exhaust can't leave, so the flame gets pushed out the front.
2. **Restricted heat exchanger passages.** On condensing models (SL280, EL296, SLP98, EL195), the secondary heat exchanger can clog with debris or corrosion. On 80% models, soot can build up in the primary cells. Either way, flue gas can't pass through.
3. **Cracked or failed heat exchanger.** A crack disrupts the draft and lets flame roll out. This is the most serious cause because it can also leak carbon monoxide into the house.
4. **Burner problems.** Dirty, rusted or misaligned burners, or burners that light late, can push flame outside the burner box at ignition.
5. **Weak combustion inducer or blocked combustion air intake.** On direct-vent models, a blocked intake pipe or an inducer that isn't moving enough air can cause rollout.
6. **Overfiring.** If manifold gas pressure is too high, or the furnace has the wrong orifices for the gas type (for example after a propane conversion), the flame is larger than the burner box can contain.
7. **Failed rollout switch.** Occasionally the switch itself is faulty or was weakened by past trips. A technician should only conclude this after ruling out every cause above.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Only do these after you've confirmed there's no gas smell and no CO alarm.

- **Turn the furnace off and leave it off.** Set the thermostat to OFF and switch off the furnace service switch or breaker.
- **Check your CO alarms.** Make sure they have working batteries and stay near them. If one sounds, get out and call 911.
- **Look at the outdoor vent and intake terminations.** Only look at what you can see from outside, usually the white PVC pipes on a wall or the roof. Clear snow, ice, leaves or a visible nest from the opening if you can reach it safely from the ground. Don't take pipes apart.
- **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter doesn't cause rollout, but the technician will need good airflow to test the furnace properly.
- **Make sure the exterior panels are seated.** Don't open the burner compartment.
- **Don't press the rollout switch reset button**, and don't restart the furnace repeatedly. A rollout trip needs diagnosis before the furnace runs again.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A proper rollout diagnosis looks like this. You can use it to judge whether a visit was thorough:

- **Confirms which switch tripped.** The tech checks the limit and rollout circuit and finds which rollout switch is open. They look for scorch marks on the burner box, wiring and switch.
- **Inspects the full vent system.** That includes pipe slope, joints, terminations and, on 80% models, the chimney or B-vent and its draft.
- **Checks the inducer and pressure switch readings.** They confirm the inducer is pulling enough draft.
- **Inspects the heat exchanger.** This is done with a camera or by removing the burners. On condensing units they check the secondary coil for blockage. Many techs also run a combustion analysis and take CO readings.
- **Pulls, cleans and inspects the burners.** They check alignment and watch ignition for late lighting.
- **Measures inlet and manifold gas pressure** and confirms the orifices match the gas type.
- **Resets the switch only after the cause is fixed.** Then they run several heating cycles while watching the flame. They replace the switch if it is damaged or won't hold.

A tech who simply resets the switch and leaves, without inspecting the venting and heat exchanger, has not diagnosed the problem.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Furnace dead after a storm or snowfall; limit or rollout code | Blocked vent or intake termination | Turn the furnace off. Clear visible snow or debris from the outside pipe openings only | Inspect the vent, reset the rollout switch, test-fire |
| Rollout trips repeatedly, condensing model | Clogged secondary heat exchanger | Keep the furnace off | Inspect or flush the secondary heat exchanger, or replace it |
| CO alarm sounding, or soot near the furnace | Cracked heat exchanger or severe venting failure | Evacuate and call 911 or the gas utility | Combustion and CO testing, heat exchanger inspection, red-tag if unsafe |
| Loud whoosh or flame flash at ignition before the trip | Delayed ignition from dirty or misaligned burners | Keep the furnace off | Clean and align burners, check ignition |
| Large, lazy or yellow flame before the trip | High gas pressure or wrong orifices | Keep the furnace off | Measure and adjust manifold pressure, correct orifices |
| Trip with no fault found anywhere | Failed rollout switch | None | Replace the rollout switch after ruling out other causes |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices, including labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic visit | $100–$200 |
| Clear a blocked vent or intake; repair a vent section | $150–$500 |
| Clean and realign burners | $150–$350 |
| Gas pressure adjustment or orifice correction | $100–$300 |
| Rollout switch replacement | $150–$350 |
| Inducer motor replacement | $400–$900 |
| Secondary heat exchanger flush or replacement (condensing models) | $300–$2,500 |
| Primary heat exchanger replacement | $1,500–$4,000+ |
| Full furnace replacement, if the heat exchanger has failed on an older unit | $4,000–$9,000 |

Lennox heat exchangers often carry long or lifetime limited warranties if the furnace was registered. The part may be covered, but labor usually isn't. Check your warranty before approving a heat exchanger repair.

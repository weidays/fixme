## What this code means

"Fan runs but no cold air" is a symptom, not a stored fault code. The indoor blower is moving air, but the outdoor half of the system isn't doing its job. Usually the compressor and outdoor fan are not running at all. Sometimes they run but aren't moving heat out of the house.

This page covers the case where **the outdoor unit is silent or humming but not cooling**. Other problems have their own pages:

- If the outdoor unit runs normally but the house still won't cool down, see "American Standard AC Not Cooling."
- If the indoor coil or suction line is iced, see "American Standard AC Frozen Evaporator Coil."

How the problem shows up depends on the model:

- **Single-stage units** usually give no code. The outdoor unit simply won't start.
- **Two-stage and variable-speed units** may show diagnostic flashes on the outdoor control board or an alert on a compatible communicating thermostat.
- **Lockout behavior also varies.** Some models restart on their own when conditions clear. Others hold a lockout until power is cycled. Check the label inside your unit's service panel or your installation manual.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Outdoor power interrupted.** A tripped AC breaker, a pulled or blown disconnect at the outdoor unit, or a blown fuse in the disconnect.
2. **Failed run capacitor (dual capacitor).** This is the most common hardware failure. The unit hums or clicks, but the compressor and/or fan won't start. Bulging tops are typical.
3. **Failed or pitted contactor.** The 24V call arrives, but the contactor won't pull in. Or it is welded shut, which shows a different pattern.
4. **Low-voltage control problem.** A broken thermostat wire to the outdoor unit, a blown 3–5A fuse on the indoor control board, or a tripped float/condensate safety switch that interrupts the cooling call. Some installs wire this switch so it kills only the outdoor unit.
5. **Thermostat setup or power issue.** Dead batteries, wrong mode, or wrong system-type configuration. In this case the fan is on manually, but there is no cooling call.
6. **Safety lockout on two-stage or variable-speed models.** High- or low-pressure switch trips, often from a dirty condenser coil or low refrigerant charge.
7. **Outdoor fan motor failure.** The compressor overheats and cycles off on its internal overload.
8. **Compressor failure.** Seized, grounded or open windings. This is the least common cause and the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Thermostat.** Set it to COOL, with the setpoint at least 3°F below room temperature and the fan on AUTO. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank. Wait 5 minutes, because many thermostats and boards have a compressor delay.
2. **Breakers.** Find the outdoor AC breaker (usually a double-pole 240V breaker) and the furnace or air-handler breaker. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
3. **Outdoor disconnect.** Make sure the disconnect box next to the unit is fully seated or switched ON. Do not open the box beyond the pull-out handle, and do not test fuses inside it.
4. **Air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter won't stop the outdoor unit, but it worsens a borderline system.
5. **Condensate line and drain pan.** If the drain pan under the indoor unit has water in it, or the drain line is clogged, a float switch may be cutting the cooling call. Clear the line at its accessible cleanout, for example with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain end. Then give the system a few minutes to resume.
6. **Vents and outdoor clearance.** Open the supply registers. Clear leaves and debris from around the outdoor unit, with at least 2 feet of clearance. Don't remove panels.
7. **One reset.** Turn the thermostat OFF, switch off both breakers for 5 minutes, then restore power and call for cooling. If the outdoor unit still won't run, or only hums, **turn the system off and call a technician.** Don't keep retrying.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Confirm the call.** The tech checks for 24V across the contactor coil at the outdoor unit and for 240V line voltage at the disconnect and the contactor.
- **No 24V at the outdoor unit:** they trace the thermostat wiring, the indoor board fuse, the float switch, and any pressure switches in series.
- **24V present but the contactor won't pull in:** they replace the contactor.
- **Contactor pulled in, but the unit hums or won't start:** they discharge and test the capacitor with a microfarad meter. A reading more than about 6% below the rated value counts as failed.
- **Motor and compressor tests:** they check the fan motor and compressor windings for resistance and shorts to ground, and check the amp draw on startup.
- **Board diagnostics:** on two-stage and variable-speed models, they read the outdoor board's flash codes or fault history.
- **Refrigerant pressures:** if pressure trips are suspected, they connect gauges. Low charge means a leak search, not just a top-off.

A good quote names the specific failed part and the test reading that proved it. Be wary of "you need a new compressor" without winding or amperage readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor unit completely silent | Tripped breaker or disconnect off | Reset breaker once; seat disconnect | Test line voltage, disconnect fuses, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums or clicks, nothing spins | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't retry | Test and replace capacitor |
| Silent, but breaker and disconnect are fine | Contactor or no 24V call | Check thermostat mode and batteries | Test contactor coil, indoor board fuse, control wiring |
| Water in drain pan, AC won't start | Float switch tripped | Clear condensate line | Verify switch, fix drain slope or pump |
| Compressor runs, outdoor fan doesn't, then shuts off | Fan motor or its capacitor section | Turn off to protect compressor | Replace fan motor or capacitor |
| Starts, then stops; repeats or locks out | Pressure switch trip (dirty coil or low charge) | Clear debris; one power reset | Clean coil, check pressures, leak search |
| Breaker trips immediately on start | Shorted compressor or wiring | Don't reset again | Megohm and winding tests, compressor diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| DIY checks (thermostat, breaker, filter, drain) | $0–$40 |
| Service call / diagnostic | $80–$200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120–$350 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Indoor board fuse or control wiring repair | $100–$300 |
| Float switch or condensate repair | $100–$300 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350–$800 |
| Outdoor control board (two-stage/variable-speed) | $400–$1,200 |
| Leak repair plus refrigerant recharge | $400–$1,500+ |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,500+ |

On older units, a failed compressor often makes full system replacement the better value. Check your registered warranty first: many American Standard compressors carry a 10–12 year parts warranty, but labor is usually extra.

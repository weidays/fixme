## Do this first

**If your CO alarm is going off, do these steps first, before reading anything else:**

1. **Get everyone outside now, including pets.** If anyone has a headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion or sleepiness, call **911**.
2. **Turn the thermostat to OFF on your way out** if you can do it in a few seconds. Don't stay inside to investigate.
3. **Leave doors open behind you** to ventilate if that's safe.
4. **From outside, call your gas utility's emergency line or the fire department.** They can measure CO levels and find the source.
5. **Do not go back in, and do not restart the York furnace,** until responders or a licensed HVAC technician say it is safe.

## What a CO alarm means

A CO alarm is not a York diagnostic code. It means your detector has measured carbon monoxide building up over time.

A gas furnace normally makes a small amount of CO in its flue gases. The venting system carries that gas safely outdoors. An alarm means one of two things has happened:

- Combustion has gone bad, so the furnace is making much more CO than normal.
- Flue gases are escaping into the house instead of going out the vent.

Often both happen at once.

York control boards have no CO sensor. The furnace may keep running with no flashing LED, so you can't rule out the furnace just because the board looks normal. Other appliances can also cause the alarm, including a water heater, range, attached garage, generator or fireplace. Responders should check all of them.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Blocked or disconnected venting.** Possible blockages include:
   - snow, ice or debris over 90%+ PVC terminations
   - bird or animal nests in a chimney
   - a separated or sagging flue pipe joint
   - an 80% unit sharing a chimney liner that is too large or deteriorated

   This is the most common furnace cause of CO spillage.
2. **Poor combustion from incorrect burner operation.** Typical sources are dirty or misaligned burners, wrong gas pressure, an incorrect orifice size (for example, after a propane conversion was done wrong) or debris in the burner box. These problems produce excess CO even when the venting works.
3. **Cracked or failed heat exchanger.** Cracks most often show up on older 80% York units and on furnaces that have overheated for years from airflow restrictions. They can let flue gases mix with the household air. Flame rollout or burner flames that change when the blower starts are warning signs.
4. **Inducer motor or pressure-switch problems that let venting fail.** A weak inducer can fail to move flue gas properly. In normal operation, the pressure switch shuts the furnace down. A technician has to confirm that this safety works.
5. **Back-drafting caused by depressurization.** Exhaust fans, a dryer or a fireplace can pull flue gases back down an atmospheric chimney. This mainly affects 80% furnaces and water heaters that vent into the same chimney.
6. **A non-furnace source or a faulty detector.** Examples are a car warming up in an attached garage, a gas range or a generator. Detectors also wear out, typically after 5–10 years, and can alarm when they shouldn't. Only rely on this explanation after a professional has tested the air.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

**While you are waiting outside for responders or a callback,** stay outside and do only these two things. Do not go back into the house or an attached garage for any reason.

- **Look at the outdoor vent terminations.** On 90%+ York furnaces, look at the two PVC pipes on the wall or roof for snow, ice, leaves or a visible nest over the openings. On 80% units, note anything visibly wrong at the chimney top if you can see it from the ground. Just look and note what you see. Tell responders.
- **Check for a running car or generator from outside.** Is a car running in the garage? Is a generator running near a window or door? Check without entering the house or garage, and tell responders.

**Only after responders have cleared the home** and said it is safe to go back in, you can do these checks:

- **Clear the outdoor vent terminations.** Clear snow, ice, leaves or a visible nest away from the PVC openings by hand. **Do not restart the furnace afterward.** Tell the technician what you found.
- **Check the detector itself.** Note its manufacture date or end-of-life date, and whether it gave a full alarm pattern or just a chirp. Replace batteries and replace any unit past its life. Do this in addition to the professional check, never instead of it.
- **Check the air filter.** A badly clogged filter causes overheating, and long-term overheating stresses the heat exchanger. Replace it if it's dirty, but leave the furnace off.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it stays on OFF until a professional clears the furnace.
- **Check for other indoor sources.** Was a gas oven or range on? Tell the technician.

Do not do any of the following:

- remove furnace panels
- look into the burner area with the unit running
- reset the furnace "to see if it happens again"

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician or utility responder should do the following:

- **Measure ambient CO** throughout the house with a calibrated meter, at each gas appliance.
- **Inspect the full vent path.** That covers:
  - flue pipe joints and slope
  - the chimney liner or PVC runs
  - the terminations
  - condensate drainage in 90%+ units, because water in the vent can restrict flow
- **Run a combustion analysis.** They will test flue CO, O2 and draft at the flue test port. Air-free CO well above the manufacturer's guidance (many techs use about 100–400 ppm air-free as an action threshold) points to a combustion problem.
- **Check gas pressure and burners.** They will confirm manifold pressure matches the York rating plate and confirm the orifices suit the fuel type. They will also inspect and clean the burners.
- **Inspect the heat exchanger.** Methods include a camera, watching the flame for disturbance when the blower starts, and checking CO at the supply registers.
- **Test the inducer and pressure switch.** They will also check the rollout and limit switches and confirm each one shuts the furnace down as designed.
- **Run a worst-case depressurization test.** This applies to atmospheric-vented systems, with exhaust fans and the dryer running.

Be cautious of anyone who calls the heat exchanger "cracked" without showing you photos or test readings. Ask for evidence, and get a second opinion before paying for a furnace replacement.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alarm during or after snowfall, 90%+ furnace | Iced or buried PVC terminations | Evacuate; after responders clear the home, clear snow from outdoor pipes; leave furnace off | Confirm vent clearance, test CO, check condensate in vent |
| Alarm plus soot or a rusty stain near the flue pipe | Disconnected or corroded flue, chimney blockage | Evacuate; call utility | Reseal or replace flue pipe, clear or reline chimney |
| Alarm plus headaches that ease when you leave home | Ongoing CO leak from furnace or other appliance | Evacuate; call 911 if symptomatic | Full CO survey and combustion analysis |
| Flames lift, wave or roll out when blower starts | Cracked heat exchanger | Evacuate; keep furnace off | Heat exchanger inspection, replace exchanger or furnace |
| Yellow, lazy flames or soot inside cabinet that you already noticed through the sight glass before the alarm | Poor combustion, wrong gas pressure or orifices | Keep furnace off; report what you saw. This is an observation to report, not a check to perform, so don't run the furnace to look | Clean burners, set manifold pressure, correct orifices |
| Alarm only when exhaust fans or dryer run | Back-drafting of 80% furnace or water heater | Keep furnace off until tested | Depressurization test, makeup air, vent corrections |
| Alarm with 3 flashes (pressure switch on most current York boards; check the legend on your unit's panel) or pressure-switch lockout | Restricted vent or weak inducer | One reset at most only after professional clearance — not before | Clear vent, test or replace inducer and pressure switch |
| Single chirp, old detector | Low battery or end-of-life detector | Replace batteries or detector; still have CO tested | Verify with calibrated meter |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including labor:

| Item | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Utility or fire department CO check | Usually free |
| HVAC diagnostic and combustion analysis | $100–$300 (emergency or after-hours visits $150–$450) |
| Replacement CO detector (DIY) | $25–$60 per unit |
| Clear blocked vent termination or reseal flue joints | $100–$350 |
| Replace sections of flue pipe | $200–$600 |
| Chimney cleaning or nest removal | $150–$400 |
| Chimney liner for orphaned water heater or 80% furnace | $800–$2,500 |
| Burner cleaning and gas pressure adjustment | $150–$400 |
| Correct orifice or propane conversion kit | $150–$400 |
| Inducer motor replacement | $400–$1,200 |
| Pressure switch replacement | $150–$350 |
| Heat exchanger replacement | $1,500–$3,500 (parts may be covered by York's limited heat exchanger warranty; labor usually isn't) |
| Full furnace replacement | $3,000–$7,500+ |

If your furnace is older than about 15 years and has a confirmed cracked heat exchanger, replacing the furnace is often the better value.

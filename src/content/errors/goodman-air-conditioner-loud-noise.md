## What this code means

"Loud noise" is not a fault code. Goodman single-stage air conditioners have no diagnostic display for it. You identify the problem by the type of sound and by where it comes from: the outdoor condenser or the indoor air handler/furnace.

Some variable-speed communicating models (ComfortBridge, such as GSXV9) can store faults on the outdoor board. A technician can read those faults, and they sometimes go along with an abnormal noise.

Noise problems don't "reset." The sound will continue or get worse until the worn or loose part is fixed. This page covers air-conditioner noises in cooling mode. Heat pump noises during defrost, including whooshing and reversing-valve sounds, are covered on the heat pump noise page.

Common sound types and where they point:

- **Buzzing or humming, with nothing starting:** electrical parts such as the capacitor, contactor or a motor struggling to start.
- **Screeching or squealing:** worn motor bearings. On older belt-drive blowers, a worn belt.
- **Rattling or clanking:** loose panels, debris, a loose fan blade or loose mounting hardware.
- **Grinding:** failing motor bearings, or a fan blade hitting something.
- **Banging or loud knocking at startup:** possible internal compressor damage.
- **Hissing or gurgling:** air or refrigerant movement. A loud, constant hiss can mean a leak or a restriction.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

This ranking reflects loud-noise complaints overall. The most likely cause depends on the type of sound. A buzz or hum points first to the capacitor or contactor. A rattle or tick points first to debris or loose hardware.

1. **Failing or weak run capacitor.** This produces a buzz or hum from the outdoor unit while the fan or compressor struggles to start. It is one of the most common Goodman condenser service calls.
2. **Worn outdoor fan motor bearings.** These cause a squeal, a grind or a rough hum. Often the fan spins slowly or wobbles.
3. **Debris or loose hardware in the condenser.** Sticks, leaves or a loose fan guard or top grille cause rattling or ticking. A fan blade loose on its shaft causes clanking.
4. **Pitted or chattering contactor.** This sounds like a loud buzz or rapid chatter from the electrical panel, sometimes with the unit trying to start.
5. **Loose cabinet panels or refrigerant lines vibrating.** You hear a rattle or drone that changes with the compressor cycle. Lines touching the cabinet or the house can transmit vibration indoors.
6. **Indoor blower problems.** These include a worn blower motor, a loose or unbalanced blower wheel, or debris in the wheel. You hear squealing or rattling from the air handler or furnace. The filter and ducts can also whistle when airflow is restricted.
7. **Compressor problems.** Hard starting causes a loud hum or clunk. Internal wear causes banging or grinding. Failing internal mounts cause clanking. A failing start component may be involved. This is the least common cause but the most expensive.
8. **Refrigerant issues.** A loud hissing or gurgling can come from a leak or a restriction. Only an EPA-certified technician can diagnose this.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the system off at the thermostat** if you hear grinding, screeching, banging or a buzz with no fan movement. Running a struggling motor or compressor makes damage worse.
- **Pin down where the sound comes from.** Is it the outdoor unit or the indoor unit? Does it happen at startup, during the run or at shutdown? A short phone video helps the technician a lot.
- **Check the air filter.** A clogged filter can cause whistling at the return or the registers, and it strains the blower. Replace it if it's dirty.
- **Check the vents and registers.** Make sure supply registers are open and returns aren't blocked by furniture. Mostly closed registers can whistle.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from outside, without opening anything.** Clear leaves, sticks and yard debris from around the cabinet. Keep about 2 feet of clearance. Don't reach through the fan grille.
- **Make sure exterior access panels are seated and their screws are snug.** A loose service panel can rattle. Don't remove any panel or open the electrical compartment.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it isn't calling for cooling repeatedly in short bursts, and replace weak batteries.
- **If the system is off or tripped, reset the breaker once.** If it trips again, stop and see the breaker page.

## How a technician will diagnose it

1. Ask when and where the noise occurs. Then listen with the unit running, and use a stethoscope or screwdriver probe on the motors and the compressor.
2. Shut off power, verify it's dead, and inspect the electrical panel. They'll look for burnt or pitted contactor contacts and loose wiring.
3. Test the run capacitor with a meter (µF reading) against the rating printed on it. A reading outside the tolerance printed on the capacitor (typically ±5–6%) means replace it. A bulging capacitor is replaced on sight.
4. Spin the condenser fan by hand with the power off. They're checking for bearing drag, wobble, a blade loose on the shaft, or a bent blade.
5. Measure amperage on the fan motor and the compressor and compare it to the nameplate. High draw or locked-rotor amps point to a failing motor or a hard-starting compressor.
6. Inspect the compressor mounts, the line set for rubbing, and the cabinet fasteners.
7. If a refrigerant issue is suspected, check pressures, superheat and subcooling, and do a leak search. This requires EPA 608 certification.
8. Indoors, check the blower wheel, the motor bearings and the mounting, and look for debris. On ComfortBridge systems, they'll also read stored faults.

**Sanity check for your quote:** a quote that jumps straight to "replace the compressor" with no amp readings and no capacitor test deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor buzz, fan not spinning | Failed run capacitor or seized fan motor | Turn the system off at the thermostat | Test and replace the capacitor or fan motor |
| Squeal or grind from the outdoor fan | Worn fan motor bearings | Turn off; clear debris around the unit | Replace the condenser fan motor (and the capacitor if weak) |
| Rattling or ticking outside | Debris, loose grille or loose panel | Clear yard debris; check that exterior panels are seated | Remove internal debris; secure the fan guard and hardware |
| Clanking or metal-on-metal sound | Loose or bent fan blade | Turn off immediately | Tighten or replace the blade; check the motor shaft |
| Loud chatter or buzz at startup | Pitted contactor or low control voltage | Turn off; check thermostat batteries | Replace the contactor; check the control wiring and transformer |
| Loud hum or clunk, compressor struggles | Hard-start issue or a failing compressor | Turn off; don't keep resetting | Amp test; capacitor or hard-start kit; evaluate the compressor |
| Banging inside the condenser | Broken compressor mounts or internal compressor damage | Turn off | Diagnose the compressor; repair the mounts or replace the compressor |
| Whistling at the vents or return | Dirty filter or closed registers | Replace the filter; open the registers | Evaluate the ductwork or return sizing |
| Squeal or rattle from the indoor unit | Blower motor bearings or a loose or dirty blower wheel | Check the filter; turn off if it's loud | Clean or rebalance the wheel; replace the blower motor |
| Loud, constant hiss or gurgle | Refrigerant leak or restriction | Turn off | Leak search, repair and recharge (EPA-certified only) |

## Repair costs

These are typical US costs, parts plus labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $75 – $200 |
| Debris removal, tighten panels or fan guard | $100 – $200 (often just the diagnostic fee) |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120 – $350 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Condenser fan blade replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Condenser fan motor replacement | $350 – $750 (more for ECM or variable-speed motors) |
| Hard-start kit | $150 – $400 |
| Indoor blower wheel cleaning or rebalancing | $150 – $400 |
| Indoor blower motor replacement | $400 – $1,800 (variable-speed ECM motors run toward the high end) |
| Refrigerant leak search and repair | $250 – $1,500+, depending on location and refrigerant type |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500 – $3,500+ (out-of-warranty replacements commonly reach $3,500 or more once refrigerant and labor are included; a part covered under warranty can greatly reduce the cost, but labor and refrigerant are usually extra) |

If the unit is out of warranty, 12 or more years old, and needs a compressor, many homeowners compare that cost against a full condenser replacement.

## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a numbered fault code. It is a behavior: the air conditioner starts, runs only briefly, shuts off, and then restarts. On a warm day a healthy system usually runs for at least 10–15 minutes per cycle.

How you notice it depends on the equipment:

- **Conventional 24V systems** (most Silver and Gold models with a standard thermostat) give no error indication. You will simply see the system cycling too often.
- **Communicating systems** (AccuLink or Link-capable Platinum and some Gold models) may log related events in the outdoor control or on the thermostat. These can include pressure-switch trips or compressor protection trips.

Whether the system recovers on its own depends on what is causing the shutdown:

- **Thermostat-driven cycling** simply repeats.
- **A tripped pressure switch or internal compressor overload** will usually allow a restart after a delay or cooldown.
- **Some communicating controls** hold a lockout after repeated trips. These need a power cycle or a service reset.

Exact lockout logic varies by model and control board. Check the label inside the unit's service panel or your installation manual. If you can't confirm it, let a technician do so.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat issues.** Weak batteries, a thermostat mounted near a supply register, sunlight or a heat source, or the wrong cycle-rate or equipment settings.
2. **Dirty air filter or restricted airflow.** Low airflow can trip the low-pressure or freeze protection, or cause rapid cycling on its own.
3. **Low refrigerant charge (a leak).** This causes low-pressure switch trips, which show up as short runs followed by restarts.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil or high head pressure.** This trips the high-pressure switch or the compressor's internal overload.
5. **Failing electrical components.** A weak run capacitor or a worn contactor can cause the compressor to start, overheat, and trip on its internal overload.
6. **Control board or wiring faults.** Loose low-voltage connections or a faulty board send intermittent call signals.
7. **Oversized equipment.** The unit satisfies the thermostat too quickly. This is common after a home has been renovated or insulated.

Related airflow and frozen-coil problems are covered on their own pages and are only summarized here.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL with the fan on AUTO. Replace the batteries. Make sure the thermostat isn't in direct sun, near a lamp, or in the airflow from a supply vent.
- **Air filter:** Pull the filter and replace it if it's dirty. Note the size printed on the frame.
- **Vents and registers:** Open all supply registers. Clear furniture and rugs from the return grilles.
- **Breaker or disconnect:** If the system is acting erratically, turn the thermostat off. Then reset the AC breaker and the outdoor disconnect **once**. Wait 5–10 minutes before calling for cooling again.
- **Condensate:** Check the indoor drain line and pan, if they're visible, for standing water. A float switch tripped by a clogged drain can shut the system down repeatedly. You may clear a clogged line at its accessible cleanout or end.
- **Outdoor unit (look only):** Hose off loose debris and clear plants within about 2 feet of the outdoor unit. Don't open the panels.
- **Exterior panels:** Make sure any panel you can see is fully seated.

If it still short cycles after these checks, stop resetting it and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician will usually:

- Ask how long the system runs and how long it stays off. They should also check the thermostat's cycle-rate and installer settings.
- Read any stored faults on communicating AccuLink or Link systems.
- Measure the superheat and subcooling with gauges to check the refrigerant charge. If the system is low, they should propose a leak search, not just a top-off.
- Test the run capacitor (µF), the contactor, and the compressor amperage. They should also check for overload trips.
- Check the pressure switches, the low-voltage wiring, and the control board's inputs.
- Measure static pressure and airflow, and inspect the condenser and evaporator coils.
- Run a load calculation if they suspect oversizing.

Be wary of a quote that recommends a new compressor or a whole new system without capacitor, contactor and charge readings to back it up.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cycles every few minutes, and the house never feels dry | Thermostat placement or settings, or an oversized unit | Replace batteries; remove heat sources near the thermostat; check the settings | Relocate or reconfigure the thermostat; perform a load calculation |
| Short runs, a weak airflow, and a dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Replace the filter and open the registers | Measure static pressure; inspect the blower and coil |
| Runs briefly, stops, and retries | Low refrigerant charge tripping the low-pressure switch | None beyond the basic checks | Find and repair the leak, then charge the system to spec |
| Outdoor unit stops on hot days and restarts later | Dirty condenser, high pressure, or compressor overload | Clear debris around the outdoor unit | Clean the coil; test the fan, capacitor and pressures |
| Compressor clicks on, then off, and hums | Weak capacitor or failing contactor | None | Test and replace the capacitor or contactor |
| System shuts down and you find water near the indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain tripping the float switch | Clear the drain line at the accessible cleanout | Clean the drain pan; check the float switch |
| Erratic starts with no pattern | Loose wiring or a faulty control board | Reset the breaker once | Inspect the wiring; diagnose or replace the board |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges, and they vary by region and model:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Filter or batteries (DIY) | $0–$40 |
| Thermostat reconfiguration or relocation | $100–$350 |
| Smart or new thermostat | $100–$350 installed. AccuLink communicating controls cost more, often $400–$900. |
| Service call and diagnosis | $90–$200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Leak search and repair, plus refrigerant | $300–$1,500+, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A or R-454B) |
| Pressure switch replacement | $200–$450 |
| Control board replacement | $300–$800 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$2,500+ (if in warranty, often labor only) |
| Replacing an oversized system | $5,000–$12,000+ |

## What this code means

An **ignition lockout** on a Carrier furnace means the control board tried to light the burners the number of times allowed by the control and never confirmed a stable flame. To keep unburned gas from building up inside the heat exchanger, the board shuts down the gas valve and stops trying — this is the "lockout."

Depending on your board generation, this shows up as a specific flash code. On many Carrier boards it appears as **Code 14** (ignition lockout) or **Code 34** (ignition proving failure / flame not sensed). Carrier documents Code 14 as a lockout after **four attempts to ignite**: the first failed attempt sets Code 34, the control retries three more times, and then it locks out. See those dedicated pages for the exact flash sequence on your model.

**Reset behavior varies by board:** Carrier's published status-code chart for Code 14 states the control **auto-resets after three hours**, and you can also clear the lockout immediately by cycling power. Auto-reset timing varies by board generation — see your unit's status-code label. Either way, a lockout that keeps returning is not "fixed" by resetting — something is preventing a clean, proven flame and needs a technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or failed flame sensor** — the most common cause. The furnace lights, but the sensor can't detect the flame, so the board shuts off and eventually locks out.
2. **Weak, cracked, or failed hot-surface igniter** — the igniter doesn't get hot enough (or at all) to light the gas.
3. **Insufficient gas supply** — partially closed manual valve, gas regulator issues, or low pressure, so the burners can't light or stay lit.
4. **Faulty gas valve** — the valve doesn't open reliably to feed the burners.
5. **Dirty or misaligned burners** — corrosion, rust, or debris disrupting a clean burner flame.
6. **Grounding or wiring problems** — flame sensing needs a good ground; poor grounding mimics a "no flame" condition.
7. **Control board fault** — less common, but a failing board can misread flame or mis-sequence ignition.

Carrier's own troubleshooting flow generally has technicians confirm the igniter and flame-sensing circuit first, then gas supply and the valve. Almost all of these are inside-the-cabinet repairs.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Stick to these — they're the only steps that don't require opening the sealed burner compartment:

- **Thermostat:** confirm it's set to **Heat** and the setpoint is above room temperature. Replace the **batteries** if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** a severely clogged filter can cause airflow-related shutdowns. Replace it if it's dirty.
- **Breaker / furnace switch:** make sure the furnace breaker is on and the service switch (looks like a light switch near the unit) is on.
- **Visible vents and registers:** confirm supply and return registers are open and unobstructed.
- **Condensate line (high-efficiency models):** limit yourself to the **exterior drain line and drain pan**. If the visible drain line is clogged and backing up, clear the obvious blockage, or empty a full pan. **Do not remove the inducer or open the burner compartment to reach the trap** — that's technician work.
- **One reset only:** turn the furnace off at the switch/breaker for 30 seconds, then back on to attempt one clean start. **If it locks out again, stop.** Do not keep resetting — that can allow gas to accumulate.

If it locks out a second time, the problem is inside the cabinet and needs a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

- Read the stored flash code and fault history from the control board.
- Watch a full ignition sequence to see exactly where it fails (igniter glow, gas valve open, flame present, flame proven).
- Measure the **hot-surface igniter** resistance and inspect it for cracks.
- Check **flame sensor microamps** with a meter and clean or replace the sensor.
- Verify **gas supply and manifold pressure** at the valve.
- Inspect and clean **burners** and confirm proper ground.
- Test the **gas valve** operation and the control board outputs.

Sanity check: any quote should identify *which* part failed. A blanket "replace the board" without testing the igniter and flame sensor first is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Furnace lights briefly, then shuts off and locks out | Dirty/failed flame sensor | One reset; replace filter | Clean or replace flame sensor, verify microamps |
| No glow, no ignition attempt at burners | Failed hot-surface igniter | One reset only | Test and replace igniter |
| Clicks/tries but no flame | Low or no gas supply / faulty gas valve | Confirm other gas appliances work | Test gas pressure, inspect/replace valve |
| Weak or uneven flame, then lockout | Dirty or misaligned burners | None | Clean and align burners |
| Intermittent lockouts, otherwise runs | Poor ground / wiring | None | Inspect wiring and ground connections |
| Lockout with no clear cause after testing | Control board fault | None | Test board outputs, replace if confirmed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **One-time reset (no parts):** $0 DIY
- **Flame sensor clean or replacement:** $80 – $250
- **Hot-surface igniter replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Burner cleaning / adjustment:** $100 – $300
- **Gas valve replacement:** $350 – $800
- **Control board replacement:** $400 – $1,200
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often applied to the repair)

Prices vary by region, model, and parts availability. High-efficiency and Infinity-series variable-speed boards sit at the top of that board range.

## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is a symptom, not a numbered Trane fault code. The indoor coil sits above your furnace or inside your air handler. Normally it runs around 40°F, above freezing. If too little warm air crosses it, or the refrigerant pressure drops too low, moisture on the coil freezes. The ice then blocks even more airflow, and the problem feeds on itself.

Signs you'll notice:

- Weak or warm airflow from the vents
- Frost on the copper suction line at the outdoor unit
- Water around the indoor unit while the ice melts
- An AC that runs constantly without cooling

Most standard Trane condensers have no diagnostic display for this condition. Some communicating systems, such as the XV-series with a ComfortLink II thermostat, may show low-pressure or airflow alerts, and what they show varies by model.

The condition does not latch. Once the ice melts, the system will try to run again. If you don't fix the cause, it will refreeze.

If your system is a heat pump with ice on the outdoor unit, that is a different issue. See the heat pump pages listed under Related codes.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter.** This is the most common cause. Restricted return air lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Closed or blocked supply registers or return grilles.** Furniture, rugs, or many closed vents reduce total airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil.** Dust and biofilm on the coil insulate it and choke airflow. It is common when filters have been skipped or are poorly fitted.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. Coil leaks of any design are a known cause of low charge, and copper-tube indoor coils can develop pinhole leaks from formicary corrosion.
5. **Blower problems.**
   - A failing blower motor or capacitor
   - A dirty blower wheel
   - A fan speed tap set too low for cooling
   - On variable-speed (ECM) air handlers, incorrect airflow settings or a failing motor module
6. **Metering device restriction.** A clogged or failing TXV or piston, or a restricted filter-drier, starves the coil.
7. **Running at low outdoor temperatures.** Running the AC below about 60–65°F outdoors can freeze the coil, especially on systems without low-ambient controls.
8. **Thermostat fan or control issues.** A thermostat or control fault that runs the compressor without the indoor blower causes rapid icing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Stop cooling and thaw.**
   - Set the thermostat to **Off** and the fan to **On**. This melts the ice faster and protects the compressor.
   - Do not chip or scrape ice off the coil.
2. **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it is gray, clogged, or sagging. Use the correct size, with the airflow arrow pointing toward the blower.
3. **Open the vents.** Open all supply registers and clear the return grilles of furniture, drapes, and rugs.
4. **Watch the condensate drain.** As the ice melts, make sure water flows out the drain line and the pan isn't overflowing.
   - If your system has a clear condensate trap or an accessible drain cleanout, you can flush the line with a cup of water or distilled vinegar.
   - A float switch may have shut the system off if the pan filled.
5. **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it is set to Cool, not set extremely low, and has fresh batteries.
   - Fan "Auto" is normal. Make sure the indoor blower actually starts when cooling runs.
6. **Check breakers and the service switch.** Confirm the furnace or air handler breaker and its service switch are on. If the indoor blower has no power while the outdoor unit runs, the coil will freeze fast.
7. **Seat the panels.** Make sure the indoor unit's blower door is fully closed and seated.
8. **Restart once fully thawed.** Run Cool and feel for strong, cold airflow.

**If the coil refreezes within a day, stop running the AC and call a technician.** That points to refrigerant, coil, or blower problems.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing these steps helps you judge whether a quote makes sense.

- **Measure airflow.** The tech checks static pressure and temperature split (typically 16–22°F across the coil). They inspect the blower wheel, motor, capacitor, and speed or airflow settings, including dip switches on Trane variable-speed air handlers.
- **Inspect the coil.** The tech looks at the coil face for dirt, often by removing an access panel or using a camera.
- **Read refrigerant pressures and temperatures.** Gauges or digital probes give suction and liquid pressures, superheat, and subcooling. These separate low charge from a restriction or a bad TXV, and from an airflow problem.
  - Low suction with high superheat suggests low charge or a restriction.
  - Low suction with low superheat typically points to low airflow across the coil.
  - A frosted spot at the drier or TXV points to a restriction.
- **Find the leak.** If the charge is low, the tech uses an electronic detector, bubble solution, or a nitrogen pressure test. Topping off refrigerant without finding the leak is a temporary fix, and you should question it.
- **Check controls.** The tech confirms the blower energizes with every cooling call and that the thermostat and control wiring are correct.
- **Check warranty.** Trane typically warranties coils for 10 years on registered equipment. Ask whether parts are covered. Labor usually isn't.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, ice on the coil, gray filter | Clogged air filter | Thaw with fan On, then replace the filter | None, unless it refreezes |
| Ice forms after vents were closed in unused rooms | Restricted airflow | Open registers and clear returns | Check duct sizing and static pressure |
| Refreezes even with a clean filter | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw and keep the filter fresh | Clean the coil chemically, and pull it if needed |
| Frost on the suction line, long runtimes, gradual loss of cooling | Low refrigerant from a leak | Shut off cooling and thaw | Find the leak, repair it, evacuate, and recharge, or replace the coil |
| Outdoor unit runs but no air comes from the vents | Blower motor, capacitor, or power fault | Check the indoor breaker, service switch, and blower door | Test and replace the motor, capacitor, or control |
| Ice only at one spot near the metering device | TXV, piston, or drier restriction | Shut off cooling | Replace the TXV, piston, or filter-drier and recharge |
| Freezes on cool nights or mild days | Low outdoor temperature operation | Avoid cooling below about 60°F outdoors | Add low-ambient controls if needed |
| Water on the floor while thawing | Meltwater overwhelming the drain | Clear or flush the condensate line and dry the area | Clear the drain, replace the float switch or pan |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges. Your price will depend on region, access, and warranty status.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter replacement | $5–$40 DIY |
| Service or diagnostic call | $90–$200 |
| Evaporator coil cleaning | $150–$500 (higher if the coil must be removed) |
| Blower capacitor | $120–$300 installed |
| PSC blower motor | $350–$700 |
| ECM / variable-speed motor or module | $600–$1,500 |
| Leak search | $150–$450 |
| Small leak repair (brazing, valve core, fitting) | $200–$800, plus refrigerant |
| R-410A or R-454B recharge | $150–$600, depending on pounds and current pricing |
| R-22 recharge (older systems) | Often $100+ per pound |
| TXV replacement | $400–$1,200 |
| Filter-drier replacement | $200–$500 with recovery and recharge |
| Evaporator coil replacement, under parts warranty | $800–$1,800 labor and refrigerant |
| Evaporator coil replacement, out of warranty | $1,500–$3,000+ |

If an older R-22 system needs a coil plus a large charge, compare the repair cost against replacing the system.

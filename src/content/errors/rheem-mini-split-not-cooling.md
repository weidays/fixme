## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single fault code on a Rheem mini-split. It's a symptom: the indoor head is on, but the room isn't getting cooler. Ductless systems fail differently from central AC. They have no ductwork or wall thermostat, and the compressor is inverter-driven, with control boards both indoors and outdoors that talk over a communication wire. Many "not cooling" cases come down to remote settings, filters behind the front panel, communication faults, or a variable-speed compressor that has throttled down to protect itself.

If you have a central split system, see the separate Rheem AC Not Cooling page. This page covers only ductless-specific causes.

Check the indoor display first. Many Rheem ductless heads show a code (letters and numbers such as E-, F-, P- or H- series) or blink a timer or operation LED when the "not cooling" is caused by a fault. The meanings differ by series and OEM, so look up any code in your own manual rather than a generic chart.

Reset behavior also varies. Many inverter protections (discharge temperature, overcurrent, voltage) retry automatically after about 3–5 minutes. Some communication and sensor faults hold until power is cycled.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Wrong mode or setpoint on the remote.** The unit is set to Fan, Dry, Auto or Heat, the setpoint is above room temperature, or a timer or sleep function is active. Dead remote batteries can also mean your changes never reached the head.
2. **Clogged washable filters.** Mini-split mesh filters clog fast, often within weeks in homes with pets. Restricted airflow sharply cuts cooling and can freeze the coil.
3. **Dirty indoor coil or blower wheel.** Dust and mold build up on the squirrel-cage wheel and fins, and filter cleaning alone won't fix it.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** Flare connections at the indoor and outdoor units are the classic leak points. Symptoms include weak cooling, frost at the outdoor service valves, or a low-pressure or protection code.
5. **Blocked or dirty outdoor unit.** Debris, vegetation or a clogged condenser coil triggers high-pressure or high-discharge-temperature protection, which slows or stops the compressor.
6. **Multi-zone mode conflict.** On multi-zone systems, one head set to Heat while others call for Cool.
7. **Communication fault between indoor and outdoor units.** Damaged or loose interconnect wiring, or wiring that doesn't meet spec, usually shows a comm code. The head blows air, but the outdoor unit never starts.
8. **Outdoor inverter board, sensor or fan motor failure.** Failed thermistors (coil, discharge, ambient), the inverter/IPM board, the outdoor fan motor, or the electronic expansion valve.
9. **Inverter compressor failure.** This is the least common cause and is usually confirmed only after the board and sensors test good.
10. **Undersized head or extreme heat load.** A unit running flat out on a 100°F day in a sunny room may not reach the setpoint. Rule this out before paying for repairs.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Remote:** Set the mode to Cool (the snowflake icon) and the setpoint at least 3–4°F below room temperature, with fan on Auto or High. Cancel any timers and sleep or eco modes. Replace the remote batteries and confirm the head beeps when you press a button.
- **Multi-zone:** Set every indoor head to Cool, not just the one that isn't cooling.
- **Filters:** Lift the front panel as your owner's manual describes, slide out the mesh filters, rinse them with lukewarm water, let them dry fully, and reinstall. Don't run the unit without filters.
- **Breaker or disconnect:** Make sure the breaker and the outdoor disconnect switch are on. If the unit shows a fault code, you may cycle power **once**: turn the breaker off for about 5 minutes, then back on, and give the inverter up to 10 minutes to start. If the code returns, stop and call a technician.
- **Airflow:** Make sure the louvers swing open and furniture or curtains aren't blocking the head. Clear leaves, snow or plants from about 2 feet around the outdoor unit, from the outside only.
- **Condensate:** Check that the drain line outlet outside isn't crushed or clogged. If you have a condensate pump, make sure it's plugged in and not alarming. Water dripping from the head can mean a blocked drain or a frozen coil.

Don't open the outdoor cabinet, remove the indoor chassis, spray coil cleaners inside the head, or touch the refrigerant service valves.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- Read and record the fault code or blink pattern, plus any stored history. Some models have a check or self-diagnostic function on the remote or board.
- Measure supply and return air temperature at the head. A healthy unit usually shows about a 15–25°F drop in Cool, depending on model and speed.
- Inspect the blower wheel and evaporator coil, and check fan speed.
- Check line voltage at the outdoor unit and the communication signal between units. Inspect the interconnect wiring for damage or the wrong gauge.
- Test thermistors against the manufacturer's resistance and temperature chart, and check the outdoor fan motor, the electronic expansion valve and inverter board outputs.
- Connect gauges to read pressures. Because inverter systems vary compressor speed, techs often force test or max-run mode for an accurate reading.
- If the system is low on charge: leak search with an electronic detector or bubble solution at the flares, repair, pressure test with nitrogen, pull a vacuum to about 500 microns, and weigh in the factory charge from the nameplate. Be wary of any quote for "add refrigerant" with no leak repair.
- Test compressor winding resistance only after the board and sensors check out.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan blows, room air temp, no code | Wrong mode, timer, dead remote | Set Cool, lower setpoint, new batteries | None if that fixes it |
| Weak airflow, slow cooling | Clogged filters | Wash and dry filters | Clean coil and blower wheel if still weak |
| Musty smell, dark specks from louvers | Dirty blower wheel or coil | Clean filters only | Full indoor deep clean |
| One zone won't cool, others fine | Mode conflict, or that zone's sensor or EEV | Set all heads to Cool | Test zone thermistor, EEV, wiring |
| Outdoor unit never starts, comm code | Interconnect wiring or board fault | Cycle power once | Check comm signal, wiring, boards |
| Frost on outdoor service valves, weak cooling | Low refrigerant leak | Turn off, call | Leak repair, vacuum, weighed charge |
| Cools briefly then stops, protection code | Dirty or blocked outdoor coil, fan motor | Clear debris around the unit | Clean coil, test fan motor and sensors |
| Code returns after one reset | Sensor, inverter board or compressor | Stop resetting | Component diagnosis and replacement |
| Runs nonstop on hottest days, near setpoint | Undersized unit or heat load | Close blinds, check filters | Load calculation, consider added capacity |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic or service call | $100 – $250 |
| Professional indoor deep clean (coil and blower wheel) | $150 – $400 per head |
| Outdoor coil cleaning | $100 – $250 |
| Leak search and flare repair, with vacuum and recharge | $300 – $900 |
| Thermistor or sensor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Communication wiring repair or replacement | $150 – $600 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $300 – $700 |
| Electronic expansion valve or coil | $300 – $800 |
| Indoor control board | $250 – $650 |
| Outdoor inverter board | $500 – $1,200 |
| Inverter compressor replacement | $1,500 – $2,500+ (replacing the outdoor unit or whole system is often the better choice) |

Rheem ductless parts warranties commonly run 5–10 years with registration, but terms vary by series and installer. Check your coverage before paying for major parts, because labor often isn't covered.

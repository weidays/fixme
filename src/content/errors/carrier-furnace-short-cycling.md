## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single Carrier fault code—it's a *behavior*. Your furnace starts a heating cycle, runs briefly, shuts off, then restarts far too soon, sometimes only running for one to three minutes at a time. On many Carrier boards, the underlying problem will eventually store a numeric fault code (for example a limit-switch or flame-proving code) that you can read from the flashing LED on the control board.

Short cycling almost always means the furnace is protecting itself. The most common driver is **overheating**: restricted airflow makes the heat exchanger too hot, the high-limit switch cuts the burner, the blower keeps running to cool things down, and the cycle repeats. Other causes include a marginal flame sensor, a mis-located or failing thermostat, or a furnace that is simply oversized for the home.

Whether the furnace auto-resets or holds in lockout depends on the specific board and the fault it logs. A tripped high-limit typically auto-resets once temperatures fall; repeated flame-proving failures usually drive the control into a timed lockout. If your furnace has locked out completely, see the related Carrier ignition and flame-proving pages below.

**Stop immediately if:** you smell gas, smell burning, see flames outside the burner compartment, or a CO alarm sounds. Shut the furnace off at its switch, leave the house, and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. There is no DIY step in that situation.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the number-one cause. Restricted return airflow overheats the heat exchanger and trips the high-limit switch, cutting the burner mid-cycle.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers, blocked returns, or a collapsed duct starve airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Overheating / tripping high-limit switch** — caused by any airflow restriction, a weak or failing blower motor, or a dirty blower wheel. The limit repeatedly opens and closes.
4. **Dirty or weak flame sensor** — the control fails to reliably prove flame, shuts the gas valve, and reattempts, producing short, repeated burner runs.
5. **Thermostat problems** — dead batteries, a thermostat in direct sun or near a supply register, loose wiring, or the "cycles per hour" / anticipator setting configured wrong.
6. **Blocked or disconnected flue / clogged condensate trap (high-efficiency models)** — blocked, disconnected or water-logged venting, or a clogged condensate trap, trips the pressure switch intermittently.
7. **Oversized furnace** — a unit too large for the home satisfies the thermostat quickly and then cycles frequently. This is a design issue, not a broken part.
8. **Failing control board** — less common; the board mis-times cycles or misreads a safety input.

**Note on repeat high-limit trips:** if overheating faults keep coming back after airflow has been corrected, a cracked heat exchanger is possible, and that carries a carbon-monoxide risk. Ask your technician to inspect the heat exchanger in that case.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should perform:

- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This resolves a large share of short-cycling calls. Note the size printed on the old filter's frame.
- **Check the thermostat.** Set it to Heat and a few degrees above room temperature. Replace the batteries if it uses them. Make sure it isn't in direct sunlight or above a heat source.
- **Open and clear vents and registers.** Confirm supply registers throughout the house are open, and that return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs. Never close more than a couple of vents.
- **Look at the breaker.** If the furnace breaker is tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the condensate line (high-efficiency furnaces).** If you see standing water near the unit or a full drain pan, a clogged condensate line can trip a safety switch. Clearing a visible external clog is fine; internal work is not.
- **One reset only.** If the furnace is locked out, you may cycle the power (switch or breaker) once. Do not repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.

Anything inside the cabinet—flame sensor, limit switch, blower motor, pressure switch, control board, wiring—is technician work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

- **Read stored fault codes** from the control board's LED to see which safety input is tripping.
- **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace and compare it to the rating-plate spec. A rise above spec confirms an airflow/overheating problem.
- **Inspect and test the blower** motor, capacitor, and wheel for buildup or weakness that reduces airflow.
- **Test the high-limit switch** for proper opening/closing and check for dirty burners or a hot heat exchanger.
- **Inspect the heat exchanger** if high-limit trips repeat after airflow has been corrected—a crack is a carbon-monoxide hazard and needs to be ruled out.
- **Check the flame sensor** microamp signal and clean or replace it if the reading is weak.
- **Verify the pressure switch and flue** on high-efficiency units, along with the condensate trap.
- **Evaluate thermostat wiring and settings**, including cycle-rate configuration.
- **Assess sizing** if no component fault is found—an oversized furnace may need staging or airflow adjustments.

This sequence lets you sanity-check a quote: a good tech confirms *why* the furnace is cycling before replacing parts.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 2-3 min, blower keeps running, then restarts | Overheating from dirty filter | Replace filter; open vents | Test limit switch, measure temp rise, clean blower |
| Short burner runs, flame lights then dies | Weak/dirty flame sensor | None (cabinet work) | Clean or replace flame sensor, check microamps |
| Furnace cycles on and off rapidly | Oversized unit or thermostat setting | Check thermostat placement/batteries | Adjust cycle rate, evaluate sizing/staging |
| Cycling with poor airflow at vents | Blocked ducts/returns or weak blower | Open registers, clear returns | Inspect ductwork, test blower motor & capacitor |
| High-efficiency unit cycling, water near furnace | Clogged condensate / pressure switch trip | Clear visible external condensate clog | Clean trap, inspect flue, test pressure switch |
| Cycling plus stored fault code | Safety input tripping | Read code, one reset only | Diagnose per code, repair root cause |
| Random cycling, no clear pattern | Failing control board | None | Test and replace control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter (DIY):** $10 – $40
- **Thermostat batteries (DIY):** $5 – $15
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150 – $400 installed
- **Flame sensor clean/replace:** $80 – $250
- **High-limit switch replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150 – $350
- **Blower motor replacement:** $450 – $1,800 installed. The motor type drives the price: an older PSC blower motor sits at the low end, while the variable-speed ECM motor/module used on Infinity and most two-stage and modulating furnaces typically runs $900 – $1,800 installed. A four-figure ECM quote on one of these furnaces is normal, not a markup.
- **Pressure switch / condensate service:** $150 – $400
- **Control board replacement:** $400 – $1,200 installed. Boards for Infinity and variable-speed furnaces commonly land in the $700 – $1,200 range.
- **Duct/airflow correction:** $200 – $1,000+ depending on scope
- **Correcting an oversized furnace:** varies widely; may involve staging setup or, in rare cases, replacement

Diagnostic/service-call fees typically run $80 – $200 and are often credited toward the repair.

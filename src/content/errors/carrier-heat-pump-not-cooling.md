## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a stored fault code. On a Carrier heat pump in cooling mode, it means one of three things:

- The system runs but the house doesn't cool.
- Air from the vents is warm.
- The outdoor unit doesn't start at all.

The general AC side of this problem is covered on our Carrier AC Not Cooling page. This page focuses on what is specific to heat pumps:

- **The reversing valve.** It must shift for cooling. On Carrier systems, the thermostat's O terminal energizes it in cooling mode.
- **Thermostat O/B configuration.**
- **The defrost control board.**

Infinity and Greenspeed systems may show a stored fault on the Infinity thermostat or as an LED flash code on the outdoor control board. Standard single-stage units usually give no code. Reset behavior varies:

- Some outdoor boards hold a lockout (for example, high-pressure or low-pressure lockouts) until power is cycled.
- Others retry automatically after a delay.

Check your model's documentation, or let a technician read the board.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow.** Low airflow can freeze the indoor coil and stop cooling.
2. **Thermostat setup issues.** Common examples:
   - Mode not set to Cool.
   - O/B reversing valve setting wrong. Carrier uses O, energized in cooling.
   - Dead batteries.
   - A new thermostat installed with the wrong configuration.
3. **Tripped breaker or outdoor disconnect off.** The indoor fan runs, but the outdoor unit is silent.
4. **Failed run capacitor or contactor.** The outdoor fan or compressor won't start, or it hums. This is common on single-stage units.
5. **Reversing valve solenoid or valve failure.** The system runs but delivers warm air in Cool mode. This is specific to heat pumps.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** You may see long run times, ice on lines or the coil, and weak cooling. A high- or low-pressure switch lockout may follow.
7. **Dirty outdoor coil or failed outdoor fan motor.** These cause high head pressure and can trip the high-pressure switch.
8. **Defrost or outdoor control board fault.** A board can fail to release defrost mode or fail to power the compressor. Behavior varies by board.
9. **Compressor failure.** This is the least common cause and the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on Auto. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. If you see ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines, set the thermostat to Off with the fan On for a few hours to thaw it before restarting.
- **Breakers and disconnect.** Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers and the outdoor disconnect switch. Reset a tripped breaker **once only**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return registers are open and not blocked by furniture.
- **Outdoor unit clearance.** Clear leaves, grass and debris from around the unit. Do not open the unit.
- **Condensate line.** A clogged drain can trigger a float switch that shuts down cooling. Check for standing water in the pan or a tripped float switch, and clear the drain line opening if it's accessible.
- **Panels.** Make sure the indoor unit's access panels are fully seated.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- Read fault codes from the Infinity thermostat or the outdoor board LED, where your model has them.
- Verify the thermostat O/B setting. Check for 24V on Y and O during a cooling call.
- Measure the line-to-line voltage, test the capacitor (in µF), and inspect the contactor.
- Check the reversing valve by comparing line temperatures. They then test the solenoid coil for voltage and continuity.
- Connect gauges to measure superheat and subcooling against Carrier's charging chart. If the charge is low, they perform leak detection.
- Inspect the outdoor coil and fan motor, and check the high- and low-pressure switches.
- Check compressor amp draw and winding resistance before condemning the compressor.

A credible quote should name the failed part and the reading that proved it, such as a capacitor's µF reading or superheat and subcooling values. "Needs Freon" alone, with no leak search, is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air from vents, outdoor unit running | Reversing valve or solenoid, or O/B misconfiguration | Check the thermostat mode | Test the solenoid and valve; correct the O/B setting |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Breaker, disconnect, contactor, or board lockout | Reset the breaker once; check the disconnect | Test the contactor, board and 24V signal |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed capacitor or seized compressor | Turn the system off | Replace the capacitor; test the compressor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace the filter; thaw with the fan on | Check charge, find and repair the leak |
| Runs constantly, weak cooling | Low charge or dirty outdoor coil | Clear debris around the unit | Measure charge; clean the coil |
| System shuts off, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain or float switch | Clear the drain line | Clean the drain, check the pump or switch |

| Repair | Typical US cost |
|---|---|
| Filter, batteries, thermostat fix | $0–$50 DIY |
| Service call and diagnosis | $90–$200 |

| Contactor | $150–$350 |

## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a stored Lennox fault code. It describes a symptom: the heat pump starts, runs only a few minutes, shuts off, and then restarts soon after. It can happen in both heating and cooling modes.

On Lennox outdoor units, the control board enforces an anti-short-cycle delay of roughly 5 minutes between compressor starts. If your unit stops and restarts at about that interval, something is ending each call early. Common culprits are the thermostat, a safety pressure switch, or the board itself.

On communicating systems (iComfort, S30/S40), the thermostat often shows an alarm number alongside the cycling. That number is the most useful single clue to give a technician. Conventional 24 V systems may flash an LED code on the outdoor control board instead.

This page covers the heat pump specifically. Gas furnace cycling, such as limit or flame-sensor trips, is covered in the furnace short cycling page.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat issues.** The thermostat may be in a poor location (near a supply register, in sunlight, or above a lamp or TV). It may have weak batteries, the wrong equipment type configured (heat pump vs. conventional, or O/B reversing valve setting), or a cycle-rate or differential setting that is too tight.
2. **Restricted airflow.** A clogged filter, closed registers, or a dirty indoor coil can cause trouble in either mode. In heating, low airflow drives head pressure up and can trip the high-pressure switch. In cooling, it can freeze the coil and trip the low-pressure or freeze sensor.
3. **Dirty or blocked outdoor coil.** In cooling mode this raises head pressure, which trips the high-pressure switch and stops the compressor. Common blockages include cottonwood, leaves, or shrubs.
4. **Incorrect refrigerant charge.** A leak or low charge trips the low-pressure switch. An overcharge can trip the high-pressure switch. Either one produces start-stop behavior.
5. **Failing electrical components.** A weak run capacitor, pitted contactor, or overheating compressor can open the compressor's internal overload. The compressor then sits off until it cools, while the outdoor fan may keep running.
6. **Faulty pressure switch or sensor.** A failed pressure switch, a defrost or coil sensor reading out of range, or a loose control connection can end cycles early.
7. **Outdoor control board fault.** The board may log repeated trips or make relay errors.
8. **Oversized equipment.** An oversized system satisfies the thermostat too quickly. This is especially likely after duct, window, or insulation upgrades. It is a design issue rather than a failure.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Replace the air filter** if it is dirty. Make sure it is the correct size and seated properly.
- **Check the thermostat.**
  - Confirm it is set to Heat or Cool, not Emergency/Aux Heat, and that the fan is on Auto.
  - Replace the batteries if it uses them.
  - Make sure it isn't receiving direct sun or a draft from a supply register.
- **Open supply registers and clear return grilles.** Do not close more than about 20% of the house's registers.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from outside.**
  - Clear leaves, snow, and debris.
  - Trim plants back about 2 feet.
  - Gently hose dirt off the outside of the coil fins with the unit off at its disconnect. Do not pressure-wash, and do not open the cabinet.
- **Check the condensate drain.** If the indoor unit has a float or overflow switch, a full pan or clogged line can shut the system off repeatedly. Clear the visible drain line opening and make sure the pan isn't overflowing.
- **Check breakers and the outdoor disconnect.** Verify they are fully on. If the unit is in hard lockout, you may cycle power **once**: turn it off at the thermostat, switch off the breaker for 5 minutes, then restore power. If cycling resumes, stop and call a technician. Repeated resets can damage the compressor.
- **Confirm the exterior access panels are fully seated** on both indoor and outdoor units.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Read fault history.** The tech checks thermostat alarms on communicating systems, or the LED codes and stored fault history on the outdoor control board, looking for high-pressure, low-pressure, or lockout counts.
- **Verify the thermostat.** This includes the equipment and O/B configuration, cycle-rate settings, and wiring at the thermostat and air handler.
- **Measure airflow.** The tech checks static pressure and blower speed settings and inspects the indoor coil.
- **Measure system operating conditions.** Gauges or probes are used to measure superheat, subcooling, and suction and discharge pressures against Lennox charging specs. If the charge is low, they should look for a leak rather than just topping it off.
- **Test electrical components.** The capacitor (µF), contactor, compressor amperage, and windings are tested. Pressure switches are checked for continuity, and sensors are compared against resistance charts.
- **Run a full cycle.** The tech times the run and watches for exactly when and why the unit drops out.
- **Assess sizing.** If no fault is found, a load calculation (Manual J) may be needed to evaluate whether the system is oversized.

A good quote names a specific failed part or measured condition. Be cautious of "add refrigerant" without leak checking, or a compressor replacement without amp and winding tests.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit shuts off minutes after starting; house never quite reaches set point | Thermostat placement, settings, or weak batteries | Replace batteries; check mode and fan settings; block nearby drafts | Reconfigure or relocate thermostat; repair wiring |
| Cycling plus weak airflow at registers | Dirty filter, closed registers, dirty indoor coil | Replace filter; open registers | Clean coil; adjust blower speed; measure static pressure |
| Outdoor unit stops in summer heat; coil visibly dirty | High head pressure trip | Clear debris; gently rinse fins with power off | Deep coil cleaning; test high-pressure switch |
| Frost on indoor lines, or poor cooling with cycling | Low refrigerant charge, low-pressure trip | Replace filter; call for service | Find and repair leak; recharge to spec |
| Compressor stops but outdoor fan keeps running, then restarts much later | Compressor overload: weak capacitor or overheating | None beyond clearing the outdoor unit | Test and replace capacitor or contactor; evaluate compressor |
| Thermostat shows alarm or board LED flashing | Pressure switch, sensor, or board fault | Note the code; one power reset | Diagnose per code; replace switch, sensor, or board |
| Indoor unit shuts down; water in drain pan | Condensate float switch tripped | Clear drain line opening; empty the pan if safe | Clear the line fully; check pump or trap |
| Short runs with no fault, especially after home upgrades | Oversized system | Use a heat pump-compatible thermostat with cycle-rate settings | Load calculation; staging or airflow adjustments |

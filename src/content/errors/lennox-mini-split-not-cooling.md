## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a single fault code. On a Lennox ductless mini-split, it means the indoor head is running, or appears to be running, but the room isn't getting cooler.

This page covers what is specific to ductless systems: wireless-remote mode settings, washable head filters, inverter compressors, flare-fitting line sets, and multi-zone mode conflicts. For central split systems, see the separate Lennox AC and heat pump pages listed under Related codes.

Mini-splits often show a fault code or blink pattern on the indoor unit display. Some also show one on the outdoor board. If you see a code, record it, because the meaning varies by model.

Many protective faults clear on their own once conditions return to normal. Others hold until power is cycled. This behavior is model-dependent, so check your manual.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Wrong mode or setpoint on the remote.** Fan, Dry, Auto or Heat mode, a setpoint above room temperature, or dead remote batteries can each look like a failure.
2. **Clogged washable filter on the indoor head.** Mini-split filters load up fast and choke airflow.
3. **Dirty indoor coil or blower wheel.** Dust and mold buildup on the barrel-style blower wheel cuts airflow sharply, even when the filter is clean.
4. **Multi-zone mode conflict.** Another head is calling for heat, so the outdoor unit won't run in cooling.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** Flare fittings at the indoor head or the outdoor service valves are the most common leak points.
6. **Dirty or blocked outdoor coil, or a failed outdoor fan.** The unit overheats and the inverter slows the compressor or shuts it down.
7. **Failed temperature sensor or thermistor.** This includes room, indoor coil, outdoor coil or discharge sensors.
8. **Communication wiring fault** between the indoor and outdoor units, usually with a code displayed.
9. **Electronic expansion valve (EEV) or inverter control board failure.**
10. **Inverter compressor failure.** This is the least likely cause and the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Check the remote.** Confirm the mode is Cool (snowflake icon) and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the remote batteries. On wired-controller models, check that controller's settings instead.
- **Clean the filters.** Lift the front panel of the indoor head as your owner's manual describes. Slide out the mesh filters, rinse them with lukewarm water, let them dry fully, and reinstall them. Do this every 2–4 weeks in cooling season.
- **Check every head on a multi-zone system.** Set all of them to Cool, or turn off any that are set to Heat.
- **Clear the outdoor unit.** Make sure there are at least 12–24 inches of clearance around it, with no leaves, cottonwood, grass clippings or covers on the coil. Don't open the unit or spray inside it.
- **Check the breaker and disconnect.** Confirm both are on. If the system shows a fault code, cut power at the breaker for about five minutes and restore it **once**. If the code returns, stop and call.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Make sure the drain line exits freely outside and isn't kinked or plugged. Water dripping from the head can signal a drain problem or icing.
- **Watch for ice.** If you see frost on the indoor coil or the copper lines, turn the system off and let it thaw. Call a technician. Running it frozen can harm the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- Reads the fault code history from the indoor display or outdoor board and checks it against the model's service manual.
- Measures supply and return air temperatures at the head. A healthy unit usually shows a 15–25°F drop between them.
- Inspects the indoor coil and blower wheel, and may quote a full coil and blower cleaning (often called a bag cleaning).
- Checks outdoor coil condition and fan operation, and confirms the compressor ramps up when cooling is called for.
- Connects gauges and checks pressures, superheat and subcooling. If the charge is low, they leak-test the flare fittings with bubble solution or an electronic detector before any recharge.
- Measures sensor resistance against the manufacturer's temperature charts.
- Checks communication voltage and inverter board outputs. Confirms EEV operation.
- Repairs leaks by re-flaring or replacing the fitting, then pulls a vacuum to the required level and charges by weight.

A recharge with no leak search is a red flag on any quote.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but feels room temperature | Wrong mode, or remote batteries dead | Set to Cool, lower the setpoint, replace batteries | None if this fixes it |
| Weak airflow from the head | Clogged filter or dirty blower wheel | Rinse the filters | Coil and blower wheel cleaning |
| One zone won't cool, others fine | Mode conflict, or a zone EEV or sensor fault | Set all heads to Cool | Test the EEV, sensors and zone wiring |
| Cooling fades over weeks, ice on lines | Refrigerant leak at the flares | Turn the unit off and let it thaw | Leak search, flare repair, vacuum, weighed charge |
| Outdoor unit hot, fan not turning | Fan motor or board fault | Clear debris around the unit | Diagnose and replace the fan motor or board |
| Code flashing on the indoor display | Sensor, communication or inverter fault | Record the code, do one power reset | Diagnose with the service manual |
| Outdoor unit silent, head running | Communication or inverter board fault, or compressor failure | Check the breaker and disconnect | Electrical diagnosis, board or compressor replacement |

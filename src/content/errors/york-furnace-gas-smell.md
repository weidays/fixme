## What a gas smell means

**If you smell gas (rotten eggs or sulfur) right now:**

1. **Do not** flip light switches, adjust the thermostat, unplug anything, use the furnace switch or light a flame. Don't use your phone or start a car in an attached garage until you're outside and away from the house.
2. Get everyone, including pets, out of the house. Leave the door open behind you if it's on your way.
3. From outside, well away from the house, **call your gas utility's emergency number or 911.**
4. Do not go back inside until the utility or fire department says it is safe.

"Gas smell" is not a York diagnostic code. The furnace control board cannot sense leaked gas. Your furnace may run normally, or even show a steady "OK" LED, while gas is escaping. Utilities add a sulfur-smelling odorant (mercaptan) to natural gas and propane so people notice a leak.

The board does not reset anything here, because it doesn't know there is a problem. The danger continues until someone shuts off the gas and repairs the leak.

A related but different hazard is **carbon monoxide (CO)**, which comes from incomplete combustion or a cracked heat exchanger. CO has no smell. If a CO alarm sounds, follow the same steps: get out and call 911.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Leaking gas fitting or flex connector at the furnace.** A loose or corroded flare nut, a union or an aging flexible connector between the shutoff valve and the furnace is the most common source. It is often disturbed during service or a move.
2. **Gas leak elsewhere on the line.** The furnace room is often where the smell is noticed, but the source may be a nearby water heater, a dryer, the meter or a pipe joint. The utility's check covers this.
3. **Leaking furnace gas valve.** An internal or external valve leak, or a loose inlet or outlet pipe connection or pressure tap plug on the valve, can release gas. A valve that is failing electrically is covered on the 10 flashes page. On many York boards, 10 flashes indicates a gas valve circuit problem. Flash-code meanings vary by control board generation, so check the label on your blower door.
4. **Delayed ignition.** Gas builds up before it lights, giving a brief smell and often a boom. Causes include dirty burners, a weak igniter or misaligned burners. See "Loud bang on startup."
5. **Venting or combustion problem.** A disconnected, blocked or back-drafting vent pipe can let flue products into the house. These have a sharp, acrid, exhaust-like smell that some people mistake for gas, and they can carry CO.
6. **Non-gas odors that resemble gas.** Burning dust at the first startup of the season, a dried-out sewer trap or a failing electrical component can smell similar. Never assume this is the cause. Have a professional confirm it first.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

With a gas smell, **the call comes first.** Do not do any checks while the odor is present. Only once the utility or fire department has cleared the home and your furnace is shut off or tagged:

- **Check CO and gas alarms.** Test or replace the batteries in your carbon monoxide alarms. Consider adding a UL-listed combustible gas alarm near the furnace and water heater.
- **Look at the visible vent pipes from outside.** Check the intake and exhaust terminations for snow, leaves, nests or crushed pipe. Note anything you see for the technician, and don't take anything apart.
- **Leave the furnace off.** If the utility tagged the furnace or shut the gas, leave it that way. Do not reset the furnace or relight anything until a licensed technician has repaired and tested it.
- **Note the details.** Write down when the smell happens, for example only at startup, all the time, or only when another appliance runs. This helps the technician narrow it down quickly.

There is no filter, thermostat or breaker fix for a gas smell.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Utility response.** The utility checks with a combustible gas detector at the meter, the appliances and the piping. If it finds a hazardous leak, it shuts off and locks or tags the supply. The utility usually does not repair appliances.
- **Leak testing at the furnace.** An HVAC technician uses an electronic gas detector and leak-detection solution on every joint. This covers the shutoff valve, flex connector, sediment trap, union, gas valve inlet and outlet, pressure taps and manifold.
- **Gas pressure check.** A manometer reads inlet and manifold pressure against the York rating plate. Typical natural gas manifold pressure is about 3.5 in. w.c., and propane is higher. Wrong pressure causes delayed ignition and odor.
- **Ignition observation.** The technician watches the burners light. Ignition should cross all burners smoothly with no rollout and no delay.
- **Combustion and venting test.** A combustion analyzer measures CO in the flue. The technician also checks vent connections and draft, inspects the heat exchanger, and measures ambient CO.
- **Repair and retest.** The technician replaces the faulty part, leak-tests the system again, and restores the gas and the pilot or ignition.

Be wary of a quote that replaces the whole furnace for a fitting leak. Expect replacement only when there is a documented cracked heat exchanger or the valve and manifold can't be repaired economically.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steady gas smell near the furnace, even when it's off | Leaking fitting, flex connector or gas line | Leave, then call the utility or 911 from outside | Leak test, replace the connector or remake the joint, retest |
| Gas smell throughout the house | Leak on the main line or another appliance | Evacuate and call the utility or 911 | Utility locates the leak; plumber or HVAC tech repairs |
| Smell right at startup, then a boom | Delayed ignition from dirty burners, a weak igniter or wrong gas pressure | If the smell persists after the boom, evacuate and call the utility first. Only switch off the furnace once no odor is present, and don't reset repeatedly | Clean and align burners, check the igniter, set manifold pressure |
| Smell localized at the gas valve | Leaking valve body or valve connection | Evacuate and call the utility | Replace the gas valve or reseal the connections, then check pressure |
| Acrid exhaust smell, headaches or a CO alarm | Venting failure or cracked heat exchanger | Get out and call 911; check the outside vents only after you're cleared | Vent repair, combustion analysis, heat exchanger inspection |
| Burning-dust smell on the first cold day that fades within an hour | Normal dust burn-off | If unsure, treat it as gas and call | Confirm there is no leak during a tune-up |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices for 2026, including labor:

- **Gas utility odor investigation:** usually $0.
- **Emergency HVAC service call (nights or weekends):** $150–$400.
- **Tightening or remaking a leaking joint:** $100–$250.
- **Replacing a flexible gas connector:** $150–$350.
- **Replacing the gas valve on a York furnace:** $300–$750.
- **Cleaning burners and correcting gas pressure (delayed ignition):** $150–$400.
- **Vent pipe repair or reconnection:** $150–$600.
- **Heat exchanger replacement:** $1,500–$3,500. This is often covered by York's limited heat exchanger warranty for parts only, but labor is extra. York warranty coverage depends on the model, product registration and whether you are the original owner. Replacing the furnace instead costs $3,500–$7,500+.
- **Combustible gas or CO alarm (homeowner purchase):** $25–$60 each.

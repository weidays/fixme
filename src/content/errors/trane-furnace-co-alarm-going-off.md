## What this code means

**If your CO alarm is sounding, do this first:**

1. Get everyone out of the house, including pets, right now.
2. Do not stop to find the source.
3. Call **911 or your fire department** from outside.
4. If you can do it on the way out with no delay, set the thermostat to OFF or flip the furnace service switch off.
5. Call your **gas utility's emergency line** too. Many utilities send a technician to test appliances.
6. Do not go back inside until responders say CO levels are safe.
7. Anyone with a headache, nausea, dizziness or confusion needs medical care immediately.

"CO alarm going off" is not a Trane fault code. Trane furnaces have no carbon monoxide sensor. The alarm comes from a separate household CO detector. A Trane gas furnace becomes a suspect when combustion gases escape into the living space instead of leaving through the vent, or when the furnace burns poorly and makes excess CO.

The alarm does not "auto-reset" in any meaningful sense. A detector may go quiet once CO levels drop or the house is aired out, but that does not mean the problem is fixed. **Do not run the furnace again until a qualified technician has inspected it and cleared it.**

## Common causes, ranked by probability

Trane publishes no CO-specific troubleshooting order. This ranking reflects how often each cause turns up in field inspections.

1. **A source other than the furnace.** Common culprits are:
   - a gas water heater that is backdrafting
   - a car or generator running in or near an attached garage
   - a gas range or oven
   - a fireplace

   Responders and technicians test every appliance for this reason.

2. **Blocked or disconnected venting.**
   - On 90%+ condensing Trane models, the PVC intake or exhaust pipe can be blocked by snow, ice, leaves or nests, or a pipe joint can separate.
   - On 80% models, the metal flue can be disconnected, rusted through, or blocked at the chimney.

   A blocked vent often also causes a pressure switch fault (3 flashes) or a flame rollout fault, but not always.

3. **Cracked or failed heat exchanger.** This allows flue gas into the airstream. It is more common on older units and on furnaces that have run hot because of restricted airflow.

4. **Poor combustion.** Possible causes include:
   - dirty or misaligned burners
   - incorrect gas pressure
   - wrong orifices, such as after a propane conversion
   - not enough combustion air

   All of these raise CO output.

5. **Backdrafting on 80% furnaces.** Strong exhaust fans or a tight house can create negative pressure that pulls flue gas back down the vent.

6. **Faulty or expired detector.** False alarms do happen, often from a detector past its 7–10 year life. Treat this as the last explanation, never the first.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

**Only do these after the fire department or utility has cleared the home.** Keep the furnace off until a technician has inspected it.

- **Check the detector itself.** Look at the manufacture or replace-by date and the battery. If the unit is expired, replace it with a UL 2034 listed alarm. Still have the furnace inspected.
- **Look at the visible exterior vent terminations** on condensing models, from the outside only. Note any snow, ice, debris or a nest. You can clear loose snow from around the terminations. Do not take vent pipes apart.
- **Check the air filter.** A heavily clogged filter makes the furnace run hot, which can stress the heat exchanger. Replace it if it is dirty.
- **Check the condensate drain** on condensing units for obvious clogs or standing water.
- **Make sure the furnace's exterior panels are seated properly.** A missing or loose blower door can pull flue gas into the airstream.
- **Do not reset and run the furnace to "see if it happens again."** This is not a situation for a test run.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A proper CO call should include:

- **Ambient CO readings** throughout the house and near each gas appliance.
- **A combustion analysis** with a calibrated analyzer, measuring CO in the flue gas and O2 or CO2. Many techs flag anything above about 100 ppm air-free CO in the flue for further investigation.
- **A heat exchanger inspection.** This may involve a camera scope, checking for flame disturbance when the blower starts, and CO readings at supply registers.
- **Venting inspection.** The tech checks pipe slope, joints, terminations, chimney liner condition, and vent sizing per Trane's installation manual.
- **Gas pressure and burner checks.** This includes manifold pressure, orifices, and burner condition and alignment.
- **Depressurization or backdraft testing** on atmospheric and 80% systems, run with exhaust fans on.
- **Review of fault history** on the control board. Look for codes such as pressure switch or rollout faults.

**Red flags in a quote:**

- A "cracked heat exchanger" diagnosis with no photo, camera footage, or measurement.
- Pressure to replace the whole furnace the same day.

Ask to see the evidence. Get a second opinion on any replacement recommendation.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alarm sounds with the furnace running in winter | Venting failure or heat exchanger | Evacuate and call 911. Keep the furnace off | Combustion analysis, vent and heat exchanger inspection |
| Alarm after heavy snow, with a 3-flash pressure switch code | Blocked PVC intake or exhaust (condensing units) | After the all-clear, clear snow from terminations outside | Verify the vent, check termination height and code compliance |
| Alarm only when the dryer or kitchen fans run | Backdrafting (80% or atmospheric units) | Evacuate. Note which fans were on | Depressurization test, add makeup air, check the flue |
| Sooty marks near the furnace, yellow flames | Poor combustion or burner problem | Keep the furnace off | Clean and align burners, set gas pressure, combustion test |
| Alarm with the furnace off, or a car or generator nearby | Non-furnace source | Evacuate. Stop the engine or generator if it is safe to do so | Test all appliances |
| Intermittent chirp, no alarm pattern | Low battery or end-of-life detector | Replace the battery or detector | Not needed if there is no alarm and an inspection is clean |
| Repeated alarms on an older furnace | Cracked heat exchanger | Keep the furnace off | Heat exchanger replacement or new furnace |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges and vary by region and model.

- **New CO detector:** $20–$60 DIY
- **Fire department or utility response:** usually $0
- **Diagnostic visit with combustion analysis:** $100–$300
- **Vent repair:**
  - Clearing a vent: $100–$250
  - Re-gluing or resloping PVC: $150–$500
  - Replacing a flue connector or chimney liner: $300–$2,500
- **Burner cleaning and gas pressure adjustment:** $150–$400
- **Makeup air or backdraft correction:** $300–$1,500
- **Heat exchanger replacement:** $1,500–$4,000. If the part is covered under warranty, expect $800–$2,000 for labor.
- **Full furnace replacement:** $4,000–$10,000+

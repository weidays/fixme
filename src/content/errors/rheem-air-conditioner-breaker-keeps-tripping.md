## What this code means

"Breaker keeps tripping" is not a Rheem fault code. It is a symptom: the dedicated 240-volt circuit breaker for your outdoor condensing unit shuts off to protect the circuit. It trips because the unit pulls more current than the breaker allows, or because current is leaking to ground through a short.

A breaker that trips **instantly** when the AC starts usually points to a dead short or a ground fault, such as a shorted compressor winding or damaged wiring. A breaker that trips **after several minutes of running** usually points to overload. Common culprits are a weak capacitor, a failing fan motor, or a compressor straining against high pressure.

The breaker does not reset itself. It stays off until someone resets it by hand. Rheem's variable-speed and EcoNet units may also show a fault on the outdoor control board or the thermostat. Single-stage units usually give no code at all.

If you have a Rheem **heat pump** rather than a straight AC, see the separate heat pump page listed below. Reversing-valve and defrost circuits add causes that don't apply here.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** Without proper capacitor assist, the compressor or fan motor draws high current trying to start, which trips the breaker. This is the most common electrical failure on residential condensers.
2. **Pitted or welded contactor.** Burned contacts cause arcing and erratic voltage. A welded contactor keeps the unit energized when it shouldn't be.
3. **Damaged, loose or corroded wiring.** Wires chewed by rodents, vibration-chafed wires touching the cabinet, and corroded lugs at the disconnect or contactor can all cause ground faults or heating.
4. **Failing condenser fan motor.** A seized or shorted fan motor overloads the circuit. If the fan stops, head pressure rises and the compressor overheats and over-amps.
5. **Dirty outdoor coil or restricted airflow.** High head pressure increases compressor amp draw, which leads to nuisance trips in hot weather.
6. **Compressor hard-starting or locked rotor.** An aging compressor may draw locked-rotor amps on every start. A hard-start kit is sometimes a stopgap.
7. **Shorted or grounded compressor.** Winding insulation breaks down and current goes to ground. The breaker trips immediately. This is the most expensive outcome.
8. **Undersized, worn or loose breaker.** The breaker may be the wrong size for the nameplate's maximum fuse or breaker rating, may be fatigued from age, or may have loose panel connections.
9. **Refrigerant system problems.** An overcharge or a non-condensables restriction raises pressure and amp draw. Only a licensed tech can confirm this with gauges.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Reset the breaker once.** Turn the thermostat to OFF first. Flip the breaker fully to OFF, then back to ON. Wait 5 minutes before calling for cooling so the compressor pressures can equalize. If it trips again, **stop and leave it off.**
- **Note the timing.** Write down whether it trips instantly, after a few seconds, or after several minutes. Telling the technician saves diagnostic time.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint. Replace the batteries if the display is weak. Rapid on/off calls from a failing thermostat can stress the start circuit.
- **Replace a dirty air filter.** Severely restricted airflow can cause icing and abnormal pressures.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from the outside only.** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and anything stacked against it, keeping about 2 feet of clearance. Look for visible signs of chewed wiring, burn marks, or a smell of burning, but don't open any panels. If you see or smell burning, keep the breaker off.
- **Make sure the exterior service panels are seated** and none are loose or missing.
- **Check the condensate line** indoors for a clog or overflow, which can trip a float switch. That float switch is a different symptom, but it's worth ruling out.

Do not open the disconnect box or the unit cabinet, and do not touch the capacitor. Capacitors hold a dangerous charge even with power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should do roughly the following, and you can use it to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Visual inspection** of the disconnect, whip, contactor, and wiring for burn marks, melted insulation, rodent damage, and loose lugs.
2. **Capacitor test** with a meter, measuring microfarads against the rated value (usually ±6%).
3. **Contactor inspection** for pitting, and a voltage drop test across the contacts.
4. **Compressor winding resistance** (common to start, common to run) and a **megohm or ground test** from each terminal to the casing. A reading to ground means the compressor is grounded.
5. **Amp draw** on the compressor and fan, compared to the nameplate RLA and FLA, under real operating conditions.
6. **Breaker verification**: compare the breaker size to the nameplate MOCP and check the panel connections.
7. **Fan motor check** for bearing seizure and winding resistance.
8. **Refrigerant pressures** only after the electrical tests pass, to look for high head pressure.
9. On **EcoNet or variable-speed** units, read the stored **control board and inverter fault history**.

Be cautious if someone condemns the compressor without showing winding or ground test readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Breaker trips the instant the AC starts | Shorted or grounded compressor, or shorted wiring | Reset once; if it repeats, leave it off | Ground and megohm test, wiring repair, or compressor replacement |
| Humming, then a trip within seconds | Failed run capacitor or locked compressor | Leave it off after one reset | Test and replace the capacitor; possibly add a hard-start kit |
| Trips after 10–30 minutes of running | Overload from a failing fan motor, dirty coil, or high head pressure | Clear debris around the unit and replace the filter | Fan motor test, coil cleaning, amp draw check |
| Outdoor fan not spinning before the trip | Fan motor or fan capacitor failure | Don't run the unit | Replace the fan motor or capacitor |
| Burning smell or scorch marks at the unit | Arcing contactor or melted wiring | Keep the breaker off | Replace the contactor and wiring |
| Trips only on the hottest days | Weak capacitor, dirty coil, or aging compressor | Keep the outdoor unit clear | Amp draw test, coil cleaning, capacitor replacement |
| Breaker warm, or trips with the AC barely loaded | Fatigued breaker or loose panel lug | None beyond the single reset | Electrician or tech replaces the breaker or tightens the lugs |
| Fault shown on an EcoNet thermostat | Inverter or board-detected electrical fault | Note the code; reset once | Read the fault history; diagnose the drive or board |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, parts plus labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic or service call | $90 – $200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Hard-start kit | $200 – $450 |
| Wiring or whip repair | $150 – $500 |
| Condenser fan motor | $350 – $800 |
| Outdoor coil cleaning | $100 – $300 |
| Breaker replacement (electrician) | $150 – $350 |
| Control board or inverter drive (variable-speed) | $600 – $1,800 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $3,500+ |

Many Rheem units carry a 10-year parts limited warranty when registered, which can cover the compressor. Labor is usually extra unless you bought an extended plan. On an older unit, a grounded compressor often makes replacing the whole system the better value.

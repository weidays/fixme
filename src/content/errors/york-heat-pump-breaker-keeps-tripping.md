## What this code means

"Breaker keeps tripping" is not a code shown on a York control board. It means the circuit breaker feeding your heat pump is cutting power because it detected too much current or a short circuit. York heat pumps don't display a fault for this. The unit simply goes dead, and you'll find a tripped breaker in the electrical panel or at the outdoor disconnect.

A breaker trip is not a lockout that clears by itself. Power stays off until someone manually resets the breaker. A York defrost or control board may log separate faults, such as high-pressure or low-pressure lockouts, but those protect the system without tripping the breaker. A tripped breaker almost always points to an electrical fault or a component drawing excessive current.

Your system typically has two circuits:

- **Outdoor unit (compressor and fan)**: trips here usually involve the compressor, the capacitor, the contactor, or outdoor wiring.
- **Indoor air handler with electric heat strips**: trips here often involve the heat strips, the blower motor, or an undersized or failing breaker. These trips tend to happen in cold weather or during defrost, when the strips switch on.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

York doesn't publish a single troubleshooting sequence for breaker trips. This order reflects standard York service practice and how common each failure is in the field.

1. **Failed or weak run/start capacitor (outdoor unit).** A bad capacitor makes the compressor or fan motor struggle to start. The motor then draws locked-rotor current, which trips the breaker within seconds of startup. This is the most common cause.
2. **Hard-starting or failing compressor.** Worn bearings, a mechanical lockup, or a shorted or grounded winding cause very high current draw. A winding grounded to the compressor shell trips the breaker instantly, every time.
3. **Damaged or corroded wiring and connections.** Problems include rodent-chewed or weather-damaged whips at the outdoor disconnect, loose lugs, and burnt terminals on the contactor or compressor. Loose connections heat up and can cause shorts.
4. **Pitted or welded contactor.** A worn contactor can chatter or arc, causing current spikes. A welded contactor can keep the unit running continuously.
5. **Electric heat strip or sequencer fault (indoor breaker).** Shorted elements, failed sequencers that bring all strips on at once, or heat kits on undersized breakers cause trips, mostly in cold weather or during defrost.
6. **Outdoor fan motor failure.** A seized or shorted condenser fan motor can trip the breaker. It also causes high head pressure, which overloads the compressor.
7. **High head pressure from airflow or refrigerant problems.** A badly clogged outdoor coil, a severely restricted indoor filter, or a refrigerant overcharge or non-condensables raise compressor load and current. These problems are more likely to trigger York's pressure switches first, but they can push a marginal breaker over its limit.
8. **Weak, worn, or wrongly sized breaker.** Breakers fatigue after repeated trips. A breaker sized below the unit nameplate's minimum circuit ampacity will trip under normal load. A breaker above the nameplate maximum is a safety violation.
9. **Water intrusion.** Water in the outdoor disconnect or junction box, often after storms or from sprinklers, can cause ground faults.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Note which breaker tripped and when.** Was it at startup, after a few minutes, during defrost, or on the coldest nights? Was it the outdoor or indoor circuit? Write it down. This is the most useful information you can give a technician.
- **Reset the breaker once.** Set the thermostat to OFF first. Flip the breaker fully OFF, then ON. Wait 5 minutes so system pressures can equalize, then turn the thermostat back to heat or cool. **If it trips again, leave it off and call a pro.** Don't reset it again.
- **Check the air filter.** A severely clogged filter raises system load. Replace it if it's dirty.
- **Check the thermostat settings.** Make sure the thermostat isn't calling for emergency heat unnecessarily, since emergency heat runs all heat strips. Replace the batteries if the display is weak.
- **Look, don't touch.** From a distance, check the outdoor unit for leaves, snow, or debris packed against the coil, and gently clear the area around it. Look for obvious chewed wires, scorch marks, or a burning smell. If you see any of these, keep the power off.
- **Make sure the exterior panels are seated** and the outdoor disconnect cover is closed.

Do not open the electrical panel cover, the disconnect box, or the unit's service panels.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically:

1. **Ask which circuit tripped and under what conditions**, then inspect the breaker, the disconnect, and the wiring for heat damage.
2. **Verify breaker size against the York nameplate**, checking the minimum circuit ampacity and the maximum fuse/breaker rating.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter that measures microfarads against its rated value.
4. **Megohm or ohm the compressor windings.** They'll check common-start-run resistance and test each terminal to ground. A grounded compressor is a definitive diagnosis.
5. **Measure amp draw** at startup and while running, using a clamp meter, and compare it to the nameplate RLA and LRA.
6. **Inspect the contactor** for pitting and burnt terminals, and check the fan motor windings and amp draw.
7. **For indoor trips**, check heat strip element resistance, sequencer or relay staging, the blower motor, and breaker sizing for the heat kit.
8. **Check system pressures** only if electrical tests point to an overload, such as high head pressure.

Be wary of a quote to replace the compressor without a documented winding or amp-draw test. Also be wary of anyone who proposes simply installing a larger breaker.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor breaker trips seconds after startup, unit hums | Failed capacitor or hard-start compressor | Reset once; if it repeats, leave off | Test and replace the capacitor; consider a hard-start kit |
| Breaker trips instantly the moment it's reset | Shorted or grounded compressor or wiring | Leave off; don't reset again | Megohm the compressor; repair wiring or replace the compressor |
| Indoor breaker trips on cold days or during defrost | Heat strip, sequencer, or breaker sizing | Check that emergency heat isn't set; reset once | Test the strips and sequencer; verify breaker and wire sizing |
| Trips after running 10 to 30 minutes | High head pressure, fan motor, weak breaker | Replace the filter; clear debris from the outdoor unit | Check the fan motor, coil, and refrigerant pressures; test the breaker |
| Burning smell or scorch marks at the disconnect | Loose or burnt connections | Keep the power off | Replace lugs, whip, or disconnect; repair wiring |
| Trips after heavy rain | Water in the disconnect or junction box | Keep off until inspected | Dry out, reseal, and replace damaged components |
| Breaker feels hot or trips at random | Worn breaker or loose panel connection | Keep off; note the pattern | Licensed electrician tests and replaces the breaker |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

- **Service call and diagnosis:** $90–$200
- **Run/start or dual capacitor:** $150–$450
- **Hard-start kit:** $200–$450
- **Contactor:** $150–$400
- **Wiring, whip, or disconnect repair:** $150–$500
- **Breaker replacement (electrician):** $150–$350
- **Outdoor fan motor:** $350–$800
- **Heat strip element or sequencer:** $250–$700
- **Indoor blower motor:** $450–$1,500 (variable-speed ECM motors cost more)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800–$3,500+. Warranty coverage can offset parts costs; York compressors often carry a 10-year parts warranty when the unit was registered. On older units, full system replacement ($6,000–$12,000+) may make more sense.

## What this code means

"Breaker keeps tripping" is not a Bryant fault code. It is a symptom: the dedicated double-pole breaker that feeds your outdoor condensing unit is cutting power to protect the circuit.

A breaker trips for one of three reasons:

- **Overcurrent:** the unit is drawing more amps than the circuit is rated for.
- **Short circuit:** current is taking an unintended path between conductors.
- **Ground fault:** current is leaking to the metal cabinet or to ground.

On a Bryant AC, the breaker does not "reset itself." It stays off until someone flips it back on.

Bryant units with an outdoor control board (two-stage and Evolution/variable-speed models) may also store their own fault codes. These include high-pressure, compressor-protection or inverter faults, and they can help a technician pin down the cause. Single-stage units usually have no outdoor diagnostics, so testing has to be done with meters.

Because the possible causes sit inside the high-voltage cabinet, this is technician work after one safe reset.

This page covers the electrical tripping symptom only. If the unit runs but cycles on and off without tripping the breaker, see the related short-cycling page.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failing or failed run/start capacitor.** A weak capacitor makes the compressor or fan motor struggle and draw high current. It is common in older units and after summer heat waves.
2. **Compressor electrical fault.** This includes a grounded (shorted-to-case) winding, a shorted winding, or a mechanically locked rotor. It often produces an instant trip and is the most serious cause.
3. **Worn or pitted contactor.** Burned contacts can arc, chatter or weld shut, causing high current or shorting.
4. **Damaged or loose wiring.** Rodent-chewed, chafed, corroded or loose connections in the outdoor unit or whip can short to the cabinet.
5. **Outdoor fan motor failure.** A shorted or seized fan motor draws excess current. A stalled fan also lets head pressure climb, which overloads the compressor.
6. **High head pressure from a dirty or blocked outdoor coil, or severely restricted indoor airflow.** The compressor works harder and draws more amps, often tripping on the hottest part of the day.
7. **Weak or worn breaker, or an incorrectly sized breaker or wiring.** Old breakers lose holding strength. A breaker that is undersized versus the unit's nameplate "Max Fuse/Breaker" rating, or one that was swapped in during other work, can also trip.
8. **Inverter or drive-board fault (variable-speed Evolution models only).** A failed drive component can short or overload the circuit. This does not apply to single-stage units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Set it to Cool with a reasonable setpoint. Replace the batteries if the display is weak or blank.
- **Air filter:** Check it and replace it if it is dirty. Run the system only after the electrical issue is resolved.
- **Breaker, one reset only:** Flip the AC breaker fully OFF, then back ON.
  - Wait 5 minutes before calling for cooling. This lets pressures equalize.
  - If it trips again, leave it off and call a technician. Do not reset it a second time.
  - If you hear buzzing, smell burning, or see scorching at the panel, do not reset at all.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Confirm the exterior disconnect is fully seated or ON. Do not open the box or touch its fuses.
- **Visible airflow:** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked. Clear leaves, grass clippings and stored items from around the outdoor unit, keeping about 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate:** Check that the indoor drain line or condensate pump isn't overflowing. Water near indoor electrical components should be reported to the technician.
- **Note the pattern:** Does it trip instantly or after minutes of running? Only on hot days? This helps the technician diagnose faster.

## How a technician will diagnose it

1. **Check the breaker and wiring against the nameplate.** They compare the breaker size and wire gauge with the unit's Minimum Circuit Ampacity and Max Fuse/Breaker rating, and inspect the panel for heat damage.
2. **Read any stored fault codes.** On Evolution and other communicating or two-stage models, they check the outdoor board LED or the thermostat's service menu.
3. **Megohm-test the compressor.** With power locked out, they test the compressor windings to ground and measure winding resistance (common-run-start) to detect a grounded or shorted compressor.
4. **Test the capacitor.** They measure microfarads against the rating on the label and look for bulging or leaking.
5. **Inspect the contactor.** They check for pitting, welding and voltage drop across the contacts.
6. **Check the fan motor.** They check its windings to ground and confirm it spins freely.
7. **Measure running amps.** They compare actual current draw with the compressor RLA and the motor nameplate ratings.
8. **Check pressures.** They look for high head pressure and check coil cleanliness and airflow.
9. **Test the breaker itself.** If every component tests good, they test or replace the breaker.

A quote that jumps straight to "replace the compressor" or "replace the system" should be backed by a megohm or winding-test reading. Ask for the numbers.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trips instantly when AC calls for cooling | Grounded or shorted compressor, shorted wiring | One breaker reset, then leave it off | Megohm and winding test, wiring repair or compressor replacement |
| Humming outdoors, then trip | Failed capacitor or locked compressor | Turn the system off at the thermostat | Test and replace capacitor, evaluate compressor (hard-start kit if appropriate) |
| Trips after 10–30 minutes on hot days | High head pressure, dirty coil, weak capacitor | Replace filter, clear debris around unit | Clean coil, check charge and pressures, test capacitor |
| Outdoor fan not spinning, then trip | Failed fan motor or fan capacitor | Clear visible debris only | Replace fan motor or capacitor |
| Buzzing or chattering contactor | Pitted or failing contactor | None; leave the system off | Replace contactor |
| Burning smell or scorch marks at panel | Loose or overheated breaker connection | Do not reset; call promptly | Repair panel connection, replace breaker |
| Breaker warm and old, unit tests normal | Worn breaker | None | Replace breaker with the correct size |
| Fault LED or thermostat alert (Evolution) | Inverter or drive fault | Note the code shown | Diagnose and repair drive or control board |

## Repair costs

Ranges below are typical US prices, including parts and labor.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic visit | $80–$200 |
| Run or dual capacitor | $120–$400 |
| Contactor | $150–$350 |
| Breaker replacement (electrician or tech) | $150–$350 |
| Wiring repair (outdoor unit or whip) | $150–$500 |
| Hard-start kit | $150–$400 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $300–$700 |
| Outdoor coil cleaning | $100–$300 |
| Inverter or drive board (variable-speed) | $600–$1,800 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,500+ (often weighed against replacing the condenser) |

Prices vary with region, warranty status and refrigerant type. Check your Bryant parts warranty, since compressors are often covered for 10 years when the unit was registered.

## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a numbered Trane fault code. It describes a pattern: the air conditioner starts, runs only a few minutes, shuts off, then starts again shortly after. It often repeats many times an hour.

How it shows up depends on your system:

- **ComfortLink II / Link communicating systems.** The thermostat or outdoor control may log an alert when a pressure, temperature or compressor protection trips. The exact codes and how they reset vary by model. Check the alert history on the thermostat or the installation guide for your model.
- **Conventional 24V systems.** There is no code. You notice it from run times, uneven cooling and high humidity.

Many Trane outdoor units and thermostats include a short-cycle protection delay, commonly around 3 to 5 minutes. This delay stops the compressor from restarting immediately after it shuts off. It does not fix the underlying cause.

Safety switches behave differently by model:

- Pressure switches generally reset on their own once pressure normalizes.
- The compressor's internal overload resets after the compressor cools, which can take 30 minutes to several hours.
- Some boards lock the unit out after repeated trips until power is cycled.

This page covers AC cooling mode only. For heating-mode cycling, see the Trane furnace and heat pump short cycling pages.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat problems.** Common examples:
   - The thermostat is mounted near a supply register, in direct sun or near a heat source.
   - Batteries are weak.
   - The cycle-rate or equipment-type setting is wrong.
   - Wiring at the thermostat is loose or failing.

2. **Restricted airflow.** A clogged filter, closed or blocked registers, or a dirty evaporator coil can cause low suction pressure. That may trip the low-pressure or freeze protection. When airflow is severely restricted, the coil can freeze. See Frozen evaporator coil.

3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil or blocked airflow around the outdoor unit.** Heat cannot escape, so head pressure rises and the high-pressure switch or compressor overload trips. The unit runs again once things cool down.

4. **Low refrigerant charge (leak).** Low charge causes low-pressure trips and overheating of the compressor's internal overload. Refrigerant does not get used up, so low charge means there is a leak.

5. **Failing run capacitor or contactor.** A weak capacitor makes the compressor or fan motor overheat and trip on its overload. A pitted contactor can chatter or drop out.

6. **Outdoor fan problems.** If the condenser fan is weak or stops, the compressor overheats within minutes. See Outdoor fan not spinning.

7. **Oversized equipment.** This applies if the problem has existed since installation and the system otherwise checks out. The house cools quickly, the unit stops early, and humidity stays high.

8. **Control board, pressure switch or compressor faults.** Examples include a faulty pressure switch, a compressor with a failing internal overload or high amp draw, or an outdoor control fault on a communicating unit.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat**
  - Set it to COOL, with the fan on AUTO, and lower the setpoint 3 to 4°F below room temperature.
  - Replace the batteries if it uses them.
  - Make sure nothing that produces heat or blows air is right next to the thermostat: lamps, sun, a supply vent.
  - Check whether the run time changes after these steps.

- **Air filter.** Pull it and hold it to a light. If it's dirty, replace it with the correct size, with the airflow arrow pointing toward the blower.

- **Vents and returns.** Open all supply registers and clear furniture and rugs away from return grilles. Closing many vents raises pressure and starves the coil.

- **Outdoor unit**
  - Make sure there are about 2 feet of clearance around it and nothing on top.
  - Remove leaves, grass clippings and debris you can reach from outside the unit by hand.
  - Do not open the panels.

- **Breaker and disconnect**
  - Confirm both the indoor and outdoor breakers are fully on, and the outdoor disconnect is seated.
  - If the unit has stopped and appears locked out, you may cycle power **once**. Turn it off at the breaker for 5 minutes, then back on. Allow the built-in delay before it restarts.
  - If a breaker trips again, leave it off and call a technician.

- **Condensate.** If you see water in the drain pan or a float switch tripping, check that the condensate line outlet is not clogged. A tripped float switch can cut power to the system in an on-off pattern.

- **Ice.** If you see ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines, turn cooling off and run the fan only to thaw it. Then call for service.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically work through these steps:

- **Verify the complaint.** Time the cycles. Confirm whether the thermostat is ending each call or the outdoor unit is dropping out on a safety while the call is still active. They do this by measuring 24V at the Y and C terminals of the outdoor unit.
- **Read alert history** on ComfortLink II / Link systems from the thermostat or outdoor control.
- **Inspect the thermostat.** Check location, configuration (equipment type and cycles per hour) and wiring, and look for loose connections.
- **Check airflow.** Inspect the filter and evaporator coil and measure static pressure and temperature split. A normal temperature drop across the coil is roughly 15 to 22°F.
- **Connect gauges.** Measure suction and head pressure, superheat and subcooling, and compare them with the charge chart for the unit. If the charge is low, perform leak detection before adding refrigerant.
- **Test electrical components.**
  - Measure capacitor microfarads against the rating.
  - Inspect the contactor for pitting.
  - Measure compressor and fan amp draw.
  - Check compressor winding resistance.
- **Check the outdoor coil and fan.** Look at coil cleanliness, fan motor speed and blade condition.
- **Test safety switches.** Check the high- and low-pressure switches for continuity and trip points where they are fitted.
- **Do a load calculation** (Manual J) if oversizing is suspected. Don't accept a "your unit is too big" diagnosis without one.

Be cautious of a quote that only adds refrigerant without leak testing, or recommends a new system without measured readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 2–5 minutes, house not yet cool, thermostat satisfied | Thermostat location, settings or batteries | Replace batteries, check settings, remove nearby heat sources | Relocate or rewire thermostat, adjust cycle rate configuration |
| Short runs, weak airflow from vents | Clogged filter or blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents | Clean evaporator coil, check blower and static pressure |
| Ice on refrigerant line or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off cooling, run fan to thaw, replace filter | Leak search, repair, recharge to specification |
| Outdoor unit stops while thermostat still calling, hot air from top | Dirty condenser coil, failing fan, high head pressure | Clear debris and clearance around unit | Clean coil, test fan motor and capacitor, check high-pressure switch |
| Outdoor unit hums, clicks or chatters, then shuts off | Weak capacitor or worn contactor | None beyond one power reset | Test and replace capacitor or contactor |
| Compressor off for long periods, outdoor fan may still run | Compressor internal overload tripping | None | Check charge, amp draw, capacitor and windings |
| Cools fast but stays clammy, has short cycled since installation | Oversized system | Fan on AUTO, not ON | Load calculation, airflow or staging adjustments, possible right-sizing |
| Alert shown on ComfortLink or Link thermostat | Protection fault logged by control | Note the code, cycle power once | Diagnose per Trane service facts for the model |
| System cuts out, water in pan | Condensate float switch tripping | Clear condensate line outlet | Clear trap and line, check pan and switch |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Filter replacement (DIY) | $10–$40 |
| Thermostat replacement | $150–$450 installed ($50–$250 DIY for non-communicating; communicating Trane thermostats cost more) |
| Service call and diagnosis | $100–$250 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Evaporator coil cleaning | $200–$600 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350–$800 |
| Pressure switch replacement | $200–$500 |
| Leak search and recharge | $300–$1,500+, depending on refrigerant type (R-22 systems cost much more) and leak location |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$3,000 |
| Outdoor control board (communicating models) | $400–$1,000 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,500+ (often weighed against replacing the system) |

Many Trane units carry a 10- to 12-year registered parts warranty, which can cover the part but not the labor. Check your registration before you approve a major repair.

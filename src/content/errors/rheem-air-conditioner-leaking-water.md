## What this code means

"Leaking water" isn't a diagnostic code that a Rheem air conditioner displays. It's a symptom. Standard Rheem split-system ACs have no fault light that reports a condensate leak. The exception is when an installer added a float or overflow switch, which simply shuts off cooling. Some communicating systems with an EcoNet control can show a related alert if a compatible sensor is wired in.

When the system cools, the indoor evaporator coil (above or beside your furnace or air handler) collects moisture. That water drips into a drain pan and leaves through a PVC condensate line. A leak almost always means one of three things:

- The water can't get out (clog, bad pump, poor slope).
- The pan holding it is damaged.
- The system is producing more water than the drain was designed for, such as meltwater from a frozen coil.

Nothing resets on its own here. If a float switch has tripped, cooling stays off until the water drains and the switch drops back down.

This page covers leaks from the **indoor AC coil during cooling season**. Winter leaks from a high-efficiency gas furnace (exhaust condensate, inducer, trap) are a separate problem. See Rheem Furnace Leaking Water.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged condensate drain line or trap.** Algae, slime, dust, and debris build up in the line or the P-trap and back water into the pan. This is the most common cause by far, especially early in the cooling season.
2. **Dirty air filter leading to a frozen evaporator coil.** Restricted airflow drops the coil temperature below freezing. When the ice melts, the water overflows the pan or drips off the coil outside the pan. Low refrigerant and blower problems can cause the same freeze. Full coverage is in Rheem AC Frozen Evaporator Coil.
3. **Failed or unplugged condensate pump.** In basements, attics, and closets without gravity drainage, a small pump lifts the water out. Pumps fail in several ways: stuck floats, clogged reservoirs, burned-out motors, kinked discharge tubing, or a pump that has been unplugged.
4. **Cracked, rusted, or displaced drain pan.** Older metal pans rust through. Plastic pans can crack with age or heat. In horizontal attic installs, the coil can sit so condensate misses the pan.
5. **Clogged or missing secondary (emergency) drain pan or line.** In attic and above-ceiling installs, the backup pan under the unit can fill with debris. Its drain line may also be blocked, or no pan was installed at all. Water then reaches the ceiling.
6. **Installation problems.** These include a drain line with too little slope or a sag, a missing or incorrectly sized trap, or an unsealed drain connection at the coil. Another is a coil that isn't level, so water pools on the wrong side of the pan. These often show up in the first season after a new install.
7. **Disconnected or cracked drain line or fittings.** A slipped PVC joint, a cracked fitting at the pan outlet, or a line knocked loose during other work can cause this.
8. **Uninsulated ductwork or refrigerant lines sweating.** In humid attics and basements, condensation can form on cold supply ducts, the plenum, or the copper suction line and drip. It looks like a unit leak, but the drain is fine.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Turn the system **off at the thermostat** first. If water is near electrical components or outlets, switch off the furnace or air handler power switch or breaker before you get close.

- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray and clogged, replace it. If you see ice on the coil area or the copper lines, leave cooling off and set the thermostat fan to ON for several hours to thaw it. Keep towels or a pan underneath. Don't chip at the ice.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint. Very low setpoints (below about 68°F) on humid days can encourage freezing. Replace thermostat batteries if the display is weak.
- **Inspect the condensate drain outlet.** Follow the white PVC line to where it ends: outside near the foundation, at a floor drain, or into a pump. If no water is coming out while the system runs, or the end is buried in mulch or dirt, the line is likely blocked.
- **Clear the drain line.** Many Rheem installations have a capped vent tee or cleanout near the coil.
  - Remove the cap and slowly pour about a cup of plain white vinegar or warm water down the line.
  - From outside, you can also attach a wet/dry vacuum to the drain outlet, seal it with a rag or tape, and run it for 1–2 minutes to pull out the clog.
  - Don't use bleach mixed with other cleaners, and don't run wire or snakes into the coil side.
- **Check the condensate pump (if you have one).** Make sure it's plugged in and the outlet works. Check that the discharge tubing isn't kinked. If the reservoir is full and the pump doesn't run, unplug it and call a technician.
- **Look at the secondary pan (attic units).** If you can see it safely, check whether it's holding water. Standing water there confirms the primary drain is failing. Empty it with a wet/dry vac or towels.
- **Check the breaker and switch once.** If cooling stopped along with the leak, and the drain is now clear and the pan is empty, confirm the breaker and the furnace or air handler switch are on. Restore power **once**. If cooling doesn't return, a float switch or other part needs a technician.
- **Make sure the access panels are fully seated** after any checks. Don't open the coil cabinet, remove taped coil panels, or disconnect wiring.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician follows a logical sequence. You can use it to judge a quote.

- **Confirm where the water comes from.** Is it the primary pan, the coil itself, the secondary pan, a pump, the drain line, or sweating ducts and lines?
- **Inspect and flush the condensate system.** They'll clear the trap and line with nitrogen, a pump, or a vacuum. They'll also check slope, venting, and that the trap is correctly installed for the coil's negative or positive pressure.
- **Open the coil cabinet and inspect the drain pan** for rust, cracks, or misalignment. Many Rheem/Ruud cased coils use an integral pan. A failed pan may mean replacing the pan alone if it's available for that coil, or the whole coil.
- **Test the float or overflow switch** to confirm it opens the low-voltage cooling circuit as designed. A stuck or bypassed switch should be replaced, not removed.
- **Test the condensate pump.** They'll check the motor, float, check valve, and safety switch wiring, and clean or replace the pump.
- **Check airflow and refrigerant if there's any sign of icing.** This includes static pressure, blower operation, coil cleanliness, and superheat/subcooling. A refrigerant leak search is a separate, licensed job with its own cost.
- **Check level and insulation.** They'll confirm the coil is level and pitched toward the drain, and look for uninsulated ducts or suction lines that are sweating.

Be wary of a quote to replace the evaporator coil or the condenser for a leak that hasn't been traced. Most leaks are fixed by clearing a drain or replacing a pump.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water around furnace/air handler base, no water at drain outlet | Clogged condensate line or trap | Turn off cooling, flush the line with vinegar, vacuum the outlet | Power-flush the line, rebuild the trap, correct slope |
| Ice on copper lines or coil, water after shutdown | Frozen coil (dirty filter, low airflow, low refrigerant) | Replace filter, run fan only to thaw | Airflow diagnosis, refrigerant leak search and repair |
| Pump reservoir full, pump silent | Failed condensate pump | Check that it's plugged in and the tubing isn't kinked | Replace the pump and its safety switch |
| Water stains on ceiling below attic unit | Primary drain failed and secondary pan overflowing or missing | Empty the secondary pan, clear the drain outlet | Clear both drains, add a pan float switch or secondary pan |
| Steady drip even with a clear drain | Cracked or rusted drain pan, or coil not level | Keep cooling off, catch water | Replace the pan or coil, level the coil |
| AC stopped cooling, water in pan | Float/overflow switch tripped | Clear the drain, let it drain, restore power once | Test or replace the float switch, check wiring |
| Water drips from a pipe joint near the coil | Loose or cracked drain fitting | Note the location, keep cooling off | Re-glue or replace PVC fittings |
| Drips from ducts or lines, not the unit | Condensation on uninsulated duct or suction line | Check that registers are open and the filter is clean | Insulate ducts and lines, seal duct leaks |

## Repair costs

Typical US price ranges for 2026, including labor. Prices vary by region and how hard the unit is to reach. Attics cost more.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| DIY drain flush, filter, tablets | $0–$40 |
| Professional condensate line clearing | $75–$250 |
| Trap rebuild or drain line repair/re-slope | $150–$400 |
| Float/overflow safety switch install or replacement | $100–$250 |
| Condensate pump replacement | $150–$400 |
| Secondary (emergency) drain pan install | $200–$500 |
| Drain pan replacement (where sold separately) | $300–$800 |
| Duct or suction line insulation | $150–$600 |
| Refrigerant leak search and repair (if frozen coil) | $250–$1,500+ |
| Evaporator coil replacement (failed integral pan or leaking coil) | $1,200–$2,800+ |

Coil and refrigerant work may be partly covered under Rheem's parts warranty, typically 10 years on registered units. Labor is usually not covered unless you bought an extended labor plan. Water damage to ceilings and floors is not covered by the equipment warranty. Check your homeowner's insurance.

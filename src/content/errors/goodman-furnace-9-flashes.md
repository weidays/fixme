## What this code means

Nine flashes on a two-stage Goodman (or Amana) furnace control board means the **high-stage pressure switch failed to close on high fire**. When the furnace calls for high heat, the board runs the inducer at high speed and expects the high-stage pressure switch to sense adequate draft and close its contacts. If that switch does not close within the allowed time, the board reports 9 flashes.

This is a **safety interlock working correctly**: the board is confirming the venting can handle high-fire combustion before it opens the gas valve to high stage. On most models, the furnace either stays on **low fire** (weaker heat) or retries before dropping back. It is not a gas leak and not an immediate hazard, but it does need a pro because the fixes live inside the cabinet.

One important caveat: **the meaning of a 9-flash count varies by control board.** On the two-stage integrated controls listed above it points to the high-stage pressure switch, but Goodman and Amana have used different flash-code legends across board generations and product families. Read the diagnostic chart printed on the inside of your blower-compartment door — that legend is the authoritative reference for your specific furnace — before acting on this code.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Blocked or restricted vent/intake on high fire** — snow, ice, nests, or debris in the flue or intake pipe that only chokes airflow enough to matter at high speed.
2. **Condensate blockage in the inducer or drain** — a clogged condensate trap or drain line lets water back up and prevent the high-stage switch from closing.
3. **Cracked, loose, or pinched pressure-switch tubing** — the hose to the high-stage switch is off, split, or has water sitting in it.
4. **Weak or failing inducer motor** — the inducer spins but no longer moves enough air to create the pressure the high-stage switch needs.
5. **Failed high-stage pressure switch** — the switch itself is out of calibration or has failed open.
6. **Wiring/connector faults** — a loose or corroded connection between the switch and the control board.
7. **Blocked heat exchanger or secondary passages** — sooting or debris raising resistance so draft can't reach high-fire spec.

Standard troubleshooting practice is to verify venting and the switch/tubing before condemning the inducer or the board.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do:

- **Air filter:** A clogged filter chokes airflow. Replace it if it's dirty and see if the code clears.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to Heat and calling. If it has batteries, replace weak ones.
- **Breaker / furnace switch:** Cycle the furnace breaker or the switch on the unit once. That gives the board one clean retry — once only, not repeatedly.
- **Outdoor vent and intake pipes:** From outside and at ground level, look for snow, ice, leaves, or a nest blocking the PVC termination. Clear only what you can safely reach.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see standing water or an overflowing trap on a high-efficiency unit, that's a clue — but only clear a visibly accessible external drain line.
- **Registers and vents:** Make sure supply and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.

If the code returns after these, stop — the remaining work is inside the cabinet.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A tech will typically:

- Read the stored fault and confirm the board legend shows high-stage pressure switch open.
- Inspect the flue and intake for restriction, then check the concentric or two-pipe termination.
- Check the **high-stage pressure switch tubing** for water, cracks, or a disconnected hose.
- Use a **manometer** to measure inducer pressure at high fire and compare it to the switch's set point, which is normally stamped on the switch body and listed in the service and parts literature.
- Verify the switch contacts close at spec with a **multimeter**; a switch that never closes at correct pressure gets replaced.
- Inspect the **inducer motor** for slow speed, bearing wear, or a debris-loaded wheel.
- Check the **condensate trap and drain** for blockage causing back-pressure.
- Inspect wiring, connectors, and the control board as a last step.

Ask the tech what draft pressure they actually measured versus the switch's stamped set point — that tells you whether it's a venting, inducer, or switch problem.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9 flashes, weak/low heat only | High-stage switch won't close | Check/replace filter; cycle breaker once | Measure high-fire draft, test switch |
| Code after snow or storm | Blocked flue/intake termination | Clear visible snow/debris outside | Inspect full vent run, clear restriction |
| Water gurgling near furnace | Clogged condensate trap/drain | Clear visible external drain only | Clear trap, inducer drain passages |
| Inducer runs but sounds weak | Failing inducer motor | None — call a pro | Test/replace inducer motor |
| Code even with clear venting | Failed pressure switch or tubing | None — call a pro | Replace switch, reseat/replace tubing |
| Intermittent 9 flashes | Loose wiring/connector | Cycle power once | Repair connections, retest |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor):

- **DIY filter / thermostat batteries / clearing outside vent:** $0–$40
- **Condensate trap or drain cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Pressure-switch tubing replacement / reseating:** $120–$220
- **High-stage pressure switch replacement:** $150–$350
- **Inducer motor/assembly replacement (two-stage or ECM):** $400–$1,200
- **Control board replacement (two-stage integrated board):** $400–$1,200
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $100–$225, higher after hours (often credited toward the repair)

Costs vary by region, model, and access. A venting or tubing fix is cheap; a two-stage inducer assembly or integrated control board is the expensive end, and either one can push the job toward the top of the range above.

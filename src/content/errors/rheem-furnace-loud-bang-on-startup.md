## What this code means

A loud bang on startup is not a numbered Rheem fault code — it is a **symptom** your furnace shows when it fires up. There are two very different causes that sound similar:

1. **Delayed ignition (a "mini-explosion").** Gas flows into the burners but does not light immediately. When it finally ignites, the accumulated gas lights all at once, producing a bang or "whoomp." This is a combustion problem and is the one to take seriously.
2. **Ductwork expansion ("oil canning").** When the blower starts or the metal heats up, thin sections of supply/return duct flex and pop. This is noisy but not a combustion hazard.

Because your Rheem furnace uses hot-surface ignition and flame-proving, delayed ignition does **not** always trigger an ignition lockout on the first event — the flame does eventually establish. That means the furnace may keep running and keep banging, which is why this is a **pro-level** problem: repeated delayed ignition can crack the heat exchanger. If you ever smell gas, stop and treat it as an emergency: shut the furnace off, ventilate, leave the home, and call your gas utility's emergency line or 911 before doing anything else.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or sooted burners.** Debris and corrosion block or slow the flame from traveling across the burners, delaying full ignition.
2. **Weak or aging hot-surface igniter.** A slow-heating igniter lets gas flow longer before it lights.
3. **Igniter/burner misalignment or carryover problems.** The flame fails to jump cleanly from one burner to the next.
4. **Gas pressure set too high (manifold pressure out of spec).** More gas accumulates before ignition.
5. **Dirty flame sensor causing re-light attempts.** The flame establishes, drops out, then re-ignites with a bang.
6. **Low airflow from a clogged filter or closed vents.** Restricted airflow can disrupt combustion timing and change how the flame behaves.
7. **Expanding/contracting ductwork.** A non-combustion bang from flexing sheet metal, often mistaken for ignition noise.

The combustion-related causes (1–5) are the most important to rule out because of the heat-exchanger risk. Rheem's own troubleshooting prioritizes ignition quality — igniter condition, burner cleanliness, and gas pressure — when diagnosing delayed ignition.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do. Anything inside the cabinet is for a technician.

- **Replace a dirty air filter.** A clogged filter restricts airflow and can affect combustion. A clean filter is free troubleshooting.
- **Open closed or blocked supply and return vents.** Restricted airflow can contribute to the noise; make sure registers are open and furniture/rugs aren't covering returns.
- **Check the thermostat.** Set it to Heat, a few degrees above room temperature, and replace the batteries if it uses them.
- **Verify the furnace switch and breaker.** Confirm the service switch (looks like a light switch near the furnace) is on and the breaker hasn't tripped.
- **Listen for where the bang comes from.** If it's clearly in the ductwork away from the furnace and happens as the blower starts, it may be duct expansion — still worth mentioning to the tech.
- **If you smell gas:** do not troubleshoot. Shut down, ventilate, leave, and call the gas utility emergency line or 911.

Do **not** open the burner compartment, clean burners or the flame sensor, adjust gas pressure, or repeatedly restart a unit that bangs — these are technician tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will confirm whether the bang is combustion or mechanical, then:

- **Watch a full ignition cycle** with the burner compartment accessible to see if the flame lights immediately or after a delay.
- **Inspect and clean the burners** and check for soot, rust, or debris.
- **Test the hot-surface igniter** for proper resistance and glow timing; replace if slow or cracked.
- **Measure manifold gas pressure** with a manometer and adjust it to Rheem's spec for your model.
- **Check flame carryover and igniter/burner alignment.**
- **Clean or test the flame sensor** and confirm steady flame signal so the flame isn't dropping out and re-lighting.
- **Inspect the heat exchanger** for cracks caused by repeated delayed ignition — a critical safety check.
- **Evaluate ductwork** for oil-canning if the sound isn't combustion-related.

Sanity check a quote: a legitimate diagnosis always includes watching the ignition sequence and a heat-exchanger inspection, not just swapping one part.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Whoomp"/bang right as burners light | Delayed ignition from dirty burners or weak igniter | Replace filter; limit use until inspected | Clean burners, test/replace igniter, check pressure |
| Bang plus brief flame then it relights | Flame dropping out and re-igniting | None safe inside cabinet | Clean/test flame sensor, verify flame signal |
| Bang worse after months of use | Burner soot buildup or aging igniter | Replace filter | Inspect/clean burners, replace igniter |
| Bang gets louder over time | Manifold gas pressure too high | None | Measure and adjust manifold pressure |
| Pop/bang in ducts away from furnace | Ductwork expansion (oil canning) | Open vents; note location for tech | Reinforce/modify duct, add dampers |
| Any gas smell with the bang | Possible gas leak / combustion fault | Shut down, ventilate, leave, call utility/911 | Full combustion and leak diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts plus labor; varies by region and model):

- **Air filter (DIY):** $15 – $40.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $90 – $180.
- **Burner cleaning / burner service:** $150 – $350.
- **Hot-surface igniter replacement:** $150 – $350.
- **Flame sensor clean or replacement:** $100 – $250.
- **Gas valve / manifold pressure adjustment:** $150 – $400; **gas valve replacement:** $400 – $900.
- **Heat exchanger inspection:** often included in the service call.
- **Heat exchanger replacement (if cracked from repeated delayed ignition):** $1,200 – $3,000+, often making furnace replacement more sensible on older units.
- **Ductwork modification for oil canning:** $150 – $600 depending on access.

Because a loud bang can signal delayed ignition that damages the heat exchanger, addressing it early is far cheaper than ignoring it.

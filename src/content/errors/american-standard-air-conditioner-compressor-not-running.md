## What this code means

"Compressor not running" is a symptom, not a numbered fault code on most American Standard central air conditioners. The thermostat is calling for cooling, but the compressor in the outdoor unit isn't pumping refrigerant. You will usually notice one of three patterns:

- **Nothing outside runs.** Both the fan and the compressor are silent.
- **The outdoor fan spins, but the compressor is silent or only hums.**
- **The compressor starts, then stops after a short time.**

This page covers the compressor itself. If your main symptom is the fan, warm air, or rapid cycling, see the related pages listed at the bottom.

**How it resets depends on the cause and the model:**

- **Internal overload.** The compressor's built-in thermal overload resets on its own once the motor cools. That can take anywhere from 30 minutes to several hours.
- **Pressure-switch lockout.** On models with high- or low-pressure switches wired through a control board, a trip may cause a lockout that holds until power is cycled.
- **Variable-speed models.** Communicating inverter models (Platinum/AccuComfort) log their own drive fault codes, which show on the outdoor board or the thermostat.

Check your unit's wiring diagram, found inside the service panel, or ask a technician which behavior applies to your model.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** This is the most common cause. The compressor hums, or the fan runs slowly or not at all, and the compressor trips on its overload.
2. **Failed contactor.** The 24V coil isn't pulling in, or the contacts are pitted or burned. Nothing outside runs, or power reaches only part of the circuit.
3. **No 24V call reaching the outdoor unit.** Possible reasons include thermostat settings, dead batteries, a tripped float/condensate safety switch, a blown control fuse, or a broken low-voltage wire.
4. **Compressor thermal overload tripped.** This is usually caused by something else: low refrigerant charge, a dirty condenser coil, a failed outdoor fan motor, or a weak capacitor.
5. **High- or low-pressure switch open, or lockout.** Common triggers are low charge, a restriction, a very dirty coil, or a non-running fan. This applies to models equipped with these switches.
6. **Loss of high voltage.** This includes a tripped breaker, a pulled or blown disconnect, or a single leg of 240V missing.
7. **Locked rotor or failed compressor windings.** The compressor itself is mechanically or electrically failed.
8. **Inverter drive fault (variable-speed models only).** The drive board or communication has failed. Diagnose this from the drive's fault code.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Check the thermostat.** Set it to Cool, set it at least 3°F below room temperature, and make sure the fan is on Auto. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
2. **Check the breakers.** Look at both the AC breaker and the furnace or air-handler breaker. If either is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro. Also see the related page on the breaker that keeps tripping.
3. **Check the outdoor disconnect.** Make sure the switch or pull-out block near the unit is fully in and switched on. Don't open the box beyond its normal handle or pull-block.
4. **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter can freeze the coil and trigger low-pressure shutdowns.
5. **Check the condensate drain.** Many systems have a float switch that cuts the cooling call if the drain pan fills. If you see standing water, clear the visible drain line opening, or use a wet/dry vac at the exterior end.
6. **Check airflow paths.** Make sure supply and return vents are open. Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the outside of the condenser, keeping about 2 feet of clearance.
7. **Wait, then reset once.** If the unit hummed or felt hot, turn the system off at the thermostat for 30–60 minutes so the overload can cool. Then cycle power once at the breaker and try again. If the compressor still doesn't run, leave it off and call a technician. Repeated restarts can damage the compressor.

Do not open the outdoor unit's panel, touch the capacitor, or push in the contactor. The capacitor stores a dangerous charge even with the power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A typical diagnosis follows this order:

1. **Verify the call.** The technician confirms 24V is reaching the contactor coil. If it isn't, they trace back through the thermostat, the float switch, the low-voltage fuse on the furnace or air-handler board, and the pressure switches or outdoor control board.
2. **Check line voltage.** They measure 240V on both legs at the disconnect and at the contactor's line and load sides.
3. **Inspect the contactor.** They look for pitting or burning, and confirm the coil pulls in.
4. **Test the capacitor.** They measure microfarads against the rating on the label. A reading more than about 6–10% below the rating, or any bulging or leaking, means replacement.
5. **Measure the compressor windings.** They check resistance across C-S, C-R, and S-R, and test for a short to ground. An open overload reads as an open circuit when the compressor is hot, so they may wait for it to cool.
6. **Measure amp draw on startup.** If the compressor shows locked-rotor amps and won't start, they may try a hard-start kit before condemning it.
7. **Check the refrigerant side.** They take pressures and superheat/subcooling readings, look for leaks if the charge is low, and check condenser coil cleanliness and outdoor fan operation as root causes of the overload.
8. **Read fault codes on variable-speed units.** They read the drive or outdoor board codes and check communication wiring.

A sound quote should name the specific failed part and the reading that proved it. "Compressor is bad" alone is not enough. Compressor replacement should always be backed by winding, ground, or locked-rotor test results.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nothing outside runs, thermostat calling | No 24V call, tripped breaker or disconnect, float switch | Check thermostat and batteries, reset breaker once, check disconnect, clear condensate drain | Trace low-voltage circuit, replace fuse, test contactor coil |
| Fan runs, compressor silent | Failed capacitor (dual), tripped overload, open pressure switch | Turn off, wait 30–60 minutes, reset power once | Test capacitor, windings, and pressure switches; replace failed part |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Weak capacitor, locked rotor | Turn system off immediately | Replace capacitor, try hard-start kit, test for locked rotor |
| Compressor starts, then shuts off | Overload from low charge, dirty coil, or fan failure | Clear debris around condenser, replace filter | Check charge, find leaks, clean coil, test fan motor |
| Outdoor unit buzzes, nothing moves | Contactor pulled in but capacitor or compressor failed | Turn system off | Test capacitor, contactor, and compressor |
| Breaker trips when compressor tries to start | Shorted or grounded compressor | Don't reset repeatedly | Megger and ohm test the compressor; quote replacement |
| Fault code on outdoor board (variable-speed) | Drive or inverter fault | Note the code, cycle power once | Diagnose per the drive's fault code list |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices, parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnosis | $80–$200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$450 |
| Hard-start kit | $200–$450 |
| Low-voltage fuse or float-switch fix | $100–$250 |
| Pressure switch replacement | $200–$500 |
| Refrigerant leak repair plus recharge | $400–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type; R-22 systems cost more) |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350–$700 |
| Inverter drive board (variable-speed) | $600–$1,500+ |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800–$3,500+ (often covered by a 10–12 year registered compressor warranty, but labor and refrigerant usually aren't) |

If the system is more than 12–15 years old and needs a compressor, compare that cost against a full system replacement.

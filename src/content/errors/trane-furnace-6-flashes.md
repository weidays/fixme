## What this code means

Six flashes on your Trane furnace's control board means the board has detected **reversed 115 VAC line polarity or poor (or missing) grounding** at the furnace.

Modern Trane integrated furnace controls (IFCs) compare line, neutral and ground references to decide whether the incoming 115 VAC is wired correctly and the equipment ground is intact. This matters because the flame-sensing circuit uses the ground as its reference. If hot and neutral are swapped, or the ground is loose or absent, the board cannot reliably prove flame — so it refuses to ignite and posts the 6-flash fault instead of risking an unsafe cycle.

This fault is **self-clearing**: once correct polarity and a solid ground are restored, the code disappears and normal operation resumes. It is not a hard lockout that needs a manual reset. That said, it is squarely **professional-level** work, because every real fix lives inside the furnace cabinet, an outlet, a junction box, or the electrical panel.

> Note: some boards report faults as numeric codes rather than flash counts. Check the flash legend printed on your blower-door label to confirm your board's convention — that printed legend governs.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Reversed hot/neutral at the furnace outlet or junction box** — the most common trigger, usually right after an outlet, receptacle, or whip was rewired with the wires swapped.
2. **Open or missing equipment ground** — a disconnected, corroded, or never-connected ground conductor at the furnace or panel.
3. **Loose neutral connection** — a backed-out or poorly seated neutral in the junction box or panel that produces a floating/unreliable reference.
4. **Miswired circuit after recent electrical work** — new subpanel, added circuit, or a swapped breaker that put the furnace on a miswired leg.
5. **Generator or transfer-switch wiring** — some portable/standby generator setups feed the furnace with reversed or ungrounded 115 VAC.
6. **Failed or ungrounded receptacle/outlet** — a worn outlet or an old two-prong/ungrounded circuit feeding the furnace.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do. None require opening the cabinet.

- **Confirm the furnace breaker is ON** and hasn't tripped. Reset it once if it's tripped; if it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the furnace service switch** (the light-switch-style disconnect near the furnace) is in the ON position.
- **Verify the thermostat** is set to Heat and the setpoint is above room temperature; replace thermostat batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Look at your recent history** — did this start right after any electrical work, a new outlet, a generator hookup, or a power event? Note it for your technician; it points straight to the cause.
- **Make sure the furnace is plugged in fully** if it uses a cord-and-plug connection, and the outlet cover is intact.

Do **not** open the blower door to probe wiring, and do not attempt to test polarity yourself with a meter — that's inside-the-cabinet and behind-the-outlet work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech or electrician will:

1. Confirm the 6-flash code and read your board's flash legend.
2. Use a **plug-in polarity tester or multimeter** to check hot-to-ground and neutral-to-ground voltages at the furnace's line-voltage terminals.
3. Verify the **equipment ground** is continuous from the furnace back to the panel, checking for corrosion, loose lugs, or a missing conductor.
4. Inspect the **junction box, receptacle, or whip** for swapped hot/neutral wiring.
5. Trace the circuit back through the **breaker and panel** if the miswiring isn't at the furnace — including any generator or transfer-switch connections.
6. Correct the wiring, re-tighten connections, and confirm the code self-clears with the furnace running through a full heat cycle.

If a quote jumps straight to replacing the control board without ever testing polarity and ground, get a second opinion — the board is usually the *messenger*, not the fault.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 flashes, no heat, started after outlet/panel work | Reversed hot/neutral | Note the recent work; confirm breaker on | Test and correct polarity at outlet/junction box |
| 6 flashes plus intermittent flame-sense faults | Open or poor ground | Confirm breaker/switch on | Locate and repair/replace ground conductor |
| Code appears randomly, furnace sometimes runs | Loose neutral connection | None safe | Tighten/re-terminate neutral at box or panel |
| Started when running on a generator | Ungrounded/reversed generator feed | Switch back to utility power if safe | Correct generator/transfer-switch wiring |
| Old home, two-prong or ungrounded circuit | No proper ground path | None safe | Install proper grounded circuit for furnace |
| Board shows numeric code, not 6 flashes | Different board legend | Read blower-door label | Confirm code meaning, then diagnose polarity/ground |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. This fault is almost never a parts cost — it's a wiring correction.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair).
- **Correcting reversed polarity at an outlet or junction box:** $100 – $250, depending on access.
- **Repairing or adding an equipment ground:** $150 – $350, more if a new grounded circuit must be run.
- **Panel or subpanel wiring correction:** $200 – $500+ if a miswired breaker or subpanel is the root cause.
- **Control board replacement:** $400 – $1,200 installed — rarely needed for this code; only if the board's line-sensing circuit is confirmed damaged.

Because all of this work is inside the cabinet, behind an outlet, or inside the panel, expect a **licensed electrician or HVAC pro** — there is no safe DIY fix for this code. Total real-world cost is commonly **$150 – $350**.

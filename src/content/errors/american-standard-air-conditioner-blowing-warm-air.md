## What this code means

"Blowing warm air" is a symptom, not a stored fault code. The indoor blower is moving air, but that air is at or near room temperature, or warmer, instead of cool.

This page covers cases where the air feels actually **warm**, not just weak or slightly cool. That usually points to one of three things:

- The thermostat is calling for the wrong mode.
- The outdoor unit isn't removing heat.
- The system is moving air through a hot attic duct or a heat source.

Some related problems have their own pages:

- **Outdoor unit completely silent:** see "Compressor not running" or "Outdoor fan not spinning."
- **Air is only somewhat cool:** see "Not cooling."

On conventional 24V American Standard systems, the condition has no code and no lockout. It persists until the cause is fixed.

On communicating (Link/AccuLink) systems, the thermostat or outdoor control may log a related alert, such as a pressure-switch trip or a compressor fault. Whether it auto-resets depends on the specific alert and model, so check the alert text on your thermostat.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat set to Heat, Auto changeover, or fan "On" with no cooling call.** With the fan set to On, the blower runs constantly. Between cycles it circulates warm attic or duct air.
2. **Outdoor unit not running because power is off.** A tripped breaker or a pulled disconnect at the outdoor unit means the indoor blower runs while the condenser sits idle.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Evaporator coil leaks are well documented on many brands, including American Standard and Trane. Low charge brings warm air, long run times, and sometimes ice on the lines.
4. **Failed run capacitor or stuck/burned contactor.** The compressor either won't start or hums and trips on overload, while the indoor fan keeps blowing.
5. **Dirty or blocked outdoor coil.** The unit can't reject heat, so head pressure rises. The high-pressure switch may trip and cycle the compressor off.
6. **Compressor failure.** This includes internal overload, mechanical failure, or failed valves. On variable-speed units, an inverter drive fault can also cause it.
7. **Duct leaks or a disconnected return in a hot attic.** The unit cools, but hot attic air is pulled in or the cooled air is lost before it reaches the rooms.
8. **Heat source on at the same time (dual-fuel or furnace/air handler setups).** Rarely, a stuck heat relay or a miswired thermostat leaves electric heat strips or the furnace on during cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm the mode is **Cool**, the setpoint is at least 3°F below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter causes weak airflow and can lead to coil freeze, which then makes the air feel warm.
- **Breakers:** Check both breakers at the main panel, the indoor unit and the outdoor AC. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop; see "Breaker keeps tripping."
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the pull-out or switch near the condenser is fully seated in the ON position. Don't open the box beyond the handle.
- **Listen at the outdoor unit:** After a cooling call, the fan should spin and you should hear the compressor hum within a few minutes. If it's silent or humming without the fan turning, call a technician.
- **Outdoor clearance:** Gently hose off visible debris, cottonwood or grass clippings from the outside of the fins with the power off. Keep plants about 2 feet away. Don't use a pressure washer.
- **Registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate:** If your system has a float switch, a full drain pan or clogged line can shut off cooling. Clear the line at the access tee with a wet/dry vac.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines, turn cooling off and run the fan only to thaw. Then call for service.
- **Exterior panels:** Make sure the panels are seated. A loose blower door can open a safety switch.

If none of these checks fixes it, turn cooling off and call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Confirm the cooling call.** The tech verifies the thermostat signal (Y and G) at the air handler and outdoor unit, or reads communicating-system alerts and fault history.
- **Check the electrical side.** This includes:
  - line voltage at the disconnect
  - the 24V coil on the contactor, and pitting on its contacts
  - run capacitor values under test
  - compressor winding resistance and amp draw
- **Check the refrigerant side.** The tech attaches gauges or digital probes and reads suction and liquid pressures, superheat and subcooling. They compare these with the charging chart on the unit's nameplate or service panel. Most current American Standard units with TXVs are charged by subcooling.
- **Measure temperature split.** A normal drop across the indoor coil is about 16–22°F. Near 0°F means no refrigeration. A low drop with normal current points to a leak or compressor valve problem.
- **Find leaks.** If the charge is low, the tech uses an electronic detector, bubbles, or a nitrogen pressure test. Evaporator coil and line-set flare/braze points are common spots.
- **Inspect coils and ductwork.** The tech checks the condenser fins, the evaporator face, and attic duct and return connections.

Be wary of a quote that adds refrigerant without any leak search or explanation, or that recommends compressor replacement without amp, winding and pressure readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, outdoor unit silent | Breaker/disconnect off, failed contactor or control | Check mode, reset breaker once, check disconnect | Test contactor, 24V signal, wiring, controls |
| Outdoor fan off, compressor humming | Bad capacitor or fan motor | Turn cooling off | Replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Both outdoor fan and compressor running, warm supply | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn cooling off, schedule service | Leak search, repair, evacuate, weigh in charge |
| Outdoor unit starts then stops repeatedly | High-pressure trip from dirty condenser, or overload | Rinse coil gently, clear debris | Clean coil, check fan, test pressure switch |
| Ice on lines, then warm air | Low charge or restricted airflow | Replace filter, thaw with fan only | Check charge, blower, evaporator coil |
| Warm air between cycles only | Fan set to "On" | Set fan to Auto | None needed |
| Cool at unit, warm at registers | Duct leak in attic | Check visible register connections | Seal or repair ducts |
| Hot air on cooling call | Heat stage energized | Confirm thermostat mode | Check thermostat wiring and heat relay |

## Repair costs

These are typical US price ranges, including labor.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Thermostat setting, filter, breaker | $0–$40 |
| Service call/diagnostic | $90–$200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350–$750 |
| Leak search | $200–$500 |
| Leak repair (accessible braze/flare) | $200–$1,500 |
| Refrigerant recharge, R-410A | $150–$500+ |
| Refrigerant recharge, R-454B | Costs more than R-410A |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$3,000 (parts may be covered by warranty) |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,500 (parts often covered within the 10-year registered warranty) |
| Inverter drive board, variable-speed models | $600–$1,800 |
| Duct sealing/repair | $300–$2,000 |

Register your unit and check your warranty status before approving major parts. American Standard parts warranties usually depend on registration within 60 days of installation.

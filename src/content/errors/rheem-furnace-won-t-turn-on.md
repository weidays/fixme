## What this code means

"Won't turn on" is not a single flash code — it's the symptom where your Rheem furnace shows **no response at all**: no blower, no inducer, no ignition sequence, and often no LED activity behind the blower door.

Rheem integrated furnace control boards do carry a diagnostic LED, but **what a given LED pattern means varies by control board**. On some boards a steady green LED is normal operation; on others a slow flash is normal and a steady light indicates a fault. Don't guess from the light alone: the **LED code chart for your specific furnace is printed on the blower-door label or inside the access panel**, and that chart is the only reliable way to read it. A board that appears dark can mean no incoming power, an open safety circuit, or a failed board — but a solid LED by itself does **not** mean the board is dead.

This differs from a furnace that tries to start but fails — if yours sparks or hums and then quits, see the ignition or short-cycling pages instead. Here, nothing happens.

Because the cause is often inside the cabinet (fuses, door switch, wiring, control board), most of these are **technician-level** diagnoses. The furnace does **not** auto-reset a hard no-power condition; you restore power by cycling the breaker or service switch **once**.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Tripped breaker or furnace service switch off.** The dedicated furnace circuit tripped, or someone flipped the switch on the furnace (it looks like a light switch) off.
2. **Thermostat problem.** Dead batteries, a thermostat set to "Off" or "Cool," a loose or broken C-wire / thermostat wiring fault, or a failed thermostat means no call for heat ever reaches the board.
3. **Open blower-door safety switch.** The access panel isn't seated fully, so the door interlock switch stays open and cuts all power — by design.
4. **Blown 3-amp low-voltage fuse on the control board.** A short in the thermostat wiring or accessory circuit pops the board's fuse, killing the 24V control circuit.
5. **Tripped high-limit or flame-rollout safety.** A prior overheat (often from a clogged filter) can open the high-limit and hold the furnace off. A **flame-rollout** trip is different and far more serious.

   > **Stop here if rollout is possible.** Flame rollout means burning gas escaped the burner compartment, typically from a blocked flue, a burner problem, or a cracked heat exchanger — all of which carry a **carbon monoxide risk**. If you smell burning, see soot or scorch marks around the burner area, or otherwise suspect rollout: **shut the furnace off at the service switch, do not restore power, do not open the burner compartment.** If there is any gas or burning smell, **leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside.** Then call a licensed technician. Rheem rollout switches are manual-reset devices inside the burner area, and they are **never to be reset or bypassed by a homeowner** — the switch did its job, and resetting it only re-exposes you to the hazard it caught.

6. **Failed low-voltage transformer.** No 24V output means the board can't energize anything.
7. **Failed integrated control board.** The board itself has failed and won't power the furnace or respond to a call for heat.

A standard no-power diagnostic sequence starts at incoming line voltage and the door switch, then the low-voltage fuse and transformer, before condemning the board — so a good tech works in roughly that order.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner — no tools inside the cabinet. **Do none of them if you suspect flame rollout** (burning smell, soot, scorch marks): shut the furnace off at the service switch, leave it off, and call a licensed technician — and leave the house and call the gas utility or 911 if you smell gas or burning.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Heat** and raise the setpoint 5°F above room temp. If it's blank or dim, **replace the batteries**.
- **Breaker:** Find the furnace breaker in your panel. If it's tripped (middle position), switch it fully **Off, then On**.
- **Furnace service switch:** On or near the furnace is a switch that looks like a light switch. Make sure it's **On**.
- **Blower door:** Push the access panel firmly until it seats. A loose panel keeps the door switch open and the furnace dead.
- **Air filter:** If it's gray and clogged, **replace it**. A choked filter can overheat the furnace into a lockout.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate line (high-efficiency models):** If the drain is clogged, a float switch may cut power. Clearing standing water at the drain trap is fine; wiring and switches are not.

Cycle power **once** after these checks. If it still won't start, stop and call a pro — repeated resets won't help.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

- Confirm **120V line voltage** at the furnace disconnect and board.
- Test the **door interlock switch** for continuity with the panel seated.
- Check the **3A low-voltage fuse** on the board and look for the short that blew it.
- Measure the **24V transformer output** at the board.
- Test continuity across the **high-limit and rollout switches**, and investigate *why* any tripped — restricted airflow for a limit, and blocked flue, burner trouble or heat-exchanger damage for a rollout, which must be found and corrected before the furnace is put back in service.
- Read the **control-board diagnostic LED** against the code chart for that specific board and run the board's self-test.
- Condemn the **integrated control board** only after power, fuse, transformer and safeties check out.

If a quote jumps straight to "new control board" without checking the fuse, transformer and door switch first, ask them to show their voltage readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| No LED, nothing runs | Tripped breaker / service switch off | Reset breaker, confirm switch on | Test line voltage, trace short |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries / failed stat | Replace batteries or thermostat | Check C-wire / thermostat wiring, 24V supply |
| Dead until door pushed in | Open blower-door switch | Reseat access panel firmly | Test/replace door interlock switch |
| No LED, breaker holds | Blown 3A board fuse | — | Replace fuse, find the short |
| Runs after cooling, dies when hot | Tripped high-limit (dirty filter) | Replace filter, cycle power once | Test limit, correct airflow |
| Burning smell, soot or scorch marks, furnace dead | Possible flame rollout | Shut off at service switch, leave power off, don't open the burner compartment; leave and call the gas utility or 911 if you smell gas or burning | Find and fix the combustion cause (flue, burners, heat exchanger) before any reset |
| High-efficiency unit dead, water pooled | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain trap | Test float switch, clear line |
| No response, power confirmed | Failed transformer or control board | — | Test transformer first, then board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Breaker reset / thermostat setting:** $0 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **New thermostat:** $25–$250 part; $100–$250 installed
- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Blower-door interlock switch:** $150–$300
- **Low-voltage board fuse (plus finding the short):** $100–$250
- **Low-voltage transformer:** $150–$350
- **High-limit / rollout switch:** $150–$350 (plus whatever it takes to correct the underlying cause)
- **Integrated control board:** roughly **$400–$1,000+ installed**. Single-stage boards sit at the low end; two-stage, variable-speed and modulating controls commonly land above $700.

Diagnostic/service-call fees typically run **$90–$200** and are often credited toward the repair.

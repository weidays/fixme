## What this code means

"Compressor not running" is a symptom, not a numbered Bryant fault code. The thermostat is calling for cooling, but the compressor in the outdoor unit isn't starting or staying on. You may see one of these patterns:

- The outdoor fan spins, but the compressor is silent.
- The unit hums or clicks repeatedly.
- Nothing outside runs at all.

Without the compressor, refrigerant doesn't circulate and the system can't remove heat.

The page "Bryant AC Fan Runs but No Cold Air" covers the broader no-cold-air picture. This page focuses on why the compressor itself won't run.

How this shows up depends on the model:

- **Single-stage Legacy and Preferred units** use a contactor and capacitor. They usually give no code at all.
- **Evolution communicating units** (two-stage and variable-speed) may display a fault on the Evolution thermostat, or show a flash code on the outdoor control board. Examples include anti-short-cycle delay, high or low pressure, or compressor protection. Variable-speed units use an inverter drive that has its own fault list.

Many Bryant controls add a 5-minute anti-short-cycle delay after power is restored or the thermostat is changed. A compressor that doesn't start immediately may simply be waiting out that delay.

The compressor's internal thermal overload resets automatically once it cools. Board-level lockouts on communicating models may hold until power is cycled. That behavior varies by model, so check the unit's literature.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run/start capacitor.** This is the most common cause on single-stage and two-stage units. The compressor hums but won't start, or it trips its overload.
2. **Failed or pitted contactor.** The contactor is the relay that sends power to the compressor. If its contacts are burned or pitted, or its coil has failed, the compressor never receives power.
3. **Compressor thermal overload tripped.** The compressor shut itself off because it overheated. This is usually caused by something else: a dirty condenser coil, low refrigerant charge, a failed outdoor fan, or a weak capacitor. The overload resets automatically after cooling.
4. **Pressure switch open.** Units that have a low-pressure switch (loss of charge) or a high-pressure switch will block the compressor if pressure is out of range. Low pressure points to a refrigerant leak. High pressure points to a dirty coil or a fan problem.
5. **Low-voltage control problem.** The compressor can't start if the 24V signal never reaches the outdoor unit. Causes include a thermostat setting, a damaged thermostat wire, a blown control-board fuse, or a tripped float or condensate safety switch indoors.
6. **Wiring or terminal damage.** Loose, burned or chewed wiring at the compressor terminals or in the electrical compartment.
7. **Inverter or drive fault (variable-speed Evolution models only).** The drive board protects the compressor and logs specific codes. A technician reads these on the thermostat or the board.
8. **Failed compressor.** Seized bearings, or open or shorted windings. This is the least common cause, but the most expensive. A technician should rule out the cheaper parts above before condemning the compressor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:**
  - Confirm it's set to COOL.
  - Set it at least 3°F below room temperature.
  - Set the fan to AUTO.
  - Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
  - Wait 5–10 minutes after any change, because the anti-short-cycle delay may be holding the compressor off.
- **Breakers and disconnect:**
  - Check that the AC breaker in your main panel is on, and that the outdoor disconnect switch or pull-out block near the unit is in place.
  - If a breaker has tripped, you may reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and see "Bryant AC Breaker Keeps Tripping."
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter can freeze the indoor coil and upset system pressures.
- **Condensate line:** Many systems have a float switch on the drain pan or drain line that cuts power to the AC when water backs up. If the pan is full or the line is visibly clogged, clear the line opening with a wet/dry vac from outside.
- **Outdoor unit airflow:**
  - Clear leaves, grass clippings and debris from around the unit. Keep about 2 feet of clearance.
  - Don't open any panels.
  - You can rinse the outside of the fins gently with a garden hose, with the power off at the disconnect.
- **Give it time once:** If the unit was hot and the compressor may have tripped its overload, turn the system off for an hour, then try one more time. If it still won't start, or it hums, turn it off and call a technician.

Don't press on the contactor, tap the capacitor, or open the electrical cover. Those parts carry 240V, and capacitors hold a charge even with the power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician will work through these steps:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling.** Check for 24V at the outdoor unit's control terminals (Y/C), or read the communication status on Evolution systems.
2. **Read fault history.** On Evolution systems, pull codes from the thermostat and the outdoor board LEDs. On variable-speed models, check the inverter drive fault log.
3. **Check line voltage.** Measure incoming 240V at the disconnect and contactor, and inspect fuses in the disconnect.
4. **Test the contactor.** Look for pitted contacts, check coil continuity, and confirm voltage drop across the closed contacts.
5. **Test the capacitor.** Measure microfarads against the rating on the label. A reading more than about 6% low (or outside the stated tolerance) means it should be replaced.
6. **Measure compressor windings.** Take resistance readings across Common, Run and Start, and check for a short to ground with a megohmmeter or meter. If the compressor is hot, the technician should wait for the internal overload to reset before condemning it.
7. **Check the pressure switches and refrigerant charge.** Read pressures with gauges, and look for leaks if the charge is low.
8. **Measure amp draw on startup.** Locked-rotor amps with no start suggests a seized compressor. A hard-start kit may help as a test or a temporary measure.

Be skeptical of a quote that jumps straight to "replace the compressor" without capacitor, contactor and winding tests.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor fan runs, compressor hums then stops | Weak or failed capacitor | Turn the system off | Test and replace the capacitor; consider a hard-start kit |
| Outdoor unit completely silent | No 24V signal, tripped breaker, or failed contactor | Check thermostat settings and batteries; reset the breaker once; check the disconnect | Test the control voltage, fuses and contactor |
| Clicking at the outdoor unit, no compressor start | Pitted contactor or low voltage | Turn the system off | Replace the contactor; check supply voltage |
| Compressor runs a few minutes, then shuts off while the fan keeps going | Thermal overload from a dirty coil, low charge or a fan issue | Clear debris; rinse the coil fins from outside with the power off | Clean the coil; check the charge and fan; find any leak |
| Unit won't start, water in the drain pan | Float/condensate safety switch tripped | Clear the condensate line | Check the drain and pump; verify the switch |
| Code shown on the Evolution thermostat | Pressure, drive or protection fault | Note the code; power-cycle once | Diagnose using Bryant's code chart for that model |
| Breaker trips when the compressor tries to start | Shorted or grounded compressor, or locked rotor | Don't keep resetting it | Test the windings; replace the compressor or the condenser |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $80–$200 |
| Run or dual capacitor replacement | $150–$450 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Hard-start kit | $200–$450 |
| Pressure switch replacement | $200–$500 (more if refrigerant work is needed) |
| Refrigerant leak search and repair, plus recharge | $300–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type: R-410A or R-454B) |
| Outdoor control board or inverter drive (Evolution) | $500–$1,500 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800–$3,500+ (parts may be covered under Bryant's 10-year limited warranty when the unit is registered; labor and refrigerant are usually extra) |
| Full condenser replacement | $3,500–$7,500+ (often considered when the unit is over 12–15 years old) |

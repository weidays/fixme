## What this code means

"Humming but not starting" is not a stored error code. It's a symptom: the outdoor condenser is getting 240V, but a motor can't begin turning. You'll hear a steady buzz or hum from the outdoor unit, while the fan sits still or the compressor never kicks on. After a while it often clicks off, then tries again later.

On a Goodman split AC, this almost always points to the start circuit for the compressor or fan motor. That circuit includes the run capacitor, the contactor, and the motors themselves.

Here is what happens while it hums:
- The stalled motor draws locked-rotor amps.
- The compressor's internal overload eventually opens to protect it.
- The overload resets automatically once the motor cools, which can take 30 to 60 minutes or more.
- There is no lockout to clear and no board code on standard single-stage models.

This page covers the humming-at-startup condition specifically. If your outdoor unit is completely silent, see "Goodman AC Won't Turn On." If the fan spins but the compressor doesn't, see "Goodman AC Compressor Not Running."

**Severity is pro-level.** Every likely cause sits inside the high-voltage electrical panel. Capacitors can hold a dangerous charge even with the power off.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak dual run capacitor.** This is by far the most common cause on Goodman condensers. A single round dual capacitor serves both the compressor (HERM terminal) and the fan (FAN terminal). Signs include:
   - A bulged or leaking top.
   - Capacitance below its rated tolerance, usually ±5–6%.
   - Heat-season failures are very common.

   If the fan hums and won't spin, suspect the FAN side. If the compressor hums and trips, suspect the HERM side.

2. **Pitted or welded contactor contacts.** Burned contacts can pass enough voltage to make a motor hum but not enough current to start it. On single-pole Goodman contactors, one leg stays energized at all times, which can also cause a faint hum while the unit is idle.

3. **Hard-starting compressor.** An aging compressor with worn bearings or high head pressure at startup can struggle even with a good capacitor. Technicians often add a hard-start kit as a fix, but that is a remedy, not a diagnosis.

4. **Seized condenser fan motor.** If the fan bearings are dry or seized, the motor hums but can't turn. Overlap: if the fan never hums and simply sits still, see "Goodman AC Outdoor Fan Not Spinning."

5. **Locked-rotor or failed compressor.** This means a mechanically seized compressor or a winding fault, such as an open start winding or a short to ground. It's less common, but it's the expensive outcome. Running repeatedly on a bad capacitor can cause it.

6. **Low supply voltage or loose high-voltage connections.** A loose lug, a corroded disconnect, or a voltage drop under load can prevent startup. If this is tripping the breaker, see "Goodman AC Breaker Keeps Tripping."

How this varies by model:
- Some older Goodman units use separate compressor and fan capacitors instead of a dual capacitor.
- Two-stage and inverter models (DSXC, GSXV) use control boards and drives, so diagnosis follows the board's fault codes instead.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These steps can't fix a bad capacitor, but they rule out simple issues and protect the equipment.

- **Turn the system OFF at the thermostat now.** Stopping the hum prevents overheating the compressor and fan motor. This is the most important step.
- **Check thermostat settings and batteries.** Confirm it's set to Cool with a setpoint below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim.
- **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter won't directly cause humming, but restricted airflow adds strain to a struggling system.
- **Check the breaker once.** If the AC breaker is tripped, reset it one time. If it trips again, leave it off and call a technician.
- **Look, don't touch.** From outside the unit, make sure nothing is jamming the fan blade, such as sticks or debris visible through the top grille. Clear leaves from around the outside of the condenser by hand. Don't reach through the grille.
- **Check that exterior panels are seated.** The service panel should be in place. Don't open it.
- **Note what you hear.** Does the fan hum, the compressor hum, or both? How long does it hum before clicking off? Is there a burning smell? This information helps the technician.

Don't push the fan blade with a stick, don't tap the capacitor, and don't open the electrical panel. Those common internet tricks expose you to 240V and stored capacitor charge.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will typically do the following:

1. **Lock out power and safely discharge the capacitor** before touching anything.
2. **Inspect the capacitor** for a bulge or leaks, then **measure its microfarads** on both the HERM and FAN sides and compare them to the label.
3. **Inspect the contactor** for pitting, check the coil voltage (24V), and measure voltage across the contacts under load.
4. **Measure supply voltage** at the disconnect and contactor during an attempted start, looking for voltage drop.
5. **Check the compressor windings** (C-S, C-R, S-R) for opens and shorts to ground. An open winding may just be a tripped internal overload, so a hot compressor needs time to cool before it's condemned.
6. **Spin the fan by hand with power off** to check for bearing drag, and check the motor's amp draw.
7. **Measure amp draw on startup.** Locked-rotor amps that never drop off indicate a stuck compressor. If the compressor runs with a new capacitor but struggles, the tech may recommend a hard-start kit.

Red flags in a quote:
- Condemning the compressor without winding and amp readings.
- Recommending system replacement before trying a $20–$60 capacitor that tested weak.
- Selling refrigerant when nothing indicates a leak. Charge has nothing to do with a motor that won't turn.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor unit hums, fan doesn't spin, compressor silent | Failed dual run capacitor (FAN or both sides) | Turn off at thermostat; check breaker once | Test and replace capacitor |
| Loud hum, clicks off after a minute, repeats later | Compressor stalling on bad capacitor, tripping overload | Shut system off to let it cool | Test HERM side of capacitor, windings, and amps |
| Faint hum while AC is idle, unit won't start on call | Pitted or stuck contactor | Shut off; note the behavior | Replace contactor |
| Fan hums, blade is stiff or hard to turn | Seized condenser fan motor | Clear visible debris outside only | Replace fan motor and capacitor |
| Hums with a new capacitor; high startup amps | Hard-starting or locked compressor | None | Hard-start kit trial; compressor diagnosis |
| Hums, lights dim, breaker trips | Locked rotor or grounded compressor | Reset breaker once only | Megger and winding test; compressor or condenser replacement |
| Bulged capacitor visible through grille | Failed capacitor | Do not touch | Replace capacitor |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic or service call | $75 – $200 |
| Dual run capacitor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Hard-start kit installed | $200 – $450 |
| Condenser fan motor replacement | $350 – $750 |
| Compressor replacement (in warranty, labor plus refrigerant) | $1,200 – $2,500 |
| Compressor replacement (out of warranty) | $1,800 – $3,500+ |
| Full condenser replacement | $3,000 – $6,500+ |

Cost tips:
- Goodman's parts warranty, often 10 years when registered, can cover the part itself. You'll usually still pay for labor.
- Replacing the capacitor and contactor together is common on older units and can save a second trip.

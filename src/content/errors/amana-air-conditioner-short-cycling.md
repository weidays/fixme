## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a numbered Amana fault code. It is a symptom: the air conditioner starts, runs only a few minutes, shuts off, and then restarts soon after, over and over. Usually the house never reaches setpoint comfortably, or it feels cool but clammy.

Many Amana systems include a built-in anti-short-cycle delay of roughly 3 to 5 minutes. The delay can come from the thermostat, from a timer, or from the outdoor control board. Outdoor control boards are found on two-stage ComfortBridge (e.g., ASXC-style), inverter (e.g., AVXC-style), and other communicating models. This delay protects the compressor, so a short pause after shutdown is normal.

Short cycling becomes a problem when one of these keeps ending the cycle early:

- **The thermostat**, by misreading the temperature or losing power.
- **A safety device**, such as a high- or low-pressure switch or the compressor's internal overload, tripping the unit.
- **The system itself**, when it is mismatched to the house.

Whether the unit restarts on its own depends on the cause:

- **Thermostat-driven cycles and internal overloads** reset automatically. An overload trip can take 15 to 60 minutes to clear.
- **Pressure switches** reset automatically on many models.
- **Outdoor control boards** on some two-stage communicating or inverter models may lock out after repeated trips. They hold the lockout until power is cycled.

On ComfortBridge and other communicating Amana models, the outdoor control stores a fault history. A technician can retrieve that history, which often shows why the unit keeps shutting down. Single-stage models without a control board do not keep this record.

If your unit runs but blows warm air, see the related "not cooling" and "fan runs but no cold air" pages instead. This page covers the on/off cycling itself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat problems.** The most common causes are:
   - weak batteries;
   - a thermostat mounted near a supply register, in sun, or above a heat source;
   - a cycle-rate or "swing" setting that is too tight;
   - a loose thermostat wire.
2. **Restricted airflow, usually a clogged air filter.** Low airflow drops evaporator pressure and coil temperature. Depending on the model, this can cause three different problems:
   - The low-pressure switch trips, on models that have one.
   - The coil freezes. A frozen coil has its own page.
   - A coil or freeze sensor stops the compressor until the coil warms, on models equipped with one.
3. **Oversized equipment.** The unit satisfies the thermostat too quickly, often before it removes humidity. This is one of the most common real-world causes. It often happens when a system was replaced "one size up" without a load calculation. Suspect oversizing in homes where cycles are short but the thermostat, airflow, charge, and electrical components all test normal.
4. **Dirty or blocked outdoor condenser coil.** Debris, grass clippings, or plants crowding the condenser raise head pressure. This can trip the high-pressure switch or the compressor's thermal overload.
5. **Low refrigerant charge (a leak).** Low charge makes suction pressure fall, so the low-pressure switch opens. It also makes the compressor run hot and trip its internal overload.
6. **Failing electrical start components (technician only).** A weak run capacitor, a pitted contactor, or a failing compressor can overheat and trip on overload. These shutdowns are often followed by long "off" periods.
7. **Condensate safety float switch tripping.** Some systems have a float switch in the drain pan or line. A partial clog can make the switch open and close intermittently. What shuts off depends on how the switch was wired at installation:
   - If it interrupts the cooling call (Y), only the outdoor unit stops and the indoor fan may keep running.
   - If it interrupts the 24V supply (R), the whole system shuts off.
8. **Control board or sensor faults on communicating models.** This applies to two-stage ComfortBridge (e.g., ASXC-style) and inverter (e.g., AVXC-style) models. A faulty board, a sensor such as a discharge or coil thermistor, or a communication fault can stop the compressor early. These models typically log a fault code on the outdoor board LEDs or the communicating thermostat.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Check the thermostat.**
  - Replace the batteries if it uses them.
  - Make sure it is set to COOL with the fan on AUTO.
  - Look for direct sun, a nearby lamp or TV, or a supply register blowing on it.
  - If your thermostat has a cycle-rate or temperature differential setting, check the manual. A setting that is too tight causes frequent cycling.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it is gray, matted, or past its change date, replace it. Install the new one with the airflow arrow pointing toward the furnace or air handler.
- **Open supply registers and clear the returns.** Keep at least most registers open, and move furniture or rugs away from return grilles.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from outside the cabinet.**
  - Clear leaves, grass, and plants so there are about 2 feet of open space around it.
  - Gently hose the outside of the fins from the outside only, with the power off at the disconnect.
  - Do not open the unit's panels.
- **Check the condensate line.** If you can see a drain pan with water in it, or your system has an accessible condensate line, clear the drain per your installer's guidance. A wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain end works well. Once the line drains, a tripped float switch should reset.
- **Check breakers and the disconnect.** Confirm the breakers are fully on and the outdoor disconnect is firmly seated.
- **Do one reset only.** If you suspect a lockout:
  1. Turn the system off at the thermostat.
  2. Switch off the AC breaker for 5 minutes.
  3. Restore power.
  4. Wait out the startup delay.

  If short cycling returns, stop and call a technician. Repeated resets can damage the compressor. On communicating models, a reset may also make the cause harder to see at a glance. The stored fault history is usually still available to the technician, but note any code shown on the thermostat before you reset.
- **Confirm the exterior panels are securely in place** on both the indoor and outdoor units.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically:

- **Watch a full cycle and time it.** They will note whether the indoor blower, the outdoor fan, and the compressor all stop together, or whether only the compressor drops out (which points to an overload or pressure switch).
- **Verify the thermostat.** They check the 24V call on Y, the wiring, the thermostat location, and the cycle settings.
- **Check airflow.** This includes static pressure, filter and coil condition, and blower speed setup.
- **Connect gauges or digital probes.** They read suction and discharge pressures, superheat, and subcooling to confirm the charge and spot a restriction. If the charge is low, a leak search should come before any recharge.
- **Test the electrical components.** They check the capacitor (in microfarads), the contactor, compressor amperage, and winding resistance to identify overload trips.
- **Test the pressure switches and float switch** for continuity and proper operation. They also note how the float switch is wired.
- **Read the stored fault history** on two-stage ComfortBridge (e.g., ASXC-style), inverter (e.g., AVXC-style), or other communicating models. They use the outdoor board LEDs or the communicating thermostat, then check the relevant sensors.
- **Evaluate sizing** if everything tests normal. That means comparing equipment tonnage with a Manual J load calculation.

**Red flags on a quote:** adding refrigerant without looking for a leak, or recommending a new compressor without electrical test results.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cycles every few minutes; thermostat screen blank or flickering | Weak batteries / loose thermostat wiring | Replace batteries; confirm settings | Check thermostat wiring and 24V supply |
| Short cycles only on sunny afternoons | Thermostat location or heat source nearby | Shade or move heat sources; consider relocation | Relocate the thermostat |
| Short cycles, weak airflow at registers | Clogged filter / blocked returns | Replace filter; open registers | Check blower, coil, and static pressure |
| Short cycles, but thermostat, airflow, charge, and electrical all test normal; home feels damp and cold | Oversized unit | None beyond thermostat settings | Load calculation; airflow or staging adjustment; possible replacement |
| Outdoor unit stops, then stays off a long time before restarting | Compressor overload: dirty coil, bad capacitor, low charge | Clear debris around condenser; rinse the outside of the fins | Test capacitor/contactor, check charge, clean coil |
| Cycles with ice on suction line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter; turn system off to thaw | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water in drain pan; the outdoor unit stops (indoor fan may keep running) or the whole system shuts off, depending on how the switch is wired | Condensate float switch tripping | Clear condensate line | Clean pan or drain, verify float switch and its wiring |
| Two-stage communicating or inverter unit stops with an LED code on outdoor board or thermostat | Sensor or board fault | One power reset; note the code | Read fault history; diagnose by code; replace sensor or board |

|---|---|

| Pressure switch | $150–$450; higher if the switch is not on a Schrader fitting and refrigerant must be recovered and recharged |
| Leak search and repair plus recharge (R-410A or R-454B) | $300–$1,500+, depending on leak location |

**Check your warranty before paying for a major repair.** Qualifying Amana models may carry a unit-replacement limited warranty in addition to parts coverage. Coverage depends on the model and on registration, so check your registration and warranty terms before approving a compressor replacement.

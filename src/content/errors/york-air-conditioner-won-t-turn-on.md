## What this code means

"Won't turn on" is not a stored fault code. It means the system shows no response to a cooling call: the indoor blower does not start, the outdoor unit stays silent, and the thermostat may be blank. This page covers that **no-response** situation.

**Who can fix it:** Most of the likely causes are things you can check and often fix yourself. These include thermostat settings and batteries, a tripped breaker, a switched-off service switch, an unlatched blower door, and a clogged condensate drain. Only the steps inside the cabinet need a technician: the low-voltage fuse, the 24V transformer, wiring faults and the control board. This page is rated "pro" because those faults are common enough that many homeowners will end up needing one.

If any part of the system runs, a different guide fits better:

- **Indoor blower runs but the outdoor unit doesn't:** see York AC Fan Runs But No Cold Air or [York AC Compressor Not Running](/error/york-air-conditioner-compressor-not-running/).
- **System runs but the air is warm:** see [York AC Not Cooling](/error/york-air-conditioner-not-cooling/).

On a York split system, the low-voltage (24V) control power comes from a transformer in the indoor York/Johnson Controls furnace or air handler. The thermostat and the outdoor contactor both depend on it. That is why most total "won't turn on" failures start indoors, not at the condenser.

How the system recovers depends on the cause:

- **Power interruption:** The system usually restarts by itself once power returns, often after a built-in compressor delay of about 3–5 minutes.
- **Blown low-voltage fuse or open float switch:** The system stays off until the cause is cleared. A blown fuse also has to be replaced.
- **Indoor board LED:** Many York and Johnson Controls furnace control boards have a diagnostic LED. The meaning of each flash pattern is printed on the label inside the furnace or air handler door. Whether your board has an LED, and what the patterns mean, depends on your indoor model. If you can see the LED without removing a panel, note the flash pattern and tell your technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat issues** *(DIY check)*. Possible causes:
   - dead batteries
   - the thermostat set to Heat or Off
   - a cooling setpoint above room temperature
   - a failed thermostat
2. **Power switched off or tripped** *(DIY check)*. Check these:
   - the indoor unit's service switch (it often looks like a light switch)
   - the furnace or air handler breaker
   - the outdoor unit's breaker
   - the outdoor disconnect
3. **Condensate float or overflow switch tripped** *(DIY check)*. A clogged drain line or a full secondary pan opens the 24V circuit. This applies only where a float switch is installed.
4. **Blown low-voltage fuse on the indoor control board** *(technician)*. On York/JCI furnace and air handler boards, this is usually a 3A or 5A automotive-style fuse plugged into the control board behind the blower door. It is typically blown by a shorted thermostat wire, for example one chafed against sheet metal or nicked by yard work at the outdoor line.
5. **Blower door safety switch open** *(DIY check)*. The furnace or air handler door is not seated, so its interlock cuts power.
6. **Failed 24V transformer** in the indoor unit *(technician)*.
7. **Damaged or loose thermostat wiring** between the thermostat, the indoor board, and the condenser *(technician)*.
8. **Failed indoor control board** *(technician)*. Less common, and diagnosed only after the items above are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Thermostat.** Set it to **Cool**, lower the setpoint 3°F or more below room temperature, and set the fan to Auto. Replace the batteries if it has them, even if the screen looks fine.
2. **Breakers.** At the main panel, find the breakers for the furnace/air handler and the AC/condenser. Reset any tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
3. **Switches.** Confirm the service switch near the indoor unit is on. Also check that the outdoor disconnect is in place and switched on. Only operate it as designed; do not open the disconnect box.
4. **Exterior panels.** Make sure the furnace or air handler door is fully seated and latched. Do not remove panels to look inside.
5. **Condensate drain.**
   - Look at the drain pan and any float switch on the drain line or secondary pan.
   - If there is standing water, clear the drain line from the outdoor or utility end, or the access tee, using a wet/dry vac.
   - Give the float time to drop and reset.
6. **Air filter.** Replace it if it is dirty. A clogged filter doesn't usually stop the system from starting, but it causes freeze-ups that can trip a float switch.
7. **Wait.** Give the system 5–10 minutes after any reset because of the compressor delay.

If the thermostat is still blank, or nothing happens after these checks, call a technician. Do not open the indoor cabinet to check fuses or wiring.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A technician typically works through these steps:

1. **Line voltage.** Verifies 120V or 240V at the indoor unit and 240V at the condenser disconnect.
2. **Indoor board and transformer.** Reads the board's diagnostic LED, if equipped, against the flash chart on the door label. Then measures the transformer's 24V secondary output.
3. **Board fuse.** Inspects the low-voltage fuse on the control board. If it is blown, they look for the short that caused it before replacing it. A tech who only swaps the fuse may see it blow again.
4. **Thermostat signal.** Jumps R to Y and G at the board to separate a thermostat or wiring fault from an equipment fault.
5. **Safety circuits.** Checks continuity through the float switch and the door switch.
6. **Outdoor side.** Confirms the condenser contactor coil receives 24V when cooling is called.
7. **Board condemnation.** Replaces the control board only if it has power in but no outputs.

**Typical diagnostic fee:** $90–$200.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries, no 24V | Replace batteries; check switch and breaker | Test transformer and board fuse |
| Thermostat on, nothing runs | Wrong mode or setpoint, open float switch | Set to Cool, lower setpoint, clear drain | Check R-Y-G signal and safety circuit |
| Water in pan or near unit, AC dead | Float switch tripped | Clear condensate line, wait for reset | Clear drain trap, verify switch |
| Breaker tripped | Electrical fault | Reset once only | Find the short or failed component |
| Worked after fuse change, then died again | Shorted thermostat wire | None | Locate and repair wiring |
| Indoor runs, outdoor silent | Contactor, capacitor, outdoor fault | Check outdoor breaker | See [Compressor Not Running](/error/york-air-conditioner-compressor-not-running/) guide |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including labor, for this problem:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Thermostat batteries / settings | $0–$10 |
| Clearing a clogged condensate line (pro) | $75–$250 |
| Float switch replacement | $120–$250 |
| Low-voltage fuse plus finding the short | $100–$300 |
| Thermostat wire repair or replacement | $150–$450 |
| 24V transformer | $150–$350 |
| Door safety switch | $120–$250 |
| New thermostat (DIY) | $30–$250 |
| New thermostat (installed) | $150–$450 |

## What this code means

Bryant Furnace **Code 24** means the **secondary voltage fuse is open** — the small 3-amp low-voltage fuse on the furnace control board has blown. That fuse protects the 24-volt (low-voltage) circuit that powers the thermostat, control board logic, and accessories like humidifiers or zone panels.

A blown 3A fuse is **almost always caused by a short in the 24V wiring** — most often the thermostat cable or an add-on accessory — that pulled too much current through the fuse. When the fuse opens, the control board loses its power supply, so the **status LED may be completely dark** rather than flashing a pattern.

Because the fix requires finding *why* the fuse blew — not just swapping it — Code 24 is a **professional repair**. Replacing the fuse alone almost always ends with a second blown fuse within seconds.

The code does **not auto-reset**; the fuse must be physically replaced and the short corrected before the board powers back up.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Shorted thermostat wire** — the most common cause. A staple through the cable, a chafed wire behind a wall, or two bare conductors touching at the thermostat terminals sends R directly to C or ground and pops the fuse.
2. **Shorted accessory wiring** — a humidifier, electronic air cleaner, zone control board, or condensate overflow switch wired to the 24V circuit with a pinched or crossed wire.
3. **Loose strands at a terminal** — a stray copper whisker at the thermostat, board, or accessory terminal bridging two contacts.
4. **Damaged or pinched wire inside the cabinet** — a low-voltage lead crushed under a panel or rubbing on sheet metal after a previous service.
5. **Failed accessory or relay** — an internally shorted humidifier solenoid, relay coil, or transformer-fed device pulling excess current.
6. **Control board fault** — least common; a board-level short that blows the fuse. Confirmed only after all external wiring is ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should attempt with Code 24:

- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's set to Heat and calling for heat. If the screen is blank on a battery-powered stat, **replace the batteries** — a dead thermostat can look like a power issue.
- **Note recent changes.** Did a new smart thermostat, humidifier, or zone panel get installed recently? Tell your technician — new accessory wiring is a leading cause of this code.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm the furnace breaker is on and the furnace service switch (the light-switch-style toggle near the unit) is on. Reset the breaker **once** if it's tripped.
- **Replace a dirty air filter** — good practice, though not a cause of this code.
- **Check visible vents and registers** are open and unblocked.
- **Check the condensate drain/line** for standing water on high-efficiency models — a full overflow switch can be on the 24V circuit.

**Do not** open the cabinet to inspect or replace the fuse, and do not repeatedly cycle the breaker hoping it clears. If the fuse is blown, there is a short that must be found by a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. **Power down** the furnace and open the blower compartment to confirm the 3A fuse is open (visually or with a meter).
2. **Disconnect the thermostat wire** at the board and install a known-good fuse. If the new fuse holds with the stat wire off, the short is in the thermostat cable or the stat itself.
3. **Isolate accessories** — disconnect humidifier, EAC, and zone-panel leads one at a time to see which circuit blows the fuse.
4. **Ohm out the low-voltage wiring** for shorts to ground or between conductors, inspecting for staples, pinch points, and chafed insulation along the cable run.
5. **Inspect terminal connections** at the board, thermostat, and accessories for stray strands or crossed wires.
6. **Test the control board** only after external wiring is cleared, to rule out a board-level short.
7. **Replace the fuse**, correct the short, and verify the 24V circuit measures normal (around 24–28 VAC) with the board powered.

A quote that includes only "replaced fuse" without any wiring diagnosis is a red flag — the fuse will likely blow again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Furnace dead, board LED dark | Open 3A secondary fuse | Verify breaker/switch on; call a pro | Confirm blown fuse, find short, replace fuse |
| Fuse blows again right after replacement | Active short in thermostat wire | Note recent thermostat changes | Isolate & repair/replace stat cable |
| Started after new humidifier/zone install | Shorted accessory wiring | Report the recent install | Disconnect & correct accessory wiring |
| Blank thermostat screen | Dead stat batteries or no 24V | Replace batteries | Verify 24V supply if batteries don't fix it |
| Intermittent no-heat, then dead | Chafed/pinched wire making occasional short | None | Inspect cable runs, repair damaged wire |
| No heat after recent service | Wire pinched under a panel | Reseat exterior panel if loose | Locate & repair crushed low-voltage lead |

## Repair costs

| Repair | Typical US cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $75 – $150 |
| Replace 3A fuse only (part) | $2 – $10 |
| Locate & repair shorted thermostat/accessory wire | $120 – $300 |
| Replace run of thermostat cable | $150 – $350 |
| Replace shorted accessory (humidifier solenoid, relay) | $75 – $250 |
| Replace control board (if board-level short) | $350 – $700 |

Most Code 24 calls land in the **$120 – $300** range because the labor is in *finding* the short, not the inexpensive fuse. A rare board replacement pushes it higher. Get an itemized quote showing wiring diagnosis, not just a fuse swap.

## What this code means

This isn't a numbered fault code. It's a symptom: the compressor or contactor in your Goodman outdoor condensing unit gets a cooling call, but the large fan on top of the unit doesn't spin.

The condenser fan pulls air across the outdoor coil to carry away the heat removed from your home. Without it:

- Head pressure and compressor temperature climb fast.
- The compressor usually trips on its internal overload within minutes.
- Indoors, you get warm or barely cool air from the vents while the indoor blower keeps running.

On most Goodman single-stage and two-stage condensers, the fan uses a PSC motor. That motor is powered through the contactor and needs a run capacitor to start, usually one side of a dual capacitor shared with the compressor.

On variable-speed communicating models (such as the DSXC series), an ECM fan motor is commanded by the outdoor control board. Those units can show fault codes on the board or the communicating thermostat. Check the diagnostic label inside your unit's panel or your installation manual for model-specific codes.

If your unit is a heat pump rather than a cooling-only AC, see the separate heat pump page. Defrost behavior there intentionally stops the fan at times.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor (fan side of the dual capacitor).** This is the most common cause on Goodman condensers.
   - Typical signs: the compressor runs or hums but the fan doesn't turn.
   - The fan may also start only after it's spun by hand.
   - A bulging or leaking capacitor top is a visual clue to the technician.
2. **Failed condenser fan motor.** Common causes are worn bearings, a seized shaft, an open winding, or a tripped internal overload.
   - A motor that's hot to the touch, hums, or turns stiffly points here.
   - Motors often fail after a capacitor has been weak for a while.
3. **Contactor problems.** A pitted or burned contactor, or one that isn't pulling in, can leave the fan without power. Usually the compressor also won't run in this case.
4. **Wiring faults.** Possible faults include a burned or loose fan lead, a chewed wire (rodents and insects are common), or a corroded connection at the capacitor or contactor.
5. **Obstruction of the fan blade.** Sticks, debris, ice, or a bent blade striking the guard can all jam the blade.
6. **Control board or ECM fault (communicating or variable-speed models only).** The board isn't commanding the fan, or the ECM motor or its module has failed. Behavior and codes are model-specific.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These checks don't involve opening the outdoor unit's electrical panel. Don't put hands, sticks, or tools into the fan guard.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL with the setpoint at least 3–4°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Breakers and disconnect:** Check the AC breaker in your main panel. Also check the outdoor disconnect switch or pull-out near the unit; make sure it's fully on and seated.
  - If the breaker tripped, reset it **once**.
  - If it trips again, leave it off and call a technician.
- **Air filter:** A badly clogged filter won't stop the outdoor fan. It does strain the system and can cause icing, so replace it if it's dirty.
- **Look from the outside only:** With the system off at the thermostat, look through the top grille for obvious debris (branches, leaves, toys) resting on the blade.
  - Clear debris from around the unit's sides, keeping about 2 feet of clearance.
  - Don't reach inside the guard.
- **Exterior panels:** Make sure the outdoor unit's service panel is fully seated and screwed down.
- **Then shut it off:** If the fan still won't spin when the system calls for cooling, set the thermostat to OFF to protect the compressor. Then schedule service.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Use this to sanity-check what you're told and quoted:

1. **Confirm the cooling call.** The tech verifies 24V at the contactor coil and that the contactor pulls in.
2. **Check voltage to the motor.** Line voltage is measured at the contactor's load side and at the fan motor leads.
3. **Test the capacitor.** The tech discharges the capacitor safely, then measures microfarads on the FAN terminal against its rating. Failure is typically a reading outside about ±6%, or a visibly bulged case.
4. **Check the motor.** This includes spinning the blade by hand with power off to feel for seized bearings, checking winding resistance, and checking for an open internal overload or grounded winding.
5. **Inspect wiring and the contactor.** The tech looks for burned terminals, pitted contacts, and damaged leads.
6. **Communicating or variable-speed units.** The tech reads fault codes on the outdoor board and checks the ECM motor's power and communication signals per Goodman's service instructions.
7. **Check for compressor damage.** After the repair, the tech confirms the compressor runs normally and checks amps and pressures to make sure it wasn't damaged by overheating.

A good tech replaces a weak capacitor even when the motor is the main failure, because the two often fail together. Ask to see the meter reading.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums or runs, fan still | Failed fan side of run capacitor | Turn system off at thermostat | Test and replace dual run capacitor |
| Fan starts only after it's nudged | Weak capacitor or failing motor bearings | Don't touch the blade; shut off | Test capacitor uF; check motor bearings and replace as needed |
| Fan hums, motor hot, won't turn | Seized or overheated fan motor | Shut off; let unit cool | Replace condenser fan motor (and usually capacitor) |
| Nothing outside runs, indoor blower runs | Contactor not pulling in, tripped breaker, or disconnect off | Check breaker and disconnect; reset once | Diagnose 24V control circuit; replace contactor if needed |
| Fan blade blocked or clicking on guard | Debris or bent blade | Clear debris visible from outside without reaching in | Remove guard; clear blade or replace bent blade |
| Burned smell at outdoor unit | Burned wiring or motor winding | Shut off at breaker | Repair wiring; replace motor |
| Communicating thermostat shows outdoor fault | ECM motor or control board fault | Note the code; power-cycle once | Diagnose per Goodman service manual; replace ECM or board |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call and diagnosis | $80 – $200 (often credited toward the repair) |
| Dual run capacitor replacement | $120 – $350 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Wiring repair | $100 – $300 |
| Condenser fan motor, PSC (often with new capacitor) | $350 – $750 |
| Fan blade replacement | $150 – $350 |
| ECM fan motor, variable-speed models | $600 – $1,200+ |
| Outdoor control board, communicating models | $400 – $900+ |

Goodman's parts warranty (often 10 years with registration) may cover the motor or capacitor. Labor usually isn't covered unless you have a labor plan.

If the compressor was damaged by running without the fan, costs rise sharply. That risk is why you should shut the system off promptly.

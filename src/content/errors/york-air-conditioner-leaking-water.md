## What this code means

"Leaking water" is not a fault code. York residential split-system air conditioners do not display a code for water leaks. It describes a symptom: condensate is escaping somewhere instead of draining away.

Every York AC produces water at the indoor evaporator coil, which sits on top of your furnace or inside an air handler. That water drips into a primary drain pan. From there, it flows through a PVC condensate line to a drain, to the outdoors, or to a condensate pump. A leak means something in that path is blocked, cracked, disconnected or overwhelmed.

Many installations include a float or overflow switch in the pan or drain line. When water rises, the switch cuts power to the AC. If your system suddenly stops cooling and there is water near the indoor unit, a tripped float switch may be the reason. These switches reset on their own once the water drains below the trip level, so the AC will not restart until the water is cleared.

This page covers water from the AC's cooling side. If water appears only in heating season, see York Furnace Leaking Water instead. High-efficiency furnaces produce their own condensate.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged condensate drain line.** This is by far the most common cause. Algae, sludge, dust and debris build up in the line and trap, and water backs up into the pan until it overflows.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil thawing.** A dirty filter, blocked return or closed registers starve the coil of airflow, and low refrigerant can also freeze it. When the ice melts, it can produce more water than the pan or drain can handle, or water can drip off iced areas outside the pan. See York AC Frozen Evaporator Coil.
3. **Failed or unplugged condensate pump.** In basements, attics and closets without gravity drainage, a small pump lifts the water out. If the motor fails, the float sticks, the pump loses power or its check valve clogs, the pump reservoir overflows.
4. **Cracked, rusted or corroded drain pan.** Older steel pans rust through, and plastic pans can crack with age and heat cycling. This is more common on systems over 10–15 years old.
5. **Disconnected or poorly sloped drain line.** A fitting can separate at the coil outlet, or the line can sag or lack a proper trap. These problems often show up soon after installation or after other work near the unit.
6. **Overflowing or clogged secondary (auxiliary) pan.** On attic and upstairs units, a secondary pan sits under the equipment. If it is clogged or its drain is blocked, ceiling stains appear.
7. **Unit not level or coil misaligned.** If the furnace or air handler is tilted, water collects at the wrong end of the pan, away from the drain outlet.
8. **Sweating ductwork or refrigerant lines.** Uninsulated suction lines or ducts in humid spaces can drip condensation that looks like a unit leak.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the AC off at the thermostat.** Set the mode to Off to stop new condensate. If you suspect a frozen coil, set the fan to On with cooling off to help it thaw.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A clogged filter is the leading cause of coil freezing. Install a new filter of the correct size, with the airflow arrow pointing toward the unit.
- **Open supply registers and clear return grilles.** Make sure furniture, rugs or curtains are not blocking them.
- **Find the condensate line outlet.** Follow the PVC pipe from the indoor unit. It may end outdoors near the foundation, at a floor drain or at a pump. If no water comes out while the AC runs, the line is likely clogged.
- **Clear the drain line.** Many York installations have a capped vent or cleanout tee near the coil. Remove the cap and slowly pour in about one cup of plain white vinegar, or a mix of warm water and dish soap. You can also use a wet/dry vacuum on the outdoor end for 1–2 minutes to pull the clog out. Do not use bleach on PVC fittings repeatedly, and do not mix chemicals.
- **Check the condensate pump.** Confirm it is plugged in, that the outlet has power, and that the GFCI is not tripped. If the reservoir is full and the pump does not run, it needs service or replacement.
- **Look at the secondary pan, if visible.** On attic units, empty standing water with a wet/dry vacuum and note whether it keeps refilling.
- **Seat panels and restore power.** Make sure the blower door and access panels are fully closed. After the water has cleared, the float switch should reset and the system can be turned back on. If it leaks or shuts off again within a day or two, call a technician.

Do not open the furnace or air handler cabinet, remove the coil access panel, or touch wiring or refrigerant lines.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically do the following:

- Confirm the water source: primary pan, drain connection, pump, secondary pan, sweating lines or ducts.
- Inspect and flush the condensate line and trap. They may use nitrogen, a pump-out tool or a drain line blower. A well-built trap should also have a vent.
- Remove the coil access panel to inspect the primary pan for rust, cracks or sludge. They will also check the coil for ice, dirt or microbial growth.
- Test the float or overflow switch for correct operation, and check that it is wired to interrupt cooling.
- Check the condensate pump's motor, float, check valve and discharge tubing.
- Measure airflow, static pressure and filter condition. If the coil was frozen, they will check refrigerant charge using superheat and subcooling, and look for leaks.
- Verify that the unit and coil are level and that the drain line slopes properly, at least 1/4 inch per foot.

Be cautious if a quote jumps to coil replacement without showing you a rusted-through pan or a confirmed coil leak. Also question a refrigerant recharge without a leak search. Most leaks are resolved by drain cleaning, a pump or a pan.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water pooling around the indoor unit, no water at the drain outlet | Clogged condensate line | Flush with vinegar at the cleanout, or wet/dry vac the outlet | Pressure-flush line, rebuild trap, add a cleanout |
| AC stopped cooling and there is water in the pan | Float switch tripped by backup | Clear the drain, let the water drop, restart once | Verify switch wiring, clear the line |
| Ice on the copper lines, then water when off | Frozen evaporator coil | Replace filter, open vents, run the fan only to thaw | Check charge and airflow, repair the refrigerant leak |
| Pump reservoir full, pump silent | Failed or unpowered condensate pump | Check the plug, outlet and GFCI | Replace the pump or clear its check valve |
| Leak continues even with a clear drain | Rusted or cracked primary pan | Turn the AC off and contain the water | Replace the pan or coil, or install a pan liner |
| Ceiling stain under an attic unit | Secondary pan overflowing or clogged | Vacuum out the pan, check its drain | Clear the pan drain, add a pan float switch |
| Water at a pipe joint near the unit | Disconnected or poorly sloped drain | Note the location, keep the system off | Reglue fittings, re-slope or support the line |
| Dripping along lines or ducts, not the unit | Missing insulation, condensation | Place a towel or bucket to catch drips | Insulate the suction line or ducts |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor, vary by region and access difficulty:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| DIY drain flush (vinegar, wet/dry vac) | $0–$20 |
| Professional drain line clearing | $100–$250 |
| Float or overflow switch install or replace | $100–$250 |
| Condensate pump replacement | $150–$400 |
| Drain line repair, re-slope or trap rebuild | $150–$450 |
| Secondary drain pan replacement | $150–$500 |
| Primary drain pan replacement | $300–$900 (labor-heavy; the coil often must be pulled) |
| Refrigerant leak repair and recharge (frozen coil cause) | $300–$1,500+ |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$2,500+ |
| Water damage repair (drywall, ceiling) | $250–$1,500+, separate from HVAC |

Check whether your York equipment is still under its parts warranty, which is typically 10 years with registration. Coils and pans may be covered, although labor usually is not.

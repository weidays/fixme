## What this code means

"Ice on indoor coil" is not a display code. It means frost or ice is forming on the evaporator coil inside the wall-mounted head. You may see white frost behind the louvers, notice weak or misty airflow, or find water dripping after the ice melts.

The coil freezes when it gets colder than 32°F. That happens for two main reasons:

- **Too little warm air crosses the coil**, so it can't pick up enough heat.
- **The system's refrigerant pressure is too low**, which drops the coil temperature too far.

Most Trane mini-splits have indoor-coil temperature (anti-freeze) protection. It briefly stops the compressor when the coil sensor reads too cold, then restarts it automatically. How aggressive this protection is, and whether it ever triggers a fault code, varies by model. Repeated icing means the root cause needs a professional diagnosis.

This page covers ductless heads specifically. For ducted Trane systems, see Frozen Evaporator Coil.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty indoor filters.** Mini-split mesh filters clog with dust and pet hair quickly. This is the most common cause of reduced airflow and icing.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil or blower wheel.** Even with clean filters, a coil matted with dust or a blower wheel caked with grime chokes airflow. Biofilm on the "squirrel cage" wheel is common in humid climates.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Leaks often start at the flare fittings on the line set, or at the coil itself. Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature.
4. **Running cooling in low outdoor or indoor temperatures.** Cooling or Dry mode at very low setpoints (for example 60–62°F), with low fan speed or a cool room, can push the coil below freezing. This is more likely on older or single-speed models.
5. **Indoor fan motor or control fault.** If the fan slows or stops while the compressor keeps running, the coil freezes quickly.
6. **Faulty indoor coil or room temperature sensor (thermistor).** A sensor reading high can prevent freeze protection from acting, or keep the compressor running too long.
7. **Restriction in the refrigerant circuit.** A failing electronic expansion valve (EEV), a kinked line set, or a debris restriction starves the coil. This is less common, but it is a documented cause on inverter systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thaw first.** Set the remote to **Fan only** (or turn the unit off) and let the ice melt completely. Place towels under the head.
- **Clean the filters.** Lift the front panel, slide out the mesh filters, and rinse them with lukewarm water. Let them dry fully before reinstalling. Do this every 2–4 weeks during heavy use.
- **Check your remote settings.** Set the mode to Cool, the setpoint to 72–76°F, and the fan to Auto or High. Avoid prolonged Dry mode or very low setpoints. Replace the remote batteries if the display is dim or the unit isn't responding.
- **Clear the airflow path.** Move curtains, furniture or anything else blocking the intake (top) or the louvers (front). Keep at least 6–12 inches of clearance.
- **Check the condensate drain.** After thawing, make sure water is draining outside from the drain line. If it overflows inside, see Leaking Water.
- **Breaker reset (once).** If the unit is unresponsive after thawing, turn the mini-split breaker off for 5 minutes, then back on. Do this once only.
- **Close the front panel securely** before restarting.

If ice comes back within 24–48 hours after these steps, call a technician. Do not spray coil cleaners into the head, remove the cover, or touch the line-set fittings.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- They will check the filters, the coil face and the blower wheel for buildup. If needed, they will do a deep clean with a coil bag and low-pressure wash.
- They will measure the indoor fan speed or motor operation and check for fault codes in the unit's history.
- They will connect gauges at the outdoor unit service port. Then they will compare suction pressure, superheat and subcooling against Trane's charging chart. Inverter units must be tested in the manufacturer's test or forced-cool mode.
- They will measure the indoor coil and room thermistors' resistance against the temperature–resistance table.
- If the charge reads low, they will leak-test the flare connections and coil with an electronic detector, bubbles or nitrogen. A proper repair includes fixing the leak, pulling a vacuum and weighing in the charge. "Just topping off" is not a proper repair.
- They will check the EEV operation and line set for restriction if the pressures don't match a simple undercharge.

**A red flag in a quote:** adding refrigerant with no leak search, on a system that was charged correctly at installation.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frost on coil, weak airflow, filters gray | Dirty filters | Thaw, then wash the filters | None needed if it stays clear |
| Ices up with clean filters, musty smell | Dirty coil or blower wheel | Thaw, clean the filters, clear the intake | Deep clean of the coil and blower wheel |
| Ice returns within a day, weak cooling, hissing or oily fittings | Refrigerant leak | Thaw, run Fan only, then call | Leak search, repair, vacuum, weigh in charge |
| Ices only at low setpoints or in Dry mode | Operating conditions | Raise the setpoint, use a higher fan speed | Check the sensor and freeze-protection operation |
| Fan slow or stopped while the outdoor unit runs | Indoor fan motor or board | Turn the unit off | Test or replace the fan motor or control board |
| Ices with no freeze-protection cycling | Faulty thermistor | Turn the unit off, note any codes | Test or replace the sensor |
| Frost only at the coil inlet, correct charge | EEV or line restriction | Turn the unit off | Diagnose the EEV, repair the restriction |

## Repair costs

| Repair | Typical US cost |
|---|---|
| Filter cleaning (DIY) | $0 |
| Professional cleaning of the coil and blower wheel | $150–$450 per head |
| Leak search and refrigerant recharge (R-410A or R-32) | $250–$800 |
| Flare-fitting leak repair, including vacuum and charge | $300–$900 |
| Indoor coil replacement | $900–$1,800+ (sometimes the whole head is replaced instead) |
| Indoor fan motor | $300–$700 |
| Thermistor (sensor) replacement | $150–$350 |
| Indoor control board | $350–$800 |
| EEV replacement | $500–$1,200 |

Trane parts warranties are often 5–10 years with registration, but labor is usually not covered unless you have an extended plan. Ask for written leak-test results before you approve recharges.

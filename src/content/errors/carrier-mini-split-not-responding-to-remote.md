## What this code means

"Not responding to remote" is not a fault code. It describes a symptom: the indoor unit does not beep, light up, or change settings when you press buttons on the infrared (IR) remote.

On Carrier ductless systems, a working remote command normally produces a beep from the indoor unit and a change on its display. When nothing happens, the fault sits somewhere in one of four places:

- The remote itself
- The line of sight between the remote and the unit
- The IR receiver and display board in the indoor unit
- The unit's power or main control board

The symptom does not "reset" on its own the way a lockout code does. If the cause is a power glitch that froze the board, it may clear after one breaker cycle. Hardware failures persist.

If the unit is also showing a blinking light or an error code, it may be refusing commands because of a protection fault. In that case, use the Blinking light error page.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dead or weak remote batteries.** This is by far the most common cause. A dim or blank remote screen is the giveaway, but an IR emitter can fail on weak batteries even while the LCD still looks normal.
2. **Remote settings or lock.** A child-lock or keypad-lock function may be on. Some models have a °F/°C or heat/cool-only jumper or setting that doesn't match the unit. A remote reset or battery pull often clears a confused remote.
3. **Line-of-sight or interference problems.** Furniture or curtains can block the receiver window. Bright direct sunlight or some LED and CFL lighting can swamp the IR receiver. Being beyond the remote's range (roughly 20–25 ft) also causes this.
4. **Faulty remote.** The remote may have been dropped, have a corroded battery contact, or have a failed IR emitter. You can confirm this with the phone-camera test.
5. **No power to the indoor unit.** A tripped breaker, an off disconnect switch, or a power outage leaves the unit dead. In that case the display stays dark and the manual button does nothing. See Won't turn on.
6. **Frozen or locked-up control board after a power surge.** The unit has power but ignores all inputs. One breaker cycle sometimes clears this.
7. **Failed IR receiver or display board.** The remote transmits and the manual button works, but the unit never beeps in response to the remote.
8. **Failed indoor main control board.** The unit ignores both the remote and the manual button even though power is confirmed. This sometimes follows a surge or lightning strike.
9. **Wiring or connector fault between the display/receiver board and the main board.** This can result from loose ribbon or harness plugs, pest damage, or corrosion.
10. **Wi-Fi module or wired-controller conflict.** This applies to some models only. When a wired wall controller or certain Wi-Fi kits are installed, IR remote input may be disabled or limited, depending on the series and configuration.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Replace the batteries** with fresh alkaline AAAs and check that they are installed with the correct polarity. Look for corrosion on the contacts. If there is heavy corrosion, replace the remote rather than scraping inside it.
- **Reset the remote.** Remove the batteries for about a minute, or use the remote's reset or clock function if it has one. Make sure the lock symbol is not showing, and check the manual for how to unlock it.
- **Do the phone-camera test.** Aim the remote at your phone camera and press a button. If there's no flicker, the remote or its batteries are at fault.
- **Improve line of sight.** Stand within about 10 ft, aim directly at the receiver window on the indoor unit, and draw the blinds if sunlight hits the unit. Try with nearby lights off.
- **Check power.** Confirm that the mini-split breaker hasn't tripped and that the outdoor disconnect is on. If the breaker has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and see Breaker keeps tripping.
- **Do one power cycle.** Turn the mini-split breaker off for about 5 minutes, then back on, and try the remote again. Do this once. Don't keep cycling it.
- **Try the manual button.** Lift the front panel, which is a normal homeowner action, and press the manual or AUTO button. Do not open any internal covers. If the unit starts, the board has power and the issue is the remote or the receiver.
- **Check for error lights.** If the display is blinking or showing a code, write it down. A protection fault is a different problem.
- **Reseat the front panel and filters.** Make sure they are properly seated after checking, because a cracked or misaligned panel can obscure the receiver window.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- Confirm the remote is transmitting, using an IR tester or camera, or by trying a known-good remote.
- Verify the supply voltage at the outdoor unit and the indoor unit power and communication terminals.
- Check the indoor board's status LEDs and any stored fault codes.
- Inspect the harness between the display/receiver board and the main board for loose, corroded, or damaged connectors.
- Test the receiver board's supply voltage and output, then swap in a replacement receiver/display board if indicated.
- Determine whether the main control board responds to the manual button. If there is power but no response, the main board is suspect, and they will check the board fuse and look for surge damage.
- On systems with a wired controller or Wi-Fi kit, verify the configuration settings that may override IR control.

A legitimate quote should name which board failed and why. Be wary of a "replace the whole indoor unit" quote unless the board is discontinued or the unit is old and has other failures.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Remote screen blank or dim | Dead batteries | Replace the batteries | None |
| Remote screen normal, no camera flicker | Failed remote emitter | Buy an OEM or compatible remote | None |
| Camera shows flicker, unit doesn't beep, manual button works | IR receiver/display board or harness | Check line of sight and sunlight; do one power cycle | Test and replace the receiver board, reseat the harness |
| Unit beeps but settings don't change | Remote lock, mode mismatch, or protection fault | Unlock or reset the remote; note any error lights | Diagnose the protection fault code |
| Display dark, manual button dead | No power | Check the breaker and disconnect; reset once | Trace the power supply, check the board fuse |
| Power confirmed, ignores remote and manual button | Locked-up or failed main board | One breaker power cycle | Diagnose and replace the main control board |
| Worked until a storm or outage | Surge damage to the board | One power cycle | Replace the damaged board; recommend surge protection |
| IR ignored since a wall controller or Wi-Fi was added | Control configuration | Use the installed controller or app | Reconfigure the control settings |

## Repair costs

| Item | Typical US cost |
|---|---|
| Batteries | $3–$10 |
| Replacement remote (universal to OEM) | $15–$80 |
| Service call / diagnostic | $90–$200 |
| IR receiver/display board, installed | $150–$350 |
| Harness or connector repair | $100–$250 |
| Indoor main control board, installed | $300–$650+ |
| Wired wall controller, installed (optional alternative) | $150–$400 |

Board prices vary widely by series and parts availability. Inverter-model boards and discontinued parts sit at the high end. Check your warranty before paying: Carrier ductless parts warranties are often 5–10 years when the unit was registered.

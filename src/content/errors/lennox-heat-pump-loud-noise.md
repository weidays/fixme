## What this code means

"Loud noise" is not a code your Lennox heat pump displays. It is a symptom, and the type of sound is the best clue to the cause. Lennox does not publish a single noise-troubleshooting sequence, so this page groups sounds by what technicians commonly find on Lennox outdoor units and air handlers.

First, know what is **normal**:

- **Defrost whoosh or hiss.** In heating mode the reversing valve shifts, the outdoor fan stops, and you may see steam for a few minutes. This is expected. If defrost happens constantly or never ends, see the defrost pages listed below.
- **Startup or shutdown thump.** A short clunk when the compressor starts or stops is common, especially on single-stage units.
- **Variable-speed hum.** Inverter models (SL25XPV, XP25, EL18XPV) ramp speed up and down, so pitch changes without warning. Some units use a quiet-shift feature that pauses briefly around defrost.

These sounds are **not normal** and need attention: grinding, screeching, loud rattling or banging, a constant loud buzz, a high-pitched scream from the outdoor unit, or gurgling and hissing that does not stop.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Debris or loose panels on the outdoor unit.** Sticks, leaves or ice hitting the fan blade, or a loose access panel or top grille, cause rattling and ticking. This is the most common and cheapest cause.
2. **Outdoor fan motor bearings wearing out.** You will hear a squeal, screech or grinding, often worst at startup or in cold weather. Fan blades can also loosen on the shaft or get bent, which causes wobble and banging.
3. **Failing run capacitor or contactor.** A loud buzz or hum, sometimes with chattering, often while the fan or compressor struggles to start. A pitted contactor can chatter loudly.
4. **Compressor issues.** Possible causes include:
   - Loose or broken internal mounts, which cause banging or clanking at start and stop.
   - Hard starting, which causes a loud hum followed by a breaker trip or the overload clicking off.
   - Internal wear, which causes grinding or knocking.
   - A loud scream on startup, which can mean very high pressure or internal failure.

   On some units, a missing or damaged compressor sound blanket makes normal operation much louder.
5. **Refrigerant-circuit noise.** Persistent hissing or bubbling outside defrost can point to a leak, a restriction, or a reversing valve that is sticking or bypassing. A reversing valve stuck between positions may hiss continuously.
6. **Indoor air handler noise.** A dirty or loose blower wheel, failing blower motor, or a badly clogged filter can make whistling, rattling or roaring indoors. Loose ductwork can pop or bang as it heats and cools.
7. **Vibration and mounting.** A unit that is not level, sitting on a settled pad, or has refrigerant lines touching the house wall or each other transmits vibration as a drone or rattle.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Identify the sound and timing.** Note whether it happens at startup, during steady running, at shutdown, or only in heating during frosty weather. A short phone video with audio is very useful to a technician.
- **Rule out defrost.** In winter, a whoosh with steam and the fan stopped that clears within roughly 10 minutes is normal.
- **Turn it off if it grinds, screeches or bangs.** Set the thermostat to OFF. Don't keep running a unit that sounds like metal on metal.
- **Check the air filter.** A clogged filter makes the indoor blower roar or whistle. Replace it if it is dirty.
- **Check registers and returns.** Make sure vents are open and unblocked. Whistling often comes from closed or blocked registers.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from outside.** Do not open it or reach into it.
  - Clear leaves, sticks and snow from around the unit, keeping at least about 2 feet of clearance.
  - Confirm the top grille and exterior access panels are seated and screwed down. Re-seat a loose exterior panel only if it simply needs pressing back into place.
  - Check that nothing is touching the cabinet.
- **Check the breaker.** If the unit hummed loudly and then the breaker tripped, you may reset it **once**. If it trips again or the hum returns, leave it off and call a technician. Repeated resets can damage the compressor.
- **Check thermostat batteries and settings.** Make sure the system is not rapidly switching modes. Rapid cycling causes repeated startup thumps.
- **Check the condensate line at the air handler.** Gurgling indoors can come from a partly clogged drain. Make sure the drain is flowing and the pan is not full.

Never put hands or tools inside the outdoor cabinet or fan guard. Never remove the electrical panel, which holds capacitors that store dangerous voltage even when the power is off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician should do most of the following. You can use this list to judge whether a quote is well-founded.

1. **Listen and isolate.** Run the system and pinpoint the source: outdoor fan, compressor, reversing valve, or indoor blower.
2. **Disconnect power and inspect the outdoor unit.**
   - Check that fan blade set screws are tight and blades are straight.
   - Spin the fan by hand to feel for rough or dragging bearings.
   - Check the motor mounts, compressor isolation grommets and sound blanket.
3. **Test electrical components.**
   - Measure run capacitor microfarads against the rating on the label.
   - Inspect the contactor for pitting.
   - Check compressor and fan motor amp draw against the nameplate.
4. **Read the controls.**
   - Check the defrost control or outdoor control diagnostic LEDs for stored faults.
   - On communicating iComfort systems, review the fault history on the thermostat.
5. **Check refrigerant pressures and temperatures.** Measure superheat and subcooling to spot restrictions, overcharge, undercharge or reversing valve bypass. Use leak detection if hissing persists.
6. **Inspect the indoor air handler.** Check the blower wheel, motor, filter and duct connections.
7. **Check the installation.** Confirm the pad is level and that line sets and conduit are not contacting the structure.

A quote for compressor replacement should be backed by amp-draw readings, winding resistance tests, or pressure readings. "It's loud" alone is not enough evidence.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whoosh or hiss, fan stops, steam in winter | Normal defrost cycle | None; wait about 10 minutes | Only needed if defrost is constant or never ends |
| Rattling or ticking outdoors | Debris, loose exterior panel or grille | Clear area around unit; check exterior panels are seated | Tighten fan blade, secure internal parts |
| Screech or squeal from outdoor fan | Worn fan motor bearings | Turn system off | Replace outdoor fan motor (and capacitor) |
| Loud buzz, fan or compressor not starting | Failed capacitor, bad contactor, seized motor | Turn off; reset breaker once if tripped | Test and replace capacitor, contactor or motor |
| Banging or clanking at start or stop | Loose compressor mounts, loose fan blade | Turn off; record a video | Inspect mounts and blade; diagnose compressor |
| Grinding or knocking from compressor | Internal compressor wear | Turn off immediately | Electrical and pressure tests; compressor replacement if confirmed |
| Constant hissing (not defrost) | Refrigerant leak or reversing valve bypass | Turn off; note when it occurs | Leak search, pressure check, valve repair |
| Indoor roar or whistle | Clogged filter, blocked returns | Replace filter; open registers | Clean or balance blower, inspect ducts |
| Indoor rattle or thump | Loose blower wheel or panel, duct expansion | Check filter door is closed | Secure blower wheel, repair duct |
| Drone or vibration through walls | Unlevel pad, lines touching house | Clear objects touching unit | Re-level pad, isolate line set |
| Gurgling at air handler | Partly clogged condensate drain | Check and clear drain flow | Clean trap and pan, check refrigerant if no clog |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call and diagnosis | $90–$250 |
| Tighten panels, fan blade or mounts | $90–$250 (often just the service call) |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Outdoor fan motor, PSC (single- or two-stage units) | $350–$800 |
| Outdoor fan motor, ECM or variable-speed (Signature or Elite models) | $600–$1,500 |
| Indoor blower motor | $450–$1,500 (ECM motors run higher) |
| Blower wheel cleaning or replacement | $150–$600 |
| Compressor sound blanket | $100–$300 |
| Re-level pad or isolate line set | $100–$400 |
| Reversing valve replacement (includes refrigerant recovery and brazing) | $1,000–$2,500 |
| Refrigerant leak repair and recharge | $300–$1,500+, depending on location and refrigerant type |
| Compressor replacement, under parts warranty | $1,500–$3,000 (labor and refrigerant) |
| Compressor replacement, out of warranty | $2,500–$4,000+ |

If your system is 12–15 years old or more, compare the cost of a compressor or reversing valve job against replacing the outdoor unit.

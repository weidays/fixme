## What this code means

A loud bang on startup is not a numeric fault code — your Carrier furnace won't flash a two-digit LED for it. It's a **symptom** you hear, and it points to one of two very different problems:

1. **A combustion boom (delayed ignition).** Gas flows into the burners but doesn't light immediately. When it finally ignites, it lights *all* the accumulated gas at once with a puff or bang. This is the dangerous version — repeated over time it can crack the heat exchanger, warp burners, and create a carbon monoxide hazard.

2. **Ductwork "oil-canning."** A metallic *bang* or *pop* when the blower starts or stops, caused by thin sheet-metal duct panels flexing suddenly under air pressure. This is annoying but not dangerous.

Because these two causes sound similar to an untrained ear and one is a safety issue, **this is rated a pro-level problem.** If the bang happens at the moment the burners light (a few seconds after the inducer and igniter cycle), treat it as delayed ignition and stop using the furnace until a technician inspects it.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty, rusted or misaligned burners (poor cross-lighting).** The most common combustion-boom cause. Carbon buildup, rust flakes or debris across the burner ports slows the flame's travel from burner to burner, so the last burners light late — into gas that has already been flowing. Misaligned burners do the same thing.
2. **Weak or misaligned hot-surface igniter.** An igniter that has aged, cracked or drifted out of position relative to the burner lights the gas late rather than promptly, allowing a small cloud to build before lightoff.
3. **Incorrect manifold gas pressure.** Pressure set too low (or too high) makes lightoff rough and slow. This must be measured by a technician with a manometer and compared to the Carrier rating-plate spec.
4. **Restricted combustion air or venting.** A blocked flue, plugged intake/exhaust termination or restricted combustion-air supply starves the burners and can delay and worsen ignition.
5. **Duct oil-canning (non-combustion).** A bang as the blower ramps up or shuts down, from undersized or unbraced return/supply ducts flexing. Common on high-CFM variable-speed Carrier blowers.
6. **Loose or thin panel/cabinet metal** vibrating on blower startup — the mildest, purely cosmetic cause.

The **combustion causes (1–4) are the ones that matter for safety.** Only a technician can inspect burners, measure gas pressure, and check the flue and heat exchanger.

**A note on the flame sensor:** a dirty flame sensor does *not* cause a delayed-ignition boom — sensing happens after the burners are already lit. A fouled sensor causes the opposite symptom: the burners light, then the gas valve shuts off a second or two later, the furnace retries, and it eventually goes to lockout (Carrier code 14 or 34). If your complaint is "lights then drops out," that's a flame-proving problem, not the boom described on this page.

**A note on the heat exchanger:** a cracked heat exchanger is usually the *consequence* of repeated ignition booms, not the trigger. The upstream cause to look for is flue or combustion-air restriction (#4 above); the crack is the damage that follows.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Stick to these — everything else is inside the cabinet and is technician work:

- **Note the timing of the bang.** Does it happen a few seconds *after* the fan/inducer starts (ignition → likely combustion boom) or right as the *blower* kicks on/off (likely ductwork)? Tell the tech; it saves diagnostic time.
- **Check and replace a dirty air filter.** A clogged filter starves airflow and can worsen both combustion and duct-flex noise. This is the one DIY fix worth doing. Buy the same size printed on the frame of the filter you pull out — furnace filter sizes vary by installation.
- **Confirm thermostat settings and batteries.** Rule out erratic short cycling that stresses ignition.
- **Look at your supply and return registers.** Make sure they're open and unblocked — heavily closed-off registers raise duct pressure and encourage oil-canning bangs.
- **Check that outside vent terminations are clear** of snow, leaves, nests or other blockage, and that the condensate drain line isn't clogged or backed up.
- **Reset the breaker or furnace switch once** if the furnace is locked out — but do **not** repeatedly cycle a furnace that bangs at ignition. If it bangs again, shut it off and call a pro.

If you smell gas at any point, stop — leave the house, and call your gas utility's emergency line or 911 (see **Carrier Furnace Gas Smell**).

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. **Watch a full ignition cycle** to confirm whether the bang occurs at burner lightoff (combustion) or at blower ramp (ductwork).
2. **Remove and inspect the burners** for carbon, rust, debris and alignment, checking how quickly flame carries across all of them, then clean or replace as needed.
3. **Inspect the hot-surface igniter** for surface condition, cracking and position relative to the burner, and replace it if it's weak or misaligned.
4. **Measure manifold gas pressure with a manometer** and compare it to the Carrier rating-plate spec, adjusting the gas valve if off.
5. **Inspect the flue, intake and combustion-air path** for restriction, then **inspect the heat exchanger** for cracks — critical if delayed ignition has been going on for a while.
6. **Check the flame sensor separately** if the furnace is also dropping out after lightoff or setting a lockout code.
7. **For duct bangs**, check duct gauge, add cross-breaking or stiffeners, or install a flexible connector to stop oil-canning.

Ask any tech to show you *why* they concluded combustion vs. ductwork — the timing observation is the key.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boom/puff a few seconds after burners try to light | Delayed ignition (dirty/misaligned burners, weak igniter) | Stop using furnace; replace filter | Inspect & clean burners, check igniter condition and alignment, verify lightoff |
| Rough, delayed ignition with sooty flame | Dirty burners or wrong manifold pressure | None — call a pro | Clean burners, measure & adjust manifold pressure |
| Bang plus signs of poor combustion | Flue or combustion-air restriction (and possible resulting heat exchanger crack) | Shut down; clear any visible outside vent blockage; call a pro | Inspect flue & combustion air, inspect heat exchanger; replace if cracked |
| Burners light, then flame drops out and unit locks out | Flame-proving failure (e.g. dirty flame sensor) — not a boom | One reset only; if it repeats, call a pro | Clean/replace flame sensor, verify flame signal (codes 14/34) |
| Metallic pop as blower starts/stops | Duct oil-canning | Open closed registers, replace filter | Add duct bracing / flexible connector |
| Rattle/pop from cabinet at startup | Loose panel or thin metal | With power off at the switch or breaker, re-seat the **outer** access door only — never open or reach into the burner or blower compartment | Secure panels, add stiffening |
| Bang with any gas smell | Possible gas leak | Leave home, call gas utility/911 | Leak test & repair after safe |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90 – $180
- **Burner cleaning / adjustment:** $150 – $350
- **Hot-surface igniter replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Flame sensor clean or replace:** $80 – $250
- **Gas valve inspection or replacement:** $400 – $800
- **Duct bracing / flexible connector to stop oil-canning:** $150 – $500
- **Heat exchanger inspection:** often included in service call; **replacement** $1,000 – $2,500+ (frequently makes an older furnace worth replacing)
- **DIY filter replacement:** $10 – $40

A cracked heat exchanger caused by long-running delayed ignition is the worst-case outcome — one more reason not to keep running a furnace that booms at startup.

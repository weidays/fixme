> **Before anything else:** if you smell gas or burning, leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not troubleshoot, flip switches or reset breakers until the gas company or fire department says it's safe.

## What this code means

"Won't turn on" is not a flash code — it describes a York furnace that shows **no response at all** when there's a call for heat: no inducer, no igniter glow, no blower, and often no LED on the control board. Many York boards blink a slow heartbeat or show a steady LED even when idle, so a completely dark board can itself be a diagnostic clue — check the diagnostic label inside the blower door for your specific board's normal-idle indication, since it varies by board.

This condition is almost always a **power or safety-circuit problem** upstream of ignition, not a failed burner. If the board *is* lit but a diagnostic flash code is showing, that specific code (not this page) tells the real story — see Related codes.

York boards do not hold a lockout for simple no-power faults; power is simply absent. If a soft lockout is the cause, cycling the breaker once clears it. A hard fault returns immediately until repaired.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Tripped breaker or furnace disconnect switch** — the single most common cause. The dedicated furnace breaker trips, or someone flips the wall switch (which looks like a light switch) near the unit.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, a blank screen, wrong mode (set to Cool or Off), or a schedule holding the setpoint below room temperature.
3. **Blower door / front panel not seated** — the door-interlock safety switch cuts all power when the panel is off or loose. Very common after a filter change.
4. **Blown low-voltage (3–5A) fuse on the control board** — a short in thermostat wiring or an accessory pops the board's automotive-style fuse, killing 24V. Technician work.
5. **Failed control-board transformer** — no 24V output means nothing energizes. Board may appear partly lit or fully dark.
6. **Open door-interlock switch itself** — the switch failed closed-to-open even with the panel seated.
7. **Failed control board** — relays or traces fail; least common but possible on older boards.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

First, the precondition above: if you smell gas or burning, get out and call the gas utility or 911 before touching anything. Otherwise, these are the only steps a homeowner should perform:

- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Heat**, raise the setpoint 5°F above room temperature, and replace the batteries if it uses them. Confirm the display is on and not holding a schedule.
- **Breaker:** Find the furnace breaker in your electrical panel. If it's tripped (middle position), push it fully **Off**, then back **On**.
- **Furnace switch:** Look for a standard-looking light switch on or beside the furnace. Make sure it's **On**.
- **Front panel:** Push the blower-access door firmly until it **clicks flush**. The interlock switch must be fully depressed.
- **Filter:** A badly clogged filter won't stop a furnace from turning on, but replace it if it's filthy while you're there. Buy the replacement by the size printed on the frame of the old filter — York cabinets take several different sizes.
- **Vents and registers:** Confirm supply/return vents are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate line (high-efficiency models):** If you see standing water or a full float-switch pan, a blocked drain can cut power on some setups — but clearing it beyond a visible overflow is technician work.

Do **one** breaker reset only. If it trips again or nothing changes, stop and call a pro. Anything inside the cabinet — including any red manual-reset button on a rollout or limit switch — is technician territory, not a homeowner reset.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A technician works from the power source inward:

1. Confirms **120V line power** at the furnace disconnect and junction box.
2. Checks the **door-interlock switch** continuity with the panel seated.
3. Tests the **low-voltage fuse** on the control board and inspects thermostat wiring for shorts.
4. Measures **transformer output** (should read ~24–28V AC); a dead transformer means no control power.
5. Verifies the **thermostat call** actually reaches the R-W terminals on the board.
6. Confirms the **board's heartbeat LED** and relay operation; replaces the board only after upstream power is proven good.

A competent diagnosis isolates the exact break before any part is quoted. Be wary of a quote to replace the control board without the earlier power and fuse checks.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Totally dead, breaker tripped | Tripped breaker / short | Reset breaker once | Find and fix the short if it re-trips |
| Blank thermostat | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries, check mode | Test thermostat wiring and 24V supply |
| No response, panel just removed | Door switch open | Re-seat front panel firmly | Test/replace interlock switch |
| Board dark, breaker holds | Blown board fuse or transformer | — | Replace fuse / transformer, find cause |
| LED on but no start | Thermostat call not reaching board | Verify Heat mode + setpoint | Test R-W signal and board relays |
| Clicks but nothing runs | Failed control board | — | Replace control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Breaker reset / thermostat setting / re-seat panel:** **$0** DIY.
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 DIY.
- **New thermostat (if faulty):** $120–$350 installed.
- **Service call / diagnostic:** $100–$250 in most US metros, often credited toward the repair; after-hours, weekend and holiday calls run higher.
- **Door-interlock switch:** $120–$250.
- **Low-voltage fuse replacement + short repair:** $120–$300 depending on the hunt for the short.
- **Control-board transformer:** $150–$350.
- **Integrated control board:** $450–$1,100 installed.

High-efficiency, two-stage and variable-speed boards sit in the upper half of that board range; single-stage boards sit near the bottom. A quote near $900 for a two-stage York board is not automatically a gouge. Get the specific fault identified before approving a board swap.

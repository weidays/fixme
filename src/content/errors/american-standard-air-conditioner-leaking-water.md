## What this code means

"Leaking water" is a symptom, not a fault code. American Standard central air conditioners don't display an error for it. The water is condensate that the indoor evaporator coil pulls from your home's air.

In normal operation, that water collects in a drain pan under the coil. It then leaves through a PVC condensate line, sometimes with help from a condensate pump. If water shows up anywhere else, the drain system is blocked, overflowing or damaged, or the coil is producing more water than the pan can handle.

The leak is almost always at the **indoor** unit: the furnace or air handler in a basement, closet, attic or garage. The outdoor condenser doesn't produce condensate in cooling mode. A little water near it is usually just runoff from the drain line outlet or from rain.

There is no lockout or reset for this. If a float switch has shut the system down, it resets on its own once the water level drops. It will trip again if the cause isn't fixed.

Mini-splits have their own page: American Standard Mini-Split Leaking Water.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged condensate drain line.** Algae, sludge, dust and debris build up in the line or its trap. Water then backs up and overflows the pan. This is the most common cause by a wide margin, especially early in the cooling season.
2. **Dirty air filter leading to a frozen coil.** Restricted airflow lets the coil ice over. When the ice melts, more water arrives than the pan can catch, and it often lands outside the pan. See American Standard AC Frozen Evaporator Coil.
3. **Failed or unplugged condensate pump.** This applies to basement and some closet installations that can't drain by gravity. Pump failure, a stuck float or a kinked discharge tube causes overflow.
4. **Tripped float switch or overflowing secondary (emergency) pan.** An attic unit dripping through the ceiling usually means the primary drain clogged and the secondary pan or its drain also failed or wasn't present.
5. **Cracked, rusted or shifted drain pan.** Older metal pans rust through, and plastic pans can crack. A pan that was knocked out of position can also miss the drip from the coil.
6. **Disconnected or poorly sloped drain line or trap.** Joints come apart, the line sags, or the trap is missing or wrongly sized. On units where the coil is under negative pressure, a bad trap can pull air back and keep water in the pan.
7. **Low refrigerant charge.** A refrigerant leak can also freeze the coil and cause the same meltwater overflow. Only a licensed technician can diagnose and repair it.
8. **Unit not level or poorly sealed cabinet panels.** Water runs away from the drain, or humid air condenses on the outside of the cabinet. Uninsulated ductwork "sweating" in an attic can be mistaken for a unit leak.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the thermostat to Off** (fan Auto) to stop new condensate from forming. Put towels down, and set a bucket under any ceiling drip.
- **Check the air filter.** If it's dirty, replace it. If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or the coil area, leave the system off and run the fan on On for a few hours to thaw it. Then call a pro if it refreezes.
- **Find the drain line outlet** outside, near the foundation, or at a floor drain. If it isn't dripping during cooling, it's probably clogged. A wet/dry vacuum held tightly over the outside end for a minute or two often pulls the clog out.
- **Use the cleanout tee** if your drain has one: a capped vertical PVC tee near the indoor unit. Remove the cap and pour in a cup of white vinegar or water to confirm it flows. Do not push wire or tools into the pan.
- **Check the condensate pump**, if you have one. Make sure it's plugged in and the outlet has power, and that the discharge tube isn't kinked. If the reservoir is full and the pump isn't running, that's a repair call.
- **Look at the secondary pan** under an attic unit. If it holds water, the primary drain is blocked. Clear the line as above and have the system checked.
- **Make sure the exterior panels are seated** firmly on the furnace or air handler.
- **Check the breaker** for the furnace or air handler and the outdoor unit if the system won't restart after the water is cleared. A float switch can take a few minutes to drop after the water is gone.

Stop there. Opening the cabinet, removing the coil cover, or testing the pump or float switch wiring is technician work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Confirm where the water comes from:** the primary pan, drain connections, the trap, pump overflow, cabinet sweating, or duct sweating.
- **Inspect and flush the drain system.** They'll blow nitrogen or CO2 through the line, or flush it with a pump. They'll also check slope, trap depth and venting against the coil manufacturer's installation instructions.
- **Inspect the drain pan** for rust, cracks and position by removing the coil access panel.
- **Test the float/safety switch and condensate pump** with a meter for operation and wiring to the control circuit.
- **Check for frozen-coil causes:** blower speed and airflow, coil cleanliness, and refrigerant pressures and superheat or subcooling. If the charge is low, expect a leak search before a recharge.
- **Check that the indoor unit is level** and that the cabinet and duct insulation are sealed.

A fair quote names the specific cause. Be cautious about "add refrigerant" without a leak check, or "replace the coil" when only the pan or drain is at fault.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water around the indoor unit, no drip at the drain outlet | Clogged condensate line | Shut off cooling, vacuum the outlet, flush the cleanout with vinegar | Pressure-flush the line, correct the trap or slope |
| AC stopped cooling, thermostat on, water in pan | Float switch tripped by backup | Clear the drain, wait for the water to fall | Test and replace the float switch if faulty |
| Water dripping from a ceiling under an attic unit | Primary clog plus full or missing secondary pan | Turn off, catch the water, clear the outlet | Clear the drains, add or replace the secondary pan and float switch |
| Ice on lines or coil, puddle after shutdown | Dirty filter, airflow problem or low refrigerant | Replace the filter, thaw with fan On | Airflow check, coil cleaning, leak search and repair |
| Pump reservoir full, pump silent | Failed condensate pump | Confirm it's plugged in and the outlet has power | Replace the pump |
| Steady drip even with a clear drain | Cracked or rusted pan, loose fitting | Keep the system off, catch the water | Repair the fitting, replace the pan or coil |
| Water beading on the outside of the cabinet or ducts | Air leaks or missing insulation | Seat the panels firmly | Seal the cabinet, insulate the ductwork |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges (2026), including labor unless noted:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter (DIY) | $10 – $40 |
| Clearing the drain line yourself | $0 – $20 |
| Professional drain line clearing or flush | $100 – $250 |
| Drain trap or line re-pipe | $150 – $400 |
| Float/safety switch replacement | $100 – $300 |
| Condensate pump replacement | $150 – $450 |
| Secondary drain pan (attic) | $200 – $600 |
| Primary drain pan replacement (if separate from the coil) | $300 – $900 |
| Coil cleaning and airflow correction | $150 – $500 |
| Refrigerant leak search and repair plus recharge | $300 – $1,500+ |
| Evaporator coil replacement (pan integral or leak in coil) | $1,200 – $2,500+ |

Water damage to drywall, ceilings or flooring is extra. Fast shutdown and a secondary pan with a float switch are the cheapest insurance against it.

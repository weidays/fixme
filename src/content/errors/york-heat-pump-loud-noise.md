## What this code means

"Loud noise" is a symptom, not a numbered York fault code. Most York heat pumps do not report noise on a display. You identify the problem by the type of sound and when it happens: at startup, during steady running, during defrost, or at shutdown.

Some sounds are normal on any heat pump:

- **Defrost whoosh or hiss.** The reversing valve shifts with a loud whoosh, the outdoor fan stops, and steam may rise from the coil. This lasts a few minutes.
- **Brief startup and shutdown sounds.** A soft click or hum is expected.
- **Steady compressor hum.** A consistent, even hum while running is normal.

Grinding, screeching, rattling, loud buzzing, banging, or repeated hard clunks are not normal. They usually mean a mechanical or electrical part needs attention.

Noise alone does not lock out the unit. However, a related fault can log a flash code on the defrost control board, such as a high- or low-pressure switch trip or a compressor that won't start. Whether that fault auto-resets or holds until power is cycled varies by board and model. Check the label inside your unit's access panel.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Debris or loose parts in or around the outdoor unit.** Sticks, leaves, or ice can contact the fan blade. Loose screws, a loose access panel, or a loose fan guard can rattle.
2. **Normal defrost-cycle sounds mistaken for a fault.** The reversing valve whoosh and the fan restart are expected in heating season.
3. **Failing outdoor fan motor or fan blade.** Worn bearings squeal or grind. A bent or loose blade can strike the shroud or make a wobbling noise.
4. **Contactor or capacitor problems.** Pitted contactor points chatter or buzz. A weak or failed capacitor causes a loud hum or buzz and hard starts.
5. **Compressor issues.** These include worn or broken internal mounts (banging or clanking), hard starting (a loud thunk), or internal mechanical wear (grinding or knocking).
6. **Refrigerant line or tubing vibration.** Lines touching the cabinet, a wall, or each other can rattle or buzz.
7. **Indoor blower problems.** A dirty or unbalanced blower wheel, failing motor bearings, or a severely restricted filter can cause whistling or rumbling at the air handler.
8. **Unit not level, or pad settling.** Vibration carries through the cabinet and into the house.
9. **Reversing valve problems.** A valve that is sticking or failing can make abnormal ongoing hissing or loud clunks outside the defrost changeover.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Identify the sound and its timing.** Note whether it happens at startup, while running, or during defrost. If you can, record a short video for the technician.
- **If you hear grinding, screeching, or metal-on-metal banging, turn the system off at the thermostat.** Leave it off until a technician inspects it.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from outside the cabinet.**
  - Clear leaves, sticks, and debris from around the base and top grille.
  - Keep at least 2 feet of clearance around the unit.
  - Do not reach into the fan guard.
- **Make sure exterior access panels are seated.** Panels the homeowner can see should sit fully in place. A loose panel rattles. Do not remove panels to look inside.
- **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it is dirty. A clogged filter can make the indoor unit whistle or roar.
- **Check vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked to reduce air whistling.
- **Check thermostat settings and batteries.** Rapid on/off cycling caused by a failing thermostat can produce repeated startup noises.
- **Cycle power once if needed.** If the unit stopped with a fault, you may reset it one time at the thermostat or breaker. If the noise or fault returns, call a technician. Do not keep resetting it.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Operate the system and identify where the noise comes from.** The technician will check the outdoor fan, compressor, contactor, reversing valve, or indoor blower in both heat and cool modes. They may also force a defrost to confirm what is normal.
- **Power down and inspect the fan.** This includes the blade for bends, cracks, or looseness on the shaft, the motor bearings for play, and the fan guard mounting.
- **Test electrical components with a meter.** The technician will check the run and dual capacitors against their microfarad rating and inspect the contactor for pitting or chatter.
- **Measure compressor amp draw and listen for internal noise.** Hard-start problems or mechanical failure show up in the readings and sounds. Checking compressor mounts and grommets is part of this step.
- **Check refrigerant pressures and temperatures.** These readings can reveal reversing valve or charge problems that cause abnormal hissing or gurgling.
- **Read the defrost control board for stored fault flash codes.** The technician will interpret them using the model-specific chart.
- **Check the unit for level and inspect line set routing.** They will also look for tubing that rubs against the cabinet or structure.
- **Inspect the indoor blower.** This covers the blower wheel, motor mounts, and motor bearings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whoosh or hiss, fan stops, steam rises in winter | Normal defrost cycle | None; confirm it stops in minutes | Only if defrost runs too often or too long |
| Rattling outdoors | Debris or a loose exterior panel | Clear debris; seat visible panels | Tighten internal fasteners and the fan guard |
| Squealing or grinding from the top fan | Failing fan motor bearings | Turn the system off | Replace the fan motor and capacitor as needed |
| Ticking or slapping from the fan | Bent blade or ice and debris contact | Clear debris outside the guard; turn off if it continues | Replace or realign the blade |
| Loud buzz, unit not starting | Weak capacitor or pitted contactor | Turn off at the thermostat | Test and replace the capacitor or contactor |
| Loud clunk or bang at compressor start or stop | Hard start or worn compressor mounts | Note the timing and call | Check the start components, mounts, and amp draw |
| Grinding or knocking from the compressor | Internal compressor damage | Shut the system off | Diagnose the compressor; quote repair or replacement |
| Buzzing or rattle through the wall | Line set or tubing contact, or unit not level | None | Re-secure or isolate the lines; level the pad |
| Whistling indoors | Dirty filter or closed vents | Replace the filter; open registers | Inspect ductwork and blower |
| Rumbling or wobble at the air handler | Unbalanced or dirty blower wheel, or worn motor | Replace the filter | Clean or balance the wheel; replace the motor |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Debris removal or tightening loose panels and fasteners (service call) | $100 – $250 |
| Run or dual capacitor replacement | $150 – $450 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Outdoor fan blade replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Outdoor fan motor replacement | $350 – $900 |
| Securing or isolating the line set, or re-leveling the pad | $100 – $400 |
| Indoor blower wheel cleaning or balancing | $150 – $500 |
| Indoor blower motor replacement | $400 – $1,500 (ECM motors cost more) |
| Reversing valve replacement | $900 – $2,500 (includes refrigerant recovery and recharge) |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $4,500+; on older units, replacing the whole system is often the better value |

Costs vary by region and by whether the part is still under York's limited parts warranty. Have your model and serial numbers ready when you call.

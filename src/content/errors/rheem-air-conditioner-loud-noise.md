## What this code means

"Loud noise" is not a fault code on most Rheem air conditioners. It is a symptom, and the type of sound is the most useful clue for diagnosis. Listen for:

- **Where it comes from:** the outdoor condenser or the indoor air handler or furnace.
- **When it happens:** at startup, while running, or at shutdown.
- **What it sounds like:** buzzing, humming, grinding, squealing, rattling, banging or hissing.

Some sounds are normal:

- A brief click when the contactor pulls in.
- A whoosh of air.
- A low compressor hum.
- On scroll-compressor units, a short metallic rattle or "whir" at shutdown, as pressures equalize.

Variable-speed Rheem units, such as the Prestige RA20, also change pitch as they ramp up and down. That is normal too.

Most single-stage units have no fault code for noise, so nothing auto-resets or locks out. A noise only shows up as a code if it comes with a protective trip. Examples are a compressor overload or a high-pressure fault on units with a diagnostic board. Those faults usually clear on their own after a cool-down period, and the timing varies by model.

If your **heat pump** is the one making noise, see the separate heat pump page. Heat pumps make defrost and reversing-valve sounds that air conditioners do not.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failing outdoor fan motor or worn bearings.** You hear squealing, grinding, or a fan that is slow to start or labors. This is one of the most common noise complaints on aging condensers.
2. **Weak or failed run capacitor.** You hear a loud buzz or hum, and the fan or compressor struggles or won't start. This often happens on hot days.
3. **Debris or a loose or bent fan blade.** Sticks, leaves or a shifted blade can hit the fan guard. You hear rattling, ticking or slapping from the top of the outdoor unit.
4. **Pitted or chattering contactor.** You hear loud buzzing or rapid clicking from the electrical compartment.
5. **Loose panels, screws or refrigerant lines vibrating.** You hear rattling or vibration that changes with load. Lines can also rub against the cabinet or the wall.
6. **Indoor blower problems.** Worn blower bearings, a loose wheel, or something caught in the blower housing. This noise comes from the air handler or furnace, not outside.
7. **Restricted airflow.** A dirty filter or closed registers can make the blower roar or whistle, and returns may whistle too.
8. **Compressor problems.** These include loud knocking, grinding, banging at startup, or a hard-start buzz with a tripping overload. Possible causes are a failing compressor, broken internal mounts, or a stalled ("locked rotor") compressor. This is the most expensive possibility.
9. **Refrigerant-side problems.** A hiss or screech can mean a leak, a restriction, or very high head pressure. High head pressure often comes from a badly dirty condenser coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Shut it off if the noise is severe.** If you hear grinding, banging or screeching, set the thermostat to OFF to prevent further damage.
- **Check the air filter.** A clogged filter makes the indoor blower strain and whistle. Replace it if it is dirty.
- **Open supply and return vents.** Make sure registers aren't closed or blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it is set to COOL with a sensible setpoint. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank, because erratic calls can cause rapid on/off clicking.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from outside the cabinet only.** Clear leaves, grass clippings and toys from around and on top of the unit, and keep about 2 feet of clearance. Do not reach into the fan guard.
- **Check the breaker and disconnect.** If a breaker has tripped, you may reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician. See the related breaker page.
- **Re-seat loose exterior panels.** If an outer service panel is visibly loose and rattling, it can be pressed back into place. Do not remove panels or open the electrical compartment.
- **Check the condensate line.** A gurgling indoor drain or a full trap can be noisy. Make sure the line is draining.

## How a technician will diagnose it

1. **Localize the noise.** The tech confirms whether it comes from the outdoor fan, the compressor, the contactor or the indoor blower. They listen through a full start, run and stop cycle.
2. **Lock out power and discharge capacitors safely.**
3. **Test the run capacitor** with a capacitance meter. Readings outside the rated µF tolerance mean replacement.
4. **Inspect the contactor** for pitted contacts, chatter, and correct coil voltage.
5. **Check the fan motor.** They spin the blade by hand to check bearings and end-play, check amp draw against the nameplate, and look for blade damage or a loose set screw.
6. **Measure compressor amps and voltage.** They compare these to the rated load amps (RLA) and locked-rotor amps (LRA). They also check the compressor's grommets and mounts. A hard-start kit may be suggested for an older compressor that is slow to start.
7. **Connect gauges** to check pressures and superheat/subcooling. This rules out a dirty condenser coil, a restriction or overcharge, which cause high head pressure and noise.
8. **Read codes** on variable-speed or communicating models, using the outdoor board LEDs or the EcoNet thermostat.
9. **Inspect the indoor blower** wheel, motor bearings and mounting.

**Sanity check for quotes:** if someone recommends a new compressor or a whole new system, ask for their amp readings and pressure readings. A $20 capacitor can make a compressor sound like it is dying.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loud buzz outside, fan not spinning | Failed capacitor, seized fan motor, or bad contactor | Turn the system off; reset the breaker once if it tripped | Test and replace the capacitor, motor or contactor |
| Squeal or grinding from the top of the outdoor unit | Worn fan motor bearings | Turn the system off | Replace the condenser fan motor (and usually its capacitor) |
| Rattling or ticking outside | Debris, a loose blade, or loose panels | Clear debris from around the unit; re-seat a loose outer panel | Tighten the blade and hardware; fix line-set rubbing |
| Rapid clicking at the outdoor unit | Chattering contactor or erratic thermostat signal | Replace thermostat batteries; check settings | Replace the contactor; check low-voltage wiring |
| Loud bang or clunk at compressor start | Hard start, worn mounts, or internal compressor wear | Turn the system off | Measure amps; install a hard-start kit, replace mounts, or replace the compressor |
| Hiss or screech | Refrigerant leak or high head pressure | Turn the system off; clear around the coil | Leak search, coil cleaning, refrigerant diagnosis |
| Roar or whistle at vents indoors | Dirty filter or blocked registers | Replace the filter; open the vents | Check static pressure and ductwork |
| Thumping or scraping from the indoor unit | Loose or damaged blower wheel, or worn motor | Turn the system off | Repair or replace the blower wheel or motor |

## Repair costs

These are typical installed US prices. They vary by region, tonnage and urgency.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| DIY filter, battery and debris checks | $0–$40 |
| Service call / diagnostic | $80–$200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120–$300 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Condenser fan blade | $150–$350 |
| Condenser fan motor | $350–$800 |
| Hard-start kit | $150–$400 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Indoor blower motor (PSC / ECM) | $400–$900 / $800–$2,000 |
| Refrigerant leak repair and recharge | $300–$1,500+ |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800–$3,500+ (parts often under warranty; labor is not) |

If the compressor fails on an older R-22 system, or on a unit more than 12–15 years old, replacing the whole system is often the better value.

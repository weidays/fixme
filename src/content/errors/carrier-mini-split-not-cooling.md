## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a stored fault code. It's a symptom. Your Carrier ductless indoor unit turns on and may blow air, but the room doesn't get cooler, or it cools only a little.

On mini-splits this symptom has a few causes you won't see on central systems:

- **Inverter compressor modulation.** The compressor speeds up and down instead of simply turning on and off.
- **Washable filters right at the coil.** There's no ducted return, so a clogged filter directly starves the coil of air.
- **Refrigerant flare connections.** Field-made flare fittings at the indoor and outdoor units are a known leak point.
- **Error codes and protection modes.** Many models stop or slow the compressor when they sense a problem, and may show a code. Code meanings vary by series and outdoor unit, so check your manual rather than generic code charts.

Protection modes often clear on their own once conditions return to normal, such as after a high-pressure or overheating event. A hard fault, like a sensor or communication failure, usually comes back after a power cycle until the root cause is repaired.

If your Carrier is a ducted central AC or heat pump, see the separate Carrier AC and heat pump pages listed under Related codes.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Wrong mode, setpoint or remote settings.**
   - The unit is set to Fan, Dry or Auto, or the setpoint is above room temperature.
   - Dead remote batteries mean your new settings never reach the unit.
   - On multi-zone systems, another indoor head running in Heat causes a mode conflict, and the outdoor unit won't cool.
2. **Clogged indoor filters or a dirty indoor coil or blower wheel.** These are the most common hardware cause. Mold and dust buildup on the squirrel-cage blower wheel sharply cuts airflow.
3. **Outdoor unit blocked or dirty.** Debris, plants, snow guards or a fouled outdoor coil can push the unit into high-pressure or high-temperature protection. It then slows or stops the compressor.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** Flare fittings, line-set damage and coil corrosion are typical sources. Signs include frost at the outdoor service valves, weak cooling, and in some cases an error code.
5. **Faulty temperature sensor (thermistor).** Room, indoor coil, or outdoor discharge/pipe sensors can misread. The control board then throttles or stops cooling.
6. **Indoor–outdoor communication fault.** Wiring, connection or board problems between the heads and the outdoor unit. This usually shows an error code.
7. **Undersized unit or wrong placement.** The head is in the wrong spot, the room is too big, or the unit is cooling multiple rooms through doorways. A unit can run perfectly and still feel like it's "not cooling."
8. **Inverter board, electronic expansion valve (EEV), outdoor fan motor or compressor failure.** These are less common and the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Check the remote.**
  - Set the mode to **Cool** (snowflake icon), not Auto, Dry or Fan.
  - Set the temperature at least 3–4°F below room temperature.
  - Replace the remote batteries, then aim the remote at the head and listen for the beep.
- **Check other zones (multi-zone only).** Make sure no other indoor head is set to Heat.
- **Clean the indoor filters.**
  1. Lift the front panel and slide out the mesh filters.
  2. Rinse them with lukewarm water and let them dry completely.
  3. Reinstall them.
  
  Don't spray cleaners into the unit or touch the coil fins.
- **Clear around the outdoor unit.**
  - Remove leaves, grass and debris from the outside, keeping about 2 feet of clearance.
  - Don't open the cabinet.
  - Make sure no cover is left on from winter.
- **Check the breaker or disconnect.**
  - Confirm the outdoor unit's breaker and disconnect are on.
  - If a breaker has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Do one power reset.**
  1. Turn the system off at the breaker for 5 minutes, then restore power.
  2. Give the inverter several minutes to start before judging cooling.
  
  Do this only once. If the problem or an error code returns, don't keep resetting.
- **Check the condensate drain.**
  - Make sure the drain line exits freely outside and isn't kinked or crushed.
  - Water dripping from the head points to a drain or airflow problem.
- **Note any error code or flashing lights.** Write down the code and the model numbers from the indoor and outdoor unit labels.

Stop and call a professional if you see ice on the lines or coil, hear grinding or loud buzzing, or the breaker trips again.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing these steps helps you judge whether a quote is reasonable.

1. **Read stored fault codes** using the remote's check function or the outdoor board's LED or display. They then interpret the codes using the service manual for your exact series.
2. **Measure temperature split.** They compare return-air temperature to supply-air temperature at the head. A 15–25°F drop is typical in Cool mode at high fan speed, though inverters vary.
3. **Inspect the indoor coil and blower wheel** with a flashlight. If they're fouled, a professional coil and wheel cleaning (bagged wash) is recommended.
4. **Check the outdoor unit.**
   - Confirm the compressor and fan are actually running.
   - Measure the outdoor coil's condition and temperatures.
   - On many models, use a test or forced-cool mode for stable readings.
5. **Check refrigerant charge.**
   - Mini-splits are charged by weight. A tech who "tops off" by gauges alone without finding a leak is a red flag.
   - Legitimate leak work includes electronic or bubble leak testing at the flares, nitrogen pressure testing, vacuum and weigh-in.
6. **Test thermistors.** They check sensor resistance against the manual's temperature chart.
7. **Test communication voltage and wiring** between the indoor and outdoor units, then check the boards, EEV coil, fan motor and compressor windings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but feels room temperature | Wrong mode or setpoint; dead remote batteries | Set Cool mode with a low setpoint; replace batteries | None, if this fixes it |
| Weak airflow from the head | Clogged filters; dirty blower wheel | Wash filters | Deep clean the coil and blower wheel |
| Outdoor unit silent, head running | Tripped breaker; protection mode; communication fault | Check the breaker and do one reset | Read codes; test communication and boards |
| Frost on outdoor valves or lines; weak cooling | Low refrigerant from a leak | Turn the unit off and call | Leak search, repair, vacuum, weigh-in charge |
| Cools, then stops after a while | High-pressure protection from a dirty or blocked outdoor coil; fan motor problem | Clear debris around the outdoor unit | Clean the outdoor coil; test the fan motor |
| Error code on display | Sensor, communication, or board fault (varies by model) | Note the code and model numbers | Diagnose with the series service manual |
| Only one zone won't cool (multi-zone) | Mode conflict; EEV or zone sensor | Set all heads to Cool | Test the EEV and zone thermistors |
| Runs fine but the room stays warm | Undersized unit or poor head placement | Close doors; check sun load | Load calculation; consider adding a head |

## Repair costs

These are typical US price ranges. Your actual cost depends on your area and model.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Filter cleaning, mode fix, battery or remote | $0–$60 |
| Service call or diagnostic fee | $90–$200 |
| Indoor coil and blower wheel deep clean | $150–$400 per head |
| Outdoor coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Thermistor (sensor) replacement | $150–$350 |
| Leak search and flare repair plus recharge | $300–$900 |
| Leak repair with coil or line-set replacement | $800–$2,000 |
| Indoor or outdoor fan motor | $300–$700 |
| EEV coil or valve | $300–$800 |
| Control or inverter board | $400–$1,200 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,000+ |

**Check your warranty first.** Many Carrier ductless units carry 5–10 year parts coverage if they were registered, sometimes 12 years on the compressor. Labor is usually not covered.

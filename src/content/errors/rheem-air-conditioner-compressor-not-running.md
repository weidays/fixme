## What this code means

"Compressor not running" is a symptom, not a numbered code. It means the outdoor compressor won't start when the thermostat calls for cooling. The compressor is the heavy, sealed component inside your outdoor unit.

You may notice one or more of these signs:

- The indoor blower runs, but the air coming out is room temperature.
- The outdoor fan spins, but you don't hear the deeper hum of the compressor.
- The outdoor unit hums or clicks but never fully starts.
- The whole outdoor unit is silent.

This page covers **air conditioner** compressor failures specifically. If the outdoor unit is completely dead, or the fan won't spin, see the related pages listed at the end.

**How resets work depends on your model:**

- **Basic single-stage Rheem/Ruud units** use a contactor and capacitor and have no fault display. The compressor's internal overload resets automatically once the compressor cools, which can take 30 minutes to a few hours.
- **Two-stage and variable-speed (EcoNet) models** have an outdoor control board. It can display fault codes and may hold a lockout after repeated pressure or temperature trips until power is cycled. The exact codes differ by board. Check the diagnostic label on the inside of the unit's service panel, or ask your technician to read it.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor (or start components).** This is the most common cause. A weak or bulged dual capacitor leaves the compressor humming without starting, often while the fan still runs. Hard-start kits fail too.
2. **Bad contactor.** Pitted or burned contacts, a failed coil, or insect debris can stop power from reaching the compressor. If the contactor doesn't pull in, both the fan and compressor usually stay off.
3. **Tripped internal overload.** The compressor shuts itself off when it gets too hot. Common triggers are a dirty outdoor coil, a failed outdoor fan, low refrigerant, or restarting too soon after a shutdown. It resets on its own once the compressor cools.
4. **Open pressure switch or board lockout.** A high-pressure switch can trip because of a dirty coil or a fan problem. A low-pressure switch can trip because of a refrigerant leak or very low indoor airflow. On board-equipped models, repeated trips cause a lockout.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** This can trip the low-pressure switch or overheat the compressor.
6. **Wiring or control-voltage faults.** Examples include a loose or burned compressor terminal, a damaged low-voltage wire (often chewed by animals or cut by a trimmer), or a blown low-voltage fuse on the indoor board.
7. **Failed compressor.** This means a shorted or open winding, a grounded compressor, or a mechanically seized compressor. It is less common, and a technician should confirm it only after ruling out everything above.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Thermostat.** Make sure it is set to **Cool**, the setpoint is at least 3°F below the room temperature, and the fan is set to Auto. Replace the batteries if the display is weak or blank.
2. **Wait for the delay.** Many thermostats and Rheem boards hold the compressor off for about 5 minutes after a change. Give it time before deciding something is wrong.
3. **Breakers and disconnect.** Check both the AC breaker (a 2-pole breaker) and the furnace or air handler breaker in your panel. Make sure the outdoor disconnect switch is in the on position. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and see "Breaker keeps tripping."
4. **One power reset.** If the compressor ran and then quit, turn the thermostat off. Switch off the AC breaker for 5 minutes, then turn it back on and set the thermostat to Cool. Do this only once. If the compressor stops again, call a technician.
5. **Air filter.** Replace the filter if it is dirty. A clogged filter restricts airflow, which can trip safeties.
6. **Vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
7. **Outside the unit.** Clear leaves, grass and debris at least 2 feet away from the outdoor unit. Rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose on gentle spray, with the power off. Don't open the cabinet.
8. **Condensate.** If your system has a float switch or the drain pan is full, the cooling call can be cut off. Clear the visible condensate line opening, or call for service if water is standing.

**Don't** touch the capacitor or contactor, push the contactor in with a stick, or keep resetting the unit. A compressor that hums without starting can overheat and be damaged further.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A typical diagnosis follows these steps, so you can sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling.** The tech checks for 24V at the contactor coil (Y and C terminals) to confirm the thermostat signal is reaching the outdoor unit. On board-equipped models, they also read any stored fault codes.
2. **Check the contactor.** They test the voltage across the line and load sides, and inspect the contacts for pitting.
3. **Test the capacitor.** They discharge it, then measure its microfarads against the rating on its label. They also check the hard-start kit, if one is installed.
4. **Check the compressor.** With the power off, they measure winding resistance (C-S, C-R and S-R) and test for a short to ground with a megohmmeter or meter. A hot compressor reading as open usually means the internal overload is tripped, so they will cool it and retest.
5. **Measure current draw.** An amp clamp shows whether the compressor is stuck at locked-rotor amps, which points to a seized compressor or a starting problem.
6. **Check pressures and safeties.** They read refrigerant pressures and check the pressure switches for continuity. If the charge is low, they should look for the leak, not just top off the refrigerant.
7. **Inspect wiring and condenser.** They check for burned terminals, damaged low-voltage wire, a dirty coil, and the condition of the outdoor fan.

**Be cautious with any quote** that calls for a new compressor or a whole new system without capacitor, winding, ground and amp-draw readings to back it up.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor unit hums, fan runs, compressor won't start | Failed capacitor or weak start | Turn off power to avoid overheating | Test and replace the capacitor; add a hard-start kit if needed |
| Outdoor unit totally silent, indoor blower runs | Contactor not pulling in, low-voltage fault, tripped breaker | Check the breaker and disconnect; check thermostat settings | Test 24V at the coil, replace the contactor, repair wiring or the fuse |
| Compressor runs briefly, then stops; restarts later | Internal overload from heat or dirty coil | Clear debris, hose off the coil, replace the filter | Clean the coil, check the fan and charge |
| Compressor won't run, ice on lines or low airflow | Low charge or low-pressure trip | Replace the filter, open vents, turn the system off to thaw | Find and repair the leak, recharge, test the pressure switch |
| Board shows fault/lockout code (two-stage/EcoNet) | Pressure or temperature lockout | One power reset | Read the code, correct the root cause |
| Breaker trips when compressor tries to start | Grounded or shorted compressor or wiring | Don't reset repeatedly | Megger the compressor, inspect wiring |
| Compressor hums, draws high amps even with new capacitor | Seized or failed compressor | None | Confirm by testing, replace the compressor or the unit |

## Repair costs

These are typical US prices including parts and labor. Expect higher prices in high-cost areas and for emergency calls.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnosis | $80 – $200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Hard-start kit | $200 – $450 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Wiring or low-voltage fuse repair | $100 – $350 |
| Outdoor control board (two-stage/variable-speed) | $400 – $1,200 |
| Pressure switch replacement | $250 – $600 (requires refrigerant recovery on many units) |
| Refrigerant leak search and repair plus recharge | $400 – $2,000+ (R-410A or R-454B, depending on the leak) |
| Coil cleaning | $100 – $250 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $4,000+ (less if the part is under Rheem's limited warranty; labor and refrigerant are usually extra) |

If the compressor has failed on a unit that is out of warranty and 12 or more years old, compare the repair price against the cost of a new system.

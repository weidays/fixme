## What this code means

"Leaking water" is not a numbered Carrier fault code — it is a physical symptom, and on a Carrier furnace it almost always points to the **condensate system**. High-efficiency condensing furnaces (90%+ AFUE, like Infinity and Performance series) intentionally cool flue gases until water vapor condenses into liquid. That liquid is supposed to drain safely away through a trap, tubing, and a drain line. When any part of that path clogs, cracks, or backs up, water pools inside or under the cabinet.

Water can also appear if the furnace shares an air handler with a **cooling coil (A-coil)** — a plugged AC condensate drain will leak in the same spot. On rare occasions, the leak signals a **cracked secondary heat exchanger** or a flue/venting problem forcing condensation back into the cabinet, both of which are serious and require a technician.

Because standing water can short the control board and rust the burner assembly, treat any persistent leak as **pro-level** if a simple drain flush does not fix it.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged condensate drain line or trap** — algae, dust, and debris block the tubing so water overflows. This is by far the most common cause on Carrier condensing furnaces.
2. **Full or cracked condensate pan / collector box** — the internal pan that catches condensate overflows if the outlet is blocked or the pan is cracked.
3. **Clogged or dirty AC coil drain (shared system)** — if your furnace sits below an evaporator coil, the AC condensate line may be the true source in cooling season.
4. **Kinked, disconnected, or improperly sloped drain tubing** — sagging or backward-pitched tubing traps water until it spills.
5. **Blocked or improperly sloped flue/vent pipe** — condensing furnaces need the vent pitched back toward the unit; a sag or blockage can pool water in the cabinet.
6. **Failed condensate pump** — furnaces that pump condensate uphill leak when the pump fails or its float sticks.
7. **Cracked secondary heat exchanger** — least common but most serious; leaks combustion condensate internally and requires professional inspection. If you suspect this (water with rust or soot inside the cabinet), leave the furnace **off** and do not run it while you wait for the technician, and make sure a working carbon monoxide alarm is installed and responding to its test button.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform:

- **Set the thermostat to OFF** and, if the leak is heavy or near the control board, **switch off the furnace at the breaker or service switch** to protect the electronics.
- **Check the air filter** — a filthy filter can cause coil freeze-then-thaw water in shared systems. Replace if dirty.
- **Look at the condensate drain line** where it exits the furnace. Confirm it is not kinked, crushed, or disconnected. Gently straighten a kink.
- **Empty the drain pan** if there is a removable overflow pan under the unit, using towels or a wet/dry vacuum.
- **Clear a visible drain clog** — you may use a wet/dry vacuum on the open end of the condensate line outside to pull out a soft clog, or pour approved condensate drain tablets into an accessible cleanout.
- **Confirm the floor drain or standpipe the line empties into is clear** so drained water can actually go somewhere.

Do **not** open the furnace cabinet, remove burner panels, probe the heat exchanger, or bypass any switch. If the leak continues after clearing the drain, stop and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. **Trace the water source** — condensate trap, collector box, coil pan, pump, or heat exchanger — since several parts leak into the same area.
2. **Inspect and flush the condensate trap and tubing**, checking pitch and clearing biofilm the vacuum missed.
3. **Test the condensate pump and float switch** (if fitted) for proper operation and shutoff.
4. **Check the flue/vent pipe slope and connections** for sags or blockages that pool condensate.
5. **Inspect the secondary heat exchanger and collector box** for cracks, corrosion, or pinholes — often with a camera or pressure test.
6. **Verify the pressure switch and drain safety switch** are seeing correct conditions, since a clogged drain can trip them.

Ask the tech to show you the actual leak source before approving heat-exchanger-level repairs — that is the most expensive outcome and should be confirmed, not assumed.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water pooling under furnace | Clogged condensate drain/trap | Vacuum the drain line end, add drain tablets | Flush and clean trap, verify pitch |
| Overflowing internal pan | Blocked pan outlet or cracked pan | Empty pan, shut unit off | Clear outlet or replace collector box/pan |
| Leak only in cooling season | Plugged AC coil drain | Check/clear shared drain line | Clear coil drain, inspect coil pan |
| Water despite clear drain | Kinked/sagging tubing | Straighten visible kink | Re-route and re-pitch tubing |
| Furnace won't run + water present | Drain safety switch tripped | Clear drain, cycle breaker once — if it trips again, stop and call a technician, do not keep resetting | Reset switch after fixing clog |
| Water plus rust/soot inside | Cracked secondary heat exchanger | Shut furnace off, leave it off, confirm a working CO alarm is present, call pro | Inspect and replace heat exchanger |
| Water near a small pump box | Failed condensate pump | Shut unit off | Replace pump / clear float |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor unless noted):

- **DIY condensate flush / tablets / filter:** $0–$30
- **Professional condensate drain cleaning & trap service:** $100–$250
- **Condensate pump replacement:** $150–$350
- **Drain/float safety switch replacement:** $120–$250
- **Collector box / condensate pan replacement:** $300–$700
- **Flue/vent re-pitch or repair:** $200–$500
- **Secondary heat exchanger replacement:** $1,200–$3,000+. Parts are often warranty-covered, but labor on a condensing furnace heat exchanger is the big cost and still lands well above $1,000 even when the part is free. On older units this can make replacing the furnace the better value.

A simple clogged drain is genuinely a homeowner fix. If the source is the pan, pump, flue, or heat exchanger, expect a service call and confirmed diagnosis before the meter runs on major parts.

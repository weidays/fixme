## What this code means

"Humming but not starting" is a symptom, not a displayed fault code. The outdoor condenser is getting power: the contactor has pulled in and 240V is reaching the motors. But the compressor, the outdoor fan, or both are not turning.

The hum is the sound of an energized motor that cannot start rotating. Most Lennox single-stage and two-stage units have no display that will flag this, so you diagnose it by ear and sight.

This page covers the specific case where the unit hums. For related situations, see the separate pages:
- **Compressor not running:** the compressor is silent.
- **Outdoor fan not spinning:** the fan is stopped and there is no hum.
- **Won't turn on:** the unit is completely dead.

Severity is **pro**. Nearly every cause sits inside the electrical panel of the condenser.

**What happens if it keeps humming:**
- A stalled compressor trips its internal thermal overload and stops.
- After it cools, the overload resets automatically and the unit tries again.
- Each failed start overheats the motor windings.

So shut the unit off rather than letting it cycle.

**Variable-capacity models:** Lennox units with an inverter (for example XC25 and SL28XCV) report faults through the outdoor control or the iComfort thermostat. On those models, check for an alert code and pass it to the technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** The dual run capacitor gives the compressor and fan motor their starting kick. It is the most common cause by far, especially after heat waves or power surges. Signs include a bulging or leaking top and a fan that hums without spinning.
2. **Pitted or sticking contactor.** Burned contacts can pass enough voltage to make a motor hum but not enough current to start it. A single leg can also fail to close, so one side of the 240V supply is missing.
3. **Seized or failing outdoor fan motor.** Worn bearings can stop the fan from turning even when the capacitor is good.
4. **Compressor hard-start problem.** A worn compressor may struggle to start against system pressure. This is more common on older units and after short power interruptions. Some Lennox units have a factory or field-installed hard-start kit, and that kit can also fail.
5. **Low supply voltage or a loose or burned connection.** Low line voltage, a loose lug at the disconnect, or burned wiring can starve the motors during startup.
6. **Locked or grounded compressor.** The compressor is mechanically seized or electrically shorted internally. This is the least likely cause but the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the system off.** Set the thermostat to OFF, then switch off the outdoor disconnect next to the unit. This stops the motor from repeatedly overheating.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it is set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the batteries are fresh if it uses them.
- **Check the breakers.** Look for a tripped double-pole AC breaker in the main panel. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, leave it off and see the Breaker keeps tripping page.
- **Replace the air filter if it is dirty.** A dirty filter won't cause the hum, but a technician will want normal airflow when testing.
- **Look, don't touch.**
  - Check that the outdoor unit is clear of debris, vines, and anything blocking the fan.
  - Check that the service panels are seated.
  - Do not reach into the fan grille or open the electrical access panel.
- **Note what you hear and see.** Is it a fan hum, a deep compressor hum, or both? Does the hum stop after a few seconds with a click? Tell the technician. It speeds up the diagnosis.
- **Do one restart only.** After the unit has been off for at least 30 minutes, you may restore power and call for cooling **once**. If it hums again, shut it off and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

1. **Visual inspection.** The technician removes power, then checks the capacitor for bulging or leakage, the contactor for pitting, and the wiring for burned or loose connections.
2. **Capacitor test.** They discharge the capacitor and measure its microfarads (µF) against the rating on its label. A reading more than about ±6% from the rating means replacement.
3. **Contactor check.** With the unit running, they measure voltage across the contacts. Any voltage drop across a closed contactor means it is worn.
4. **Supply voltage.** They confirm about 208–240V at the unit while it is trying to start, watching for sag.
5. **Fan motor.** They check that the fan turns freely by hand with power off and measure the motor windings.
6. **Compressor windings.** They measure resistance across Common, Start, and Run, and test from each terminal to ground. An open, shorted, or grounded winding means the compressor has failed.
7. **Amp draw.** A compressor drawing locked-rotor amps (LRA) without starting points to a hard-start problem or a locked compressor.
8. **Hard-start kit.** If the compressor windings check good but the compressor still won't start, a hard-start kit may be tried. A compressor that still won't start with a kit is usually condemned.

**Red flag on a quote:** being told you need a new compressor or system without these measurements. Ask for the capacitor reading and the winding readings before you approve a major repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor fan hums but doesn't spin; compressor may or may not run | Failed dual run capacitor | Turn off at thermostat and disconnect; call a pro | Test and replace the capacitor |
| Loud hum, then click, then silence; repeats later | Weak capacitor or hard-start problem tripping the overload | Leave the unit off; don't keep restarting | Test the capacitor; measure LRA; install a hard-start kit if appropriate |
| Buzzing hum, nothing turns | Pitted or single-leg contactor, or low voltage | Reset the breaker once; check the thermostat | Replace the contactor; repair wiring or the supply |
| Fan hums but compressor runs fine | Seized fan motor bearings or fan capacitor section | Clear debris from around the unit only | Replace the fan motor and/or capacitor |
| Compressor hums, breaker trips | Locked or grounded compressor | Leave the breaker off | Test the windings; replace the compressor or system |
| Burning smell or scorched wiring | Loose or burned connection | Shut off power; don't restart | Repair terminals and wiring; replace damaged parts |

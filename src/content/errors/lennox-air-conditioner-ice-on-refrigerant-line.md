## What this code means

"Ice on refrigerant line" is not a stored Lennox fault code. It is a visible symptom. You will usually see frost or a coat of ice on the larger insulated copper line (the suction line) running between the indoor coil and the outdoor condenser. It is often worst near the outdoor service valves or where the line enters the furnace or air handler.

Ice forms when refrigerant inside the evaporator gets colder than 32°F. That happens for two broad reasons:

- **Too little heat reaches the coil.** This is an airflow problem.
- **Too little refrigerant moves through the coil.** This is a charge or metering problem.

This page covers what you see on the line set and at the outdoor unit. If the ice is mainly on the indoor coil itself, see Lennox AC Frozen Evaporator Coil, which covers coil-side airflow causes in depth.

Lennox residential ACs do not "reset" for this condition. The ice stays until the system is shut off and thaws. Some models with a high- or low-pressure switch may lock out the compressor during the event. On many Lennox outdoor controls, that lockout clears after a timed delay or when power is cycled, depending on the board. Communicating (iComfort/S30) systems may log a low-pressure or low-charge alert on the thermostat.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

This list ranks causes for ice on the line set and at the outdoor unit. Low charge comes first because frost at the outdoor service valve is the classic sign of undercharge. In everyday terms, though, a dirty filter or other airflow restriction is one of the most frequent reasons any part of the system freezes. It is also the easiest to fix, so rule out the filter first (see Safe checks below). Coil-side airflow causes are covered in depth on the Lennox AC Frozen Evaporator Coil page.

1. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** This is the classic cause of frost on the suction line and the outdoor service valve. Lennox's troubleshooting guidance points to undercharge when suction pressure and saturated temperature are low and superheat is high. Common leak spots are the evaporator coil, flare/braze joints, and Schrader valve cores.
2. **Restricted indoor airflow.** A clogged filter, closed registers, a dirty evaporator coil, or a weak or failing blower lowers the coil temperature until it freezes. The ice then extends out along the suction line.
3. **Metering device restriction.** A sticking or failed TXV, or a debris-clogged orifice or filter-drier, starves the coil. You often see frost starting right at the restriction, sometimes on the small liquid line near the drier.
4. **Running cooling in cool outdoor weather or with a very low thermostat setpoint.** Below roughly 60–65°F outdoors, head pressure drops and the coil can freeze. This is more likely on units without low-ambient controls.
5. **Wrong blower speed after an install or service.** The furnace or air handler may have a too-low CFM setting (via DIP switches or taps) for the matched Lennox outdoor unit.
6. **Outdoor fan or contactor issues that let the compressor run oddly.** These are less common causes of icing. They are covered under Lennox AC Outdoor Fan Not Spinning.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Turn cooling off.** Set the thermostat to Off and the fan to On to let the ice thaw. Do not chip or scrape ice off the lines or coil.
2. **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it is dirty or clogged. Confirm it is the correct size and faces the right direction (arrow toward the blower).
3. **Open supply registers and clear return grilles.** Keep furniture, rugs and drapes off them. Avoid closing more than a couple of rooms.
4. **Check thermostat settings and batteries.** Make sure the setpoint isn't extremely low (below about 68°F). Replace weak batteries.
5. **Watch the condensate drain.** Thawing ice produces a lot of water. Make sure the drain line and pan aren't overflowing, and keep towels handy. If a float switch shut the system off, it should resume once the water clears.
6. **Restart once after a full thaw.** Run cooling and check the line after 30–60 minutes. If the ice returns with a clean filter and open vents, call a technician. Don't keep cycling the system.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Thaw first.** The tech confirms the system is fully defrosted, because readings taken on an iced coil are meaningless.
- **Measure airflow.** This includes checking the filter, total external static pressure, blower speed/CFM setting versus the Lennox spec (about 350–400 CFM per ton is typical), blower motor and capacitor (or ECM operation), and evaporator coil cleanliness.
- **Connect gauges or digital probes.** The tech measures suction and liquid pressures and calculates superheat and subcooling, using the method on the Lennox unit charging label. A TXV system is charged by subcooling; a fixed orifice is charged by superheat.
- **Interpret the readings:**
  - Low suction with high superheat and low subcooling points to an undercharge or leak.
  - Low suction with high superheat but normal or high subcooling points to a restriction (TXV, drier or orifice).
- **Search for leaks** using an electronic detector, bubble solution, UV dye, or a nitrogen pressure test. Lennox evaporator coils and line-set joints are common suspects.
- **Check for a temperature drop** across the filter-drier and liquid line.
- **Repair, then recover, evacuate and recharge.** After fixing the leak or restriction, the tech pulls vacuum to about 500 microns and charges to spec.
- **Check warranty status.** Lennox coils and compressors often carry 5–10 year parts warranties if the unit was registered.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frost on large line and outdoor service valve, weak cooling | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off cooling, let it thaw, call a pro | Leak search, repair, evacuate, recharge |
| Ice on line, very dirty filter, weak airflow at vents | Restricted airflow | Replace filter, open registers, thaw, retest | Clean coil, check blower and static pressure |
| Frost on the small liquid line near the filter-drier | Clogged filter-drier | Shut off and call a pro | Replace drier, evacuate, recharge |
| Ice returns quickly even with a clean filter, odd pressures | Stuck TXV or blocked orifice | Shut off and call a pro | Test superheat/subcooling, replace metering device |
| Icing only on cool evenings or nights | Low outdoor temperature or setpoint too low | Raise setpoint, avoid cooling below about 60°F outdoors | Verify charge, add low-ambient control if needed |
| Icing started after a new install or furnace swap | Blower speed set too low | Note when it started, call your installer | Reset blower CFM taps or DIP switches |
| Water pooling during thaw | Melting ice overloads the drain | Clear visible drain line, mop up | Check pan, trap and float switch |

## Repair costs

| Repair | Typical US cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter replacement (DIY) | $10–$40 |
| Diagnostic service call | $90–$200 |
| Evaporator coil cleaning | $150–$450 |
| Leak search (electronic or nitrogen) | $200–$600 |
| Minor leak repair (Schrader core, flare, braze joint) plus recharge | $300–$900 |
| R-410A recharge | $250–$700 (about $50–$150 per lb) |
| R-22 recharge (older units) | $500–$1,500+, often not worth it |
| Filter-drier replacement | $250–$600 |
| TXV replacement | $400–$1,200 |
| Blower capacitor | $150–$350 |
| Blower motor | $400–$1,800 (variable-speed ECM motors fall at the higher end) |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$3,000+ (parts may be covered under Lennox warranty, but labor usually is not) |

Newer systems using R-454B refrigerant usually cost more per pound to recharge than R-410A systems. Expect roughly $80–$200 per lb, depending on your region and local refrigerant supply. Check the unit's nameplate to see which refrigerant your system uses.

If a large leak is in an old R-22 unit or a coil out of warranty, ask for a replacement quote alongside the repair quote before deciding.

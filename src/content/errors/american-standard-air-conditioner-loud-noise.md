## What this code means

"Loud noise" is a symptom, not a stored fault code. American Standard central air conditioners do not show an error for noise. Some communicating (AccuLink) systems may log related faults on the thermostat if a motor or compressor problem trips a protection, so check the thermostat's alert history if you have one.

The sound itself is the best clue, along with where it comes from: the outdoor condenser or the indoor air handler/furnace.

- **Buzzing or humming:** usually electrical (capacitor, contactor, or a motor that won't start).
- **Grinding or screeching:** usually a failing fan motor bearing.
- **Rattling or banging:** usually loose panels, debris, a damaged fan blade, or an internal compressor problem.
- **Hissing or screaming:** can mean a refrigerant leak or very high pressure. Shut the system off.

Noise problems do not "reset." The compressor's internal overload resets by itself as it cools down. If the unit keeps stopping and restarting with a loud hum, that cycling is a sign of the fault, not a fix.

This page covers the central split-system AC. Ductless units are covered separately in the American Standard Mini-Split Loud Noise page.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failing run capacitor (outdoor unit):** Produces a loud hum or buzz. The fan or compressor struggles or fails to start. This is the most common noise-related service call on residential condensers.
2. **Worn condenser fan motor bearings:** Grinding, squealing or screeching from the top of the outdoor unit, often worse at startup.
3. **Debris, loose panels or a damaged fan blade:** Rattling or ticking from sticks, leaves or loose screws. A bent blade causes a rhythmic thump or wobble.
4. **Pitted or sticking contactor:** Loud buzzing or chattering from the outdoor electrical compartment, sometimes even when the AC is off.
5. **Indoor blower problems:** Squealing, rattling or a roar from the air handler or furnace. Causes include a failing blower motor, a dirty or unbalanced blower wheel, or a clogged filter.
6. **Compressor problems:** Loud clanking, banging, or a hard-start "thunk." Possible causes are worn internal mounts, a failing compressor, or the compressor tripping on its overload. Not all models have a sound blanket, and a missing or damaged blanket also makes a normal compressor louder.
7. **Refrigerant issues:** Hissing, gurgling or a high-pitched scream. Causes include a leak, overcharge, or a restriction. A scream can indicate dangerously high pressure, so turn the unit off immediately.
8. **Vibrating refrigerant lines or an unlevel pad:** Lines rubbing against the house or the cabinet buzz or rattle during operation.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn it off if the sound is severe.** For screeching, grinding, banging, or a constant buzz with no fan movement, set the thermostat to OFF and leave it off.
- **Check the thermostat:** Confirm it is set to COOL with a setpoint below room temperature. Replace the batteries if your model uses them.
- **Replace the air filter.** A clogged filter can cause blower whistling and roaring at the return.
- **Check vents and registers.** Open closed supply registers and clear blocked return grilles to reduce air whistle.
- **Clear around the outdoor unit.** Remove leaves and branches from the *outside* of the unit, and keep about 2 feet of clearance. Don't reach through the fan grille.
- **Seat exterior panels.** If an outer access panel is visibly loose and rattling, press it back into place. Don't remove panels or open the electrical compartment.
- **Check the condensate line.** Gurgling indoors can come from a partly clogged drain. Make sure water flows from the drain outlet and that the line is clear where it is accessible.
- **Breaker:** If the outdoor unit hummed and tripped its breaker, you may reset it **once**. If it trips again, leave it off and call a pro. See American Standard AC Breaker Keeps Tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically:

1. **Locate the noise.** They run the system and pinpoint whether the sound comes from the compressor, condenser fan, contactor, or indoor blower.
2. **Test the capacitor.** They kill power, discharge the capacitor, and measure microfarads against the rated value. Out-of-spec or bulging capacitors are replaced.
3. **Check the contactor and wiring.** They look for pitting, chatter, and loose or burned connections.
4. **Check the fan motor.** They spin the blade by hand with power off to feel for bearing drag or wobble, and measure the motor's amp draw.
5. **Check the compressor.** They measure amp draw and look for hard-start behavior, check winding resistance if it isn't starting, and listen for internal knocking.
6. **Check refrigerant pressures.** They connect gauges to check for overcharge, undercharge, or restriction, and leak-search if a hiss was reported. Only EPA-certified technicians may handle refrigerant.
7. **Inspect the indoor blower.** They check the blower wheel for dirt or imbalance, the motor bearings, and the mounts.
8. **Check mechanical items.** They inspect line set clearance, the pad level, the compressor sound blanket, and cabinet screws.

**Quote red flags:** Be cautious if someone recommends replacing the compressor or the whole system without first testing the capacitor, contactor and amp draw. Do the same if they recommend adding refrigerant without finding a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loud hum/buzz outdoors, fan not spinning | Failed run capacitor or seized fan motor | Turn system off at thermostat | Test/replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Grinding or screeching from top of outdoor unit | Worn condenser fan motor bearings | Turn system off | Replace condenser fan motor (and capacitor if weak) |
| Rattling or ticking outdoors | Debris, loose panel, bent fan blade | Clear debris outside unit; seat loose exterior panel | Tighten cabinet hardware, replace damaged blade |
| Buzzing/chattering at outdoor unit even when off | Pitted or stuck contactor | Turn system off at thermostat; call pro | Replace contactor, check thermostat wiring |
| Loud clank or bang at startup/shutdown | Compressor hard start or internal mount wear | Turn system off; note when it happens | Amp-draw test, hard-start kit, or compressor replacement |
| Hissing or high-pitched scream | Refrigerant leak or excessive pressure | Turn system off immediately | Leak search, pressure diagnosis, repair and recharge |
| Squeal or roar from indoor unit | Blower motor/wheel issue or clogged filter | Replace filter; open registers | Clean/balance blower wheel, replace blower motor |
| Gurgling indoors | Partly clogged condensate drain or refrigerant flow | Check and clear condensate line | Clear trap, check charge |
| Buzz/rattle from lines near wall | Refrigerant lines vibrating against house or cabinet | None beyond noting location | Re-secure and isolate line set |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor. They vary by region, by series, and by whether the unit is under American Standard's parts warranty (usually 10 years with registration) and any labor warranty.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Filter replacement (DIY) | $10 – $40 |
| Service call / diagnosis | $90 – $200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120 – $350 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Condenser fan motor replacement | $300 – $750 |
| Fan blade replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Hard-start kit | $150 – $400 |
| Indoor blower motor (PSC) | $400 – $900 |
| Indoor blower motor (ECM/variable-speed) | $600 – $1,800 |
| Blower wheel cleaning/balancing | $150 – $500 |
| Refrigerant leak repair + recharge | $300 – $1,500+ |
| Line set re-securing / vibration isolation | $100 – $300 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500 – $2,800+ (often more for variable-speed models) |

If the compressor fails on an older unit that's out of warranty, ask for a replacement-system quote to compare against the repair.

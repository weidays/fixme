## What this code means

"Ice on indoor coil" is a visible symptom, not a numbered Lennox fault code. If you look behind the front grille or louver of the wall head, you'll see frost or solid ice on the evaporator coil fins. You may also notice weak or warm airflow, dripping, or a hissing or crackling sound.

The coil freezes when its surface drops below 32°F. This happens for two main reasons:

- **Too little air crosses the coil.** This is usually caused by clogged filters, a dirty blower wheel, or a fan problem.
- **The refrigerant side is running too cold.** This is usually caused by a low charge from a leak, or by a metering or sensor problem.

Most Lennox mini-split heads have an indoor coil temperature sensor that provides anti-freeze protection. When the sensor detects a near-freezing coil, the system pauses the compressor and then resumes automatically once the coil warms up. This is an auto-resetting protection, not a lockout. However:

- If the underlying cause remains, the ice keeps coming back.
- If the sensor itself has failed, the protection never trips.

Whether a code appears during anti-freeze protection, and which code, varies by series.

This page covers the ductless indoor head specifically. For ice on the copper lines, see Lennox AC Ice on Refrigerant Line. For ducted systems, see Lennox AC Frozen Evaporator Coil.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged filter screens.** Mini-split mesh filters clog quickly, often within 2 to 4 weeks in dusty homes or homes with pets. This is the most common cause by far.
2. **Dirty coil or blower wheel.** Dust, pet hair, and biofilm (often called "mold slime") build up on the coil face and in the cylindrical blower wheel. This chokes airflow even when the filters are clean. The problem is very common in heads that are several years old.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Common leak spots are the flare connections at the indoor head or outdoor unit, and pinholes in the coil. Low charge makes the coil run too cold. A charge that is slowly dropping often shows up first as frost on one section of the coil.
4. **Running too cold for conditions.** Examples include very low setpoints (around 60–62°F), continuous low fan speed, or dry mode in a cool room. Each system also has a published outdoor cooling operating range in its manual. Running in cooling outside that range can contribute to icing.
5. **Indoor fan motor or fan control fault.** A fan that runs slowly, runs intermittently, or doesn't run at all can't move enough air across the coil.
6. **Failed indoor coil temperature sensor (thermistor).** A sensor that reads warmer than the coil really is lets the coil freeze without triggering anti-freeze protection.
7. **Electronic expansion valve (EEV) or control board fault in the outdoor unit.** This is less common, but it is documented on inverter systems. A valve stuck partly closed starves the coil of refrigerant.
8. **Kinked or restricted line set.** This usually traces back to the original installation, so it's more likely on newer systems or ones that were just serviced.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thaw first.** Set the unit to fan-only, or turn it off, and let the ice melt completely. Place towels under the head. Don't scrape or pick at the ice. Don't restart cooling until the coil is fully thawed and no ice remains on the fins.
- **Clean the filter screens.** Turn the unit off with the remote, then:
    1. Lift the front panel and slide out the mesh filters.
    2. Vacuum them, or rinse them with lukewarm water.
    3. Let them dry fully, then reinstall them.

  Replace any filter that is torn.
- **Check remote settings.** If the remote's display is weak, replace its batteries. Confirm the unit is in Cool, not Dry, and raise the setpoint to around 72–76°F. For now, set the fan to Auto or Medium rather than the lowest speed.
- **Check outdoor conditions.** Look up the outdoor cooling operating range in your system's owner's or installation manual. If the outdoor temperature is outside that range, avoid running cooling until conditions are back within it.
- **Look at the louvers and airflow path.** Make sure curtains, furniture, or shelves aren't blocking the intake on top of the head or the discharge below it.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Make sure the drain line outside, or the condensate pump if you have one, is draining and isn't blocked. A backed-up drain can send meltwater down your wall.
- **Do ONE reset.** Do this only after the coil is fully thawed. Switch off the dedicated breaker or outdoor disconnect, wait 5 minutes, and restore power. Then run the unit in Cool and watch it for 30–60 minutes.

If the ice returns after you've cleaned the filters and set normal settings, stop running cooling and call a technician. Don't spray coil cleaners into the head, and don't remove the front housing yourself. The blower wheel, coil, drain pan, and wiring are technician work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Visual inspection and airflow check.** The tech will remove the housing and inspect the coil face, blower wheel, and drain pan. They'll measure the air temperature drop across the head and compare it with the expected values for the operating mode. On inverter heads, this temperature drop varies with compressor speed and is often larger than on ducted systems. They'll also listen to the fan speed.
- **Fan motor check.** If the fan is suspect, they'll test the indoor fan motor and its feedback signal to the board.
- **Refrigerant-side readings.** They'll connect gauges at the outdoor service port and compare suction pressure and superheat with Lennox specifications for your model. On inverter systems, the tech should run the system in test or forced-cooling mode to get stable readings. Low suction pressure with high superheat points to a low charge.
- **Leak search if the charge is low.** This uses an electronic detector, bubble solution on the flare nuts, or a nitrogen pressure test. A good tech finds and fixes the leak, then evacuates the system and weighs in the correct charge. If they simply "top off" the refrigerant, the problem will come back.
- **Sensor check.** They'll measure the indoor coil thermistor's resistance and compare it with the temperature chart in the service manual.
- **Outdoor unit checks.** They'll check EEV operation and any stored fault history on the outdoor board.

**Red flags in a quote:**

- Recommending refrigerant added "every year" without a leak search.
- Quoting a full system replacement before checking airflow and charge.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Even frost across the coil, weak airflow | Clogged filters | Thaw, clean filters, run on Auto fan | None if it resolves |
| Ice returns with clean filters, musty smell, black specks from vents | Dirty blower wheel or coil | Thaw, keep filters clean | Deep clean of coil, blower wheel, and drain pan (often a bagged wash) |
| Frost on part of the coil, slowly worsening cooling, possible oily spot at a flare | Refrigerant leak or low charge | Thaw, stop using cooling | Leak search, repair, evacuation, weighed-in charge |
| Freezes only at night or in mild weather | Setpoint too low, low fan speed, or operating outside the published outdoor cooling range | Raise setpoint, use Auto fan, check the manual's outdoor cooling range and avoid cooling outside it | Check system operation against the manual if the issue continues |
| Little or no air from the head, ice builds fast | Indoor fan motor or fan control fault | Thaw, do one power reset | Test and replace fan motor or board |
| Freezes with no protection pause or code | Failed coil thermistor | Thaw, do one power reset | Test and replace indoor coil sensor |
| Ice plus erratic cooling or outdoor fault codes | EEV or outdoor board fault | Note any codes, do one power reset | Diagnose and replace EEV coil or valve, or board |
| Ice on a newly installed system | Kinked line set or installation charge error | Contact the installer | Inspect line set, correct the charge |

## Repair costs

Typical US price ranges, including labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Filter cleaning (DIY) | $0 |
| Replacement filter mesh | $10–$40 |
| Service call and diagnosis | $90–$200 |
| Professional deep clean of the indoor head (coil, blower wheel, drain pan) | $150–$450 per head |
| Leak search | $150–$400 |
| Flare joint re-make or repair | $150–$400 |
| Evacuation and recharge | $200–$600+ |
| Indoor coil temperature sensor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Indoor fan motor replacement | $300–$700 |
| Indoor or outdoor control board | $300–$1,000+ |
| EEV coil or valve replacement | $350–$900 |
| Indoor coil replacement (if leaking) | $900–$2,500+ |

The cost of evacuation and recharge depends on the amount of charge and the refrigerant type. Newer equipment may use R-454B instead of R-410A. Longer line sets and multi-zone systems hold more refrigerant, so they can land at the higher end of this range or above it.

Outdoor inverter boards usually cost more than indoor boards.

Parts may be covered under Lennox's limited warranty if the system was registered. Labor usually isn't covered unless you have a separate labor plan.

If an older head has a leaking coil, compare the repair cost with the price of a new indoor unit. If the outdoor unit is also aging, compare it with the price of a full system.

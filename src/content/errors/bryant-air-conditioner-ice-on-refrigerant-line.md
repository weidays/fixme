## What this code means

"Ice on refrigerant line" is a symptom, not a Bryant fault code. On a Bryant split-system AC, it usually means frost or ice is forming on the larger, insulated suction line. You may see it at the indoor coil, along the line set, or at the service valves on the outdoor unit.

A healthy suction line runs cool, roughly 40–50°F, and sweats lightly. Ice forms when the refrigerant inside drops below 32°F. That happens for two main reasons:

- **Not enough heat is reaching the indoor coil.** This is an airflow problem.
- **The refrigerant pressure is too low.** This is a charge or restriction problem.

Ice on the line usually means the evaporator coil is freezing too. The separate page "Bryant AC Frozen Evaporator Coil" covers the indoor coil itself. This page focuses on what visible line icing tells you and what to do about it.

**There is nothing to reset.** Bryant ACs do not display a code for line icing. On Evolution systems, the control may log low-pressure trips or airflow events. Some units with a low-pressure switch will shut the compressor off and restart it automatically after a delay. Behavior varies by model, so do not rely on the unit protecting itself.

Treat ice as a reason to stop cooling now. Liquid refrigerant flooding back to the compressor is a common path to compressor failure.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Restricted airflow from a dirty or clogged air filter.** This is the most common cause. Less air across the coil drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Low charge lowers suction pressure and coil temperature. Typical leak points are the evaporator coil, flare or brazed joints, and Schrader valve cores. A low charge always means a leak.
3. **Blocked or closed supply registers or return grilles.** Too many closed vents, or furniture over a return, starves the coil of air.
4. **Dirty indoor evaporator coil.** Dust and debris matting on the coil face restricts airflow even with a clean filter.
5. **Indoor blower problem.** Possible causes include a failing blower motor or capacitor, a wrong blower speed or airflow setting, or a variable-speed motor not reaching its target airflow. On Evolution and ECM systems, airflow setup matters.
6. **Refrigerant metering restriction.** A clogged TXV or piston, or a plugged filter-drier, starves the coil and creates a cold spot. Ice may form right after the restriction.
7. **Running at very low outdoor temperatures.** Operating below roughly 60–65°F outdoors without low-ambient controls can drop head pressure enough to ice the coil and line.
8. **Collapsed or undersized ductwork, or a kinked line set.** These cause ongoing airflow or refrigerant restriction, often after a renovation or a new install.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Stop cooling and thaw the system.** Set the thermostat to **Off** (cooling off) and the fan to **On**. Let it run until all ice is gone, usually 1–3 hours and sometimes longer. Do not chip or scrape ice off the lines or coil. Do not use a heat gun.
2. **Check and replace the air filter.** If it looks gray, matted or bowed, replace it with the correct size. Avoid very high-MERV filters unless your system was designed for them, because they can restrict airflow.
3. **Open supply registers and clear return grilles.** Make sure at least 80% of supply vents are open and nothing blocks the returns.
4. **Check thermostat settings and batteries.** Replace weak batteries. Avoid setting the temperature extremely low, such as 60°F, in hopes of faster cooling. Do not run cooling on cool nights below about 60°F outside.
5. **Watch the condensate drain during the thaw.** Make sure the drain line and pan are carrying the melt water away. If the pan overflows or a float switch shuts the system down, the drain may be clogged.
6. **Confirm exterior panels are seated.** Make sure the indoor unit's blower and filter access doors are fully closed. A loose blower door can stop the blower or change airflow.
7. **Restart and watch.** After a full thaw with a clean filter, run cooling and check the suction line after 15–30 minutes. Light sweating is normal. If frost returns, shut it off and call a technician.

Stop there. Opening the cabinet, testing parts or touching the refrigerant system is technician work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician confirms the cause before adding refrigerant or replacing parts. Expect these steps:

- **Verify the coil is fully thawed first.** Gauge readings taken on a frozen coil are meaningless.
- **Check airflow.**
  - Inspect the filter and the evaporator coil face.
  - Measure total external static pressure.
  - Check blower speed or airflow settings, and the Evolution airflow configuration if applicable.
  - Test the blower motor and capacitor.
- **Connect gauges or digital probes.** The technician measures suction and liquid pressures. Then they calculate:
  - **Superheat**, for piston systems.
  - **Subcooling**, for TXV systems, which most current Bryant units use.

  These are compared against the charging chart on the outdoor unit. Low suction pressure with high superheat suggests low charge or a restriction. Normal charge with low airflow points back to the indoor side.
- **Leak search, if the charge is low.** Methods include an electronic leak detector, bubble solution, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure testing. Coils and joints are the usual suspects.
- **Restriction check.** The technician looks for a temperature drop across the filter-drier or metering device, and frost starting at a specific point.
- **Review logged faults on Evolution systems.** Low-pressure or airflow events in the control's history can support the diagnosis.

**Red flag:** a quote to "top off the refrigerant" with no airflow check and no leak search. Low charge means a leak, and an honest quote should address finding it.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on suction line, filter very dirty | Restricted airflow | Thaw, replace filter, restart | None if it clears; otherwise check static pressure |
| Ice returns within hours despite clean filter | Low refrigerant from a leak | Turn cooling off, run fan to thaw | Leak search, repair, evacuate, recharge by weight or subcooling |
| Ice plus weak airflow at all vents | Blower motor, capacitor or setting issue | Check filter and open all vents | Test motor and capacitor, correct airflow setup |
| Ice with clean filter, good fan, dusty coil | Dirty evaporator coil | Keep filters changed going forward | Clean the evaporator coil |
| Frost starting at one spot on the line or at the drier | Metering device or filter-drier restriction | Shut off cooling | Diagnose restriction, replace TXV or drier |
| Ice only on cool nights | Running in low outdoor temperatures | Don't run cooling below about 60°F outside | Add low-ambient controls if needed |
| Water overflowing during thaw | Clogged condensate drain | Clear the condensate line, check the pan | Clear the drain, check the float switch |
| Ice started after remodel or new install | Duct restriction or charge or setup error | Check that registers are open | Duct inspection, re-verify the charge |

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor. Costs vary by region, warranty status and refrigerant type. Most current Bryant units use R-410A or R-454B.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter (DIY) | $10 – $50 |
| Service call and diagnosis | $90 – $250 |
| Evaporator coil cleaning | $150 – $500 |
| Blower capacitor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Blower motor (PSC or ECM) | $400 – $1,500 |
| Leak search | $150 – $500 |
| Minor leak repair (Schrader core, flare, braze joint) | $200 – $800 |
| Refrigerant recharge (varies by pounds and refrigerant type) | $200 – $900 |

| TXV replacement | $500 – $1,500 |

Check your registration and warranty status before approving large repairs. Bryant parts warranties often run 10 years when the equipment was registered on time.

## What this code means

**If you see smoke or flames, get everyone out and call 911.** If a carbon monoxide (CO) alarm is sounding, get everyone out immediately and call 911 from outside. If the smell is rotten egg or sulfur, that is gas, not burning: leave the house and call your gas utility's emergency line or 911 from outside.

For any burning plastic, rubber, electrical, or persistent burning odor:

1. Set the thermostat to OFF.
2. Turn off the furnace service switch (it looks like a light switch on or near the unit) or its breaker.
3. Open windows to ventilate.
4. Call an HVAC technician.

Do not keep running the furnace "to see if it goes away."

"Burning smell" is a symptom, not a Goodman control-board code. The type of smell is the most useful clue:

- **Dusty or "burnt dust" smell at the first startup of the season.** This is usually normal burn-off.
- **Hot electrical, plastic, or acrid smell.** This points to overheating wiring, a failing motor, or a failing component inside the cabinet. Treat it as an emergency.
- **Burning smell together with visible flame outside the burners or scorch marks around the burner opening.** This suggests flame rollout. It is covered in its own guide, Goodman Furnace Flame Rollout: Shut Down Now. This page covers the burning odors that are not rollout.

If the furnace shows an LED flash code, do not guess what it means. Compare it against the diagnostic label on your own blower door. How limit and rollout faults are reported varies by board: depending on the control, they may appear as a limit-circuit flash code or as a continuous or lockout indication.

Any safety switches that trip along the way, such as the high-limit or rollout switch, behave the way their own code pages describe. On most Goodman boards, a tripped rollout switch is manual-reset and must not be reset by a homeowner.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Seasonal dust burn-off.** Dust settles on the heat exchanger and burners over summer. It produces a mild odor for the first one or two hours of heating, with no smoke. This is normal and does not return once it has cleared.
2. **Clogged air filter causing overheating.** Restricted airflow makes the heat exchanger and blower motor run hot, and you may notice a hot or dusty smell. It often comes with a high-limit trip and short heating cycles. Many Goodman boards report a limit trip as 4 flashes, but check your blower-door label.
3. **Failing or seized blower motor.** Worn bearings or a failing motor winding overheats and gives off a hot-electrical or burning-varnish smell. On PSC-motor models, a failed run capacitor can make the motor hum, overheat, and smell. ECM motors can also fail and burn out their electronics.
4. **Overheating wiring or a loose electrical connection.** A loose spade terminal, a chafed wire, or a burned connector at the control board, transformer, or motor plug creates a sharp electrical or plastic smell. It may produce no flash code at all.
5. **Failing inducer (draft) motor.** The small motor that vents combustion gases can overheat as its bearings wear. It usually also causes noise or pressure-switch codes.
6. **Items stored against the furnace or vent pipe.** Boxes, laundry, paint cans, or other items touching the cabinet, flue pipe, or vent connector can scorch and give off a burning odor. This is a fire hazard. Clear the area right away.
7. **Foreign material in the ductwork or on a register.** Plastic toys, paper, or other items near hot supply registers or inside the return can scorch.
8. **Flame rollout or a combustion problem.** Covered separately, on the Goodman Furnace Flame Rollout page. It is listed here only so you can rule it out: look for scorching around the burner opening.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

**If a CO alarm sounds at any point, stop and get everyone out of the house immediately.** Call 911 from outside and do not go back in until responders say it is safe.

If the smell is electrical, plastic, or accompanied by smoke, shut the furnace down first: thermostat to OFF, then the service switch or breaker. If the LED flash code is visible through the sight glass in the lower panel at that moment, write it down. Never delay shutdown to read or wait for a code. The flashing may stop once power is off, and that is fine.

Then do only these checks:

- **Identify the smell.** A mild dusty smell on the first cold day that fades is likely burn-off. If you are unsure, treat it as electrical.
- **Check the area around the furnace.** Remove anything stored against the cabinet, the flue pipe, or the vent connector, such as boxes, laundry, or paint cans.
- **Check the air filter.** With the power off, slide out the filter. If it is gray, matted, or bowed, replace it with the same size. A badly clogged filter is a common and fixable cause of overheating.
- **Check the registers and returns.** Look for melted, scorched, or foreign items on or inside supply registers and return grilles. Make sure nothing is blocking them.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it is set correctly. Replace its batteries if it uses them. A thermostat calling constantly will not cause burning on its own, but correct settings help a technician reproduce the problem.
- **Check the panels.** Make sure the front panels are fully seated. On most Goodman models, a loose blower door keeps the door switch open and prevents operation.
- **Compare any code you noted.** Match the flash pattern you wrote down against the diagnostic label on your blower door, and pass it along to your technician.
- **Dust burn-off only:** if the smell was clearly mild dust burn-off with no smoke, you can run the furnace with windows cracked. Stop immediately if the smell changes or persists beyond a couple of hours.

Do not open the burner or blower compartments, touch wiring, test with a meter, or reset a rollout switch. Do not cycle power repeatedly to "clear" the problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should:

- **Interview and inspect.** Ask what the smell was like, when it occurs (startup, during the burn, or with the blower only), and whether any codes appeared. Then do a visual sweep for discolored wires, melted insulation, or scorch marks on the board, transformer, and burner area.
- **Check the blower motor.**
  - Spin the wheel by hand with the power off to check for bearing drag.
  - Measure amperage draw against the nameplate rating.
  - On PSC motors, test the run capacitor's microfarad rating.
  - On ECM motors, check the module and the motor's power and signal.
- **Check electrical connections.** Inspect every terminal, the motor plugs, the limit and rollout switch wiring, and the transformer for heat damage. Tighten or replace connectors as needed.
- **Check the inducer motor.** Look at its amp draw, bearing noise, and housing for heat damage.
- **Check airflow.** Measure static pressure and temperature rise. Excessive rise points to a filter or duct restriction causing overheating.
- **Check combustion.** Inspect burner flame pattern and alignment, check for rollout evidence, and inspect the heat exchanger. Many techs also run a CO test as standard practice when an odor complaint involves a gas appliance.
- **Confirm the fix.** Run a full heating cycle after the repair to make sure the smell is gone.

If a quote jumps straight to "replace the furnace" without any of these findings, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mild dusty smell at first seasonal startup, fades | Dust burn-off | Replace filter, ventilate, monitor | None unless it persists |
| Hot or dusty smell plus a limit code (check your blower-door label), short cycles | Clogged filter or airflow restriction | Replace filter, open registers | Static pressure test, duct or blower check |
| Hot electrical or varnish smell, blower humming or slow | Failing blower motor or run capacitor | Shut down at the service switch | Capacitor test or replacement, motor replacement |
| Sharp burning plastic smell, no code | Overheated wiring or connector | Shut down, keep off | Find and repair the burned wiring or terminal |
| Burning smell plus grinding or whine at startup | Inducer motor bearing failure | Shut down | Replace the inducer motor |
| Scorching smell near the furnace, items stored against it or the vent pipe | Stored items heating up (fire hazard) | Clear the area around the cabinet and vent pipe | Inspection if anything was scorched |
| Smell only from one register | Foreign object in the duct or register | Remove items you can reach and see | Duct inspection or cleaning if the item is deep |
| Burning smell plus scorch marks around the burner opening | Flame rollout | Shut down; do not reset | See the flame rollout guide |
| CO alarm sounding | Possible carbon monoxide | Evacuate immediately, call 911 from outside | Full combustion and CO inspection |
| Rotten-egg odor | Gas leak (not burning) | Leave the home, call the utility or 911 | Utility and licensed tech |
| Visible smoke or flames | Fire | Evacuate, call 911 | Full inspection after the fire department clears it |

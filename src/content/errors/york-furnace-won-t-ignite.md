## What this code means

"Won't ignite" is not a single blink code on a York furnace—it's the symptom you see (or don't see) when the furnace runs through its startup sequence but never produces or proves a flame. On modern York boards, a failed ignition attempt ends in a **retry cycle** and, after the allowed number of tries (usually three), an **ignition lockout**. The board will display a lockout flash code on its LED—check the code table on the furnace door label, which varies by control board, to see exactly what that flash count means on your unit.

York furnaces use **hot-surface ignition (HSI)**: on a call for heat, the inducer motor spins up, the pressure switch proves draft, the igniter glows, the gas valve opens, and a flame sensor confirms the burners lit. If any link in that chain fails, the furnace won't ignite.

Whether the board **auto-retries or holds in lockout** depends on where the sequence failed and on your specific control board. A soft fault may retry automatically; a hard lockout holds until you cycle power once. If you see a steady flash code, look it up against the legend inside the blower door—the code tells you *where* in the sequence it stopped.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or cracked hot-surface igniter** — the most common no-ignition cause. HSI elements are brittle and degrade over time. It may still glow faintly or not at all.
2. **Dirty or failed flame sensor** — the furnace lights briefly, then shuts off because it can't prove flame. Ends in lockout after repeated tries.
3. **Pressure switch not closing** — if draft isn't proven, the board never energizes the igniter or gas valve. (See York 2/3 flashes.)
4. **No gas supply to the house (utility interruption, empty propane tank, closed manual valve)** — the igniter glows but no flame appears.
5. **Failed gas valve** — the valve doesn't open on command even with good gas supply and control signal.
6. **Control board fault** — a board that won't sequence the ignition components correctly.
7. **Blown low-voltage fuse or wiring fault** — a bad connection anywhere in the ignition circuit stops the sequence.
8. **Tripped high-limit or rollout switch** — a safety switch that won't reset blocks ignition.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should perform. Everything else is inside the cabinet and is technician work.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to HEAT and the setpoint is above room temperature. Replace the batteries if it uses them.
- **Air filter:** A severely clogged filter can cause overheating and limit trips. Replace a dirty filter with the correct size.
- **Furnace switch & breaker:** Make sure the switch beside the furnace is ON and the breaker hasn't tripped. Reset a tripped breaker once.
- **Gas supply:** Look at the handle on the manual gas shutoff at the furnace *without touching it*—parallel with the pipe means open—and check whether other gas appliances in the house still work. **Do not** put your hands on gas piping, operate any gas valve, or relight anything.
- **Vents & registers:** Make sure supply and return registers are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate line (high-efficiency models):** A clogged drain can trip a safety and block operation. Check for standing water or a full drain pan.
- **One reset:** Cycle the furnace switch off, wait 30 seconds, and back on. Let it run one full ignition attempt. **If it fails again, stop**—do not reset repeatedly.

**If you ever smell gas**, leave the house immediately, don't touch switches, and call your gas utility's emergency line or 911 from outside.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A tech works the ignition sequence in order to find the break:

1. **Read the flash code** at the control board, against that board's own legend, to see where the sequence stopped.
2. **Verify 24V and line voltage** and check the low-voltage fuse.
3. **Watch a full ignition cycle**: inducer spin-up, pressure switch closure, igniter glow, gas valve opening, flame proving.
4. **Test the hot-surface igniter** for resistance and visible glow.
5. **Check the flame sensor** microamp signal and clean or replace it.
6. **Confirm gas pressure** at the valve with a manometer.
7. **Test the gas valve** for proper opening voltage and operation.
8. **Inspect safety switches** (limit, rollout) and wiring for continuity.

Sanity check: a good tech identifies the exact failed component before quoting—not just "replace the board."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Igniter never glows | Failed igniter, board, or pressure switch open | Cycle power once; check filter & switch | Test igniter, board, pressure switch; replace faulty part |
| Igniter glows, no flame | Closed gas valve, empty tank, failed gas valve | Confirm gas is on to other appliances | Verify gas pressure; test/replace gas valve |
| Lights then shuts off, retries, locks out | Dirty/failed flame sensor | Cycle power once | Clean or replace flame sensor |
| Inducer runs, nothing else happens | Pressure switch not closing | Check condensate drain, vents | Test pressure switch, inspect hoses/vent |
| No response at all | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, no 24V | Reset breaker & switch once | Check fuse, transformer, wiring |
| Furnace short cycles on heat | Clogged filter, limit trip | Replace filter | Test limit switch, check airflow |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thermostat batteries (DIY):** $5–$15
- **Hot-surface igniter:** $150–$350 installed
- **Flame sensor (clean or replace):** $100–$250
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$350
- **Gas valve:** $400–$900+ installed, depending on model and local labor rates
- **Control board:** $400–$1,200 installed, depending on model and local labor rates
- **Limit or rollout switch:** $150–$350
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180 (often credited toward the repair)

If your furnace is old and needs a board *and* a gas valve, get a replacement quote too—repair costs can approach the value of an aging unit.

## What this code means

"Keeps shutting off" isn't a single Rheem blink code — it's a behavior. Your furnace starts a heat cycle, runs for a stretch, then stops before the thermostat is satisfied, often repeating. On Rheem Classic and Classic Plus furnaces with an integrated furnace control (IFC) board, this usually means a safety device is interrupting the cycle: a high-limit switch sensing overheat, a flame sensor losing flame signal, a pressure switch opening, or the board itself detecting a fault and shutting down the gas valve and igniter.

The key distinction is *when* it shuts off. If it stops during ignition, that overlaps with our "Won't ignite" page. If it fires, runs a few minutes, then quits — that's typically a limit, flame-sense or venting problem, which is what this page covers.

Many of these faults **auto-reset** once the unit cools or the condition clears, which is why the furnace keeps cycling. If the board counts several failed tries, it goes into a **hard lockout** that holds until you cycle power at the breaker. Check the LED through the sight glass and match the blink pattern to the label inside the door — exact codes vary by board revision.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Restricted airflow causing overheat (high-limit trip).** A clogged filter, closed registers, or a dirty blower wheel makes the heat exchanger run hot. The high-limit switch opens, cuts the gas, and the furnace shuts off — then restarts when it cools. This is the single most common reason a Rheem furnace cycles off early.
2. **Dirty or weak flame sensor.** If the flame sensor loses its signal mid-cycle, the board shuts the gas valve to prevent unburned gas. Rheem's sequence will retry and may eventually lock out.
3. **Pressure switch opening during the run cycle.** A partially blocked flue, condensate backup (on 90-series condensing units), or a failing inducer motor causes the pressure switch to open, dropping the burners.
4. **Flame rollout or auxiliary limit trip.** A rollout switch or secondary limit opening indicates a serious airflow or venting problem — sometimes a cracked heat exchanger. **If you suspect a rollout trip, shut the furnace off at its service switch or breaker and call a licensed technician. Do not attempt a reset — not at the switch, not at the breaker.** A rollout switch exists because flame left the burner compartment, and resetting it without finding out why can put fire and carbon monoxide into your house.
5. **Blower motor or capacitor issues.** A blower that doesn't move enough air lets the furnace overheat and trip the limit repeatedly.
6. **Failing control board or loose low-voltage wiring.** Intermittent connections or a marginal IFC board can drop the cycle unpredictably.
7. **Thermostat problems.** Low batteries or a loose thermostat wire can make the furnace behave as if it's being told to stop.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps you should do yourself:

- **Replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the number-one cause of overheat shutdowns. Swap in a clean one and run the furnace.
- **Open all supply and return registers.** Closed or blocked vents restrict airflow and cause limit trips. Make sure furniture and rugs aren't covering them.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Heat, the setpoint is above room temperature, and replace the batteries if it takes them.
- **Reset the breaker once.** If the furnace is locked out, cycle its breaker off and on a single time. Do **not** reset repeatedly — that bypasses the safety logic. If a rollout trip is suspected, skip this step entirely and leave the unit off.
- **Check the condensate line (90-series condensing furnaces).** Look for an overflowing or clogged drain near the furnace. A backed-up line can open the pressure switch. Clear a visible external clog only.
- **Confirm exterior panels are seated.** The door must be fully closed for the door switch to allow operation.

If the furnace still cycles off after a fresh filter and open vents, stop here and call a technician. If you ever smell gas or your CO alarm sounds, leave the house and call 911 or your gas utility's emergency line.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

- Read the IFC blink code and the stored fault history on the board.
- Check static pressure and temperature rise against Rheem's spec — the target temperature-rise range is printed on the furnace's rating plate (data label) inside the cabinet door, so you can ask the tech to show you the number they're working against and confirm whether airflow is the problem.
- Test the high-limit, rollout and auxiliary limit switches for continuity and trip points.
- Clean or replace the flame sensor and measure the microamp signal.
- Test the pressure switch and inspect the inducer, flue and condensate trap for restriction.
- Measure blower motor amperage and capacitor value.
- Inspect the heat exchanger for cracks — critical when rollout or limit trips repeat.
- Verify low-voltage wiring and the control board output.

This order lets you sanity-check a quote: airflow and flame-sense checks come before anyone proposes replacing a board or motor.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs a few minutes, shuts off, restarts when cool | High-limit trip from restricted airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Check temperature rise, test limit, clean blower |
| Fires then quits, retries, then locks out | Dirty/weak flame sensor | One breaker reset | Clean/replace flame sensor, measure microamps |
| Shuts off with inducer running; water near furnace | Pressure switch open / condensate backup | Clear visible drain clog | Test pressure switch, clear flue/trap, check inducer |
| Hard lockout, rollout indicator | Flame rollout / cracked heat exchanger | None — shut off at the switch or breaker and call a pro | Inspect heat exchanger, reset rollout, verify venting |
| Weak airflow, frequent overheat trips | Blower motor or capacitor | Replace filter | Test motor amps and capacitor |
| Random shutdowns, no clear pattern | Control board or loose wiring | Check thermostat batteries | Inspect low-voltage wiring, test/replace IFC board |

## Repair costs

Installed 2026 US pricing, parts plus labor. Condensing 90-series furnaces and models with ECM/variable-speed blowers generally land at the high end of these bands or above them.

- **Air filter:** $8–$30 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY).
- **Flame sensor clean or replace:** $90–$250.
- **Pressure switch:** $150–$350.
- **High-limit / rollout switch:** $150–$400.
- **Inducer motor:** $400–$1,200 installed on Rheem 80/90-series units.
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$350. **Blower motor:** $400–$900 for a standard PSC motor; ECM and variable-speed blowers routinely run $1,000–$1,800.
- **Integrated furnace control board:** $400–$1,200 for an IFC with diagnostics.
- **Heat exchanger (if cracked):** $1,500–$3,500+, frequently making furnace replacement the better value. On sealed-combustion 90-series furnaces, parts-plus-labor commonly exceeds $2,500 even when the part itself is covered under warranty.

Diagnostic fees of $80–$150 are typical and sometimes credited toward the repair.

## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not usually flash a specific "frozen coil" fault code — the freeze shows up as ice or frost on the indoor evaporator coil and refrigerant lines, weak or no cold air, and often water dripping from the air handler once it thaws. Some Goodman systems paired with a communicating thermostat or a modern control board may report a low-suction or airflow fault, but the physical symptom is what most homeowners notice first.

A frozen coil means the evaporator is running colder than 32°F long enough for condensation to freeze into ice. Ice acts as an insulator, so the coil stops absorbing heat — your home gets warmer even though the outdoor unit keeps running. Left unchecked, it can flood the compressor with liquid refrigerant, which is why we rate this **pro** severity even though the first fix is a simple one you can try yourself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature until it freezes.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — closing too many registers starves the coil of warm return air.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil the same way a dirty filter restricts airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This is a technician-only fix.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — not enough air moves across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating cooling below about 60°F outdoor temp can ice the coil.
7. **Stuck or failed expansion valve / TXV** — meters refrigerant incorrectly, causing localized freezing.
8. **Restricted condensate drain** causing airflow/humidity issues in tightly sealed handlers (less common).

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This stops making ice and thaws the coil. Do not run cooling while iced.
- **Replace the air filter** if it is gray, dusty, or older than 30–90 days. This alone fixes a large share of freeze-ups.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers aren't closed and return grilles aren't covered by furniture or rugs. Keep at least 80% of registers open.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and that it isn't set below 68°F while the outdoor temperature is cool.
- **Check the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and outdoor unit. A tripped breaker on the indoor blower stops airflow and can lead to freezing.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** Once thawed, a lot of water will drain. Make sure the line isn't clogged; you can clear standing water with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain outlet.
- **Wait for a full thaw** (1–3 hours) before restarting cooling. Restarting on ice just refreezes.

Do not add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or bypass any safety controls — those are technician tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the coil has fully thawed, then works through airflow before refrigerant:

- **Measures static pressure and airflow (CFM)** across the coil and blower to rule out duct, filter, or blower problems.
- **Inspects the evaporator coil** for dirt, bent fins, and ice patterns — uniform frost points to airflow; localized frost points to a metering or charge issue.
- **Checks blower motor amp draw and capacitor** to confirm the fan spins at full speed.
- **Connects gauges and takes superheat/subcooling readings** to verify refrigerant charge against Goodman's spec for that model. Low subcooling with high superheat suggests undercharge/leak.
- **Performs a leak search** (electronic sniffer, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low — because simply topping off refrigerant on a leaking system is a temporary and costly fix.
- **Tests the TXV/expansion device** if pressures indicate a metering fault.

Sanity check: if a tech quotes a refrigerant recharge without mentioning a leak search, ask why. A recharge without finding the leak means it will freeze again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm air from vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Turn off cooling, run fan, replace filter | Verify CFM, clean coil |
| Coil refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw and stop cooling | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow, blower sounds slow | Failing blower or capacitor | Check breaker; then call | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Localized frost on one coil section | Failed TXV / metering | Thaw only | Replace expansion valve |
| Water leaking from air handler | Melting ice + full drain | Clear drain with wet/dry vac | Confirm no coil leak |
| Freezes in cool/spring weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Stop cooling until warmer | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 professional.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor (ECM or PSC):** $450–$900+ depending on type.
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A units cost more).
- **TXV / expansion valve replacement:** $400–$800.
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $900–$1,800+, higher if the coil is under warranty but labor is not.

If your Goodman is still under its parts warranty, the coil part may be covered — but you'll typically still pay labor and refrigerant.

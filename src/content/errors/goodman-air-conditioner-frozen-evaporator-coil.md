## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code — Goodman air conditioners don't flash a specific error for it. It's a physical condition: the indoor coil (the CAPF, CHPF, or CAPT cabinet sitting on your furnace or in your air handler) gets so cold that condensation turns to ice on the fins.

Under normal operation the coil runs cold but stays above freezing. When something drops the coil temperature or reduces the heat reaching it, moisture freezes into a growing block of ice. You may see:

- Warm air (or no air) from vents despite the AC running
- Ice or frost on the copper refrigerant line at the indoor unit or outside condenser
- Water pooling around the furnace/air handler once the ice melts
- The system "cooling less and less" over a day or two

**Two root categories cause almost every case: not enough airflow across the coil, or not enough refrigerant in the system.** Both make the coil colder than it should be.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricts airflow so the coil starves and freezes.
2. **Blocked airflow** — closed/blocked supply registers, closed return grilles, collapsed or crushed flex duct, or a fan set too low.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust cake the fins and insulate the coil, dropping its surface temperature.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — lower pressure means a colder coil. This is a repair, never a "top-off and forget."
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — reduced CFM across the coil. Common on furnaces with ECM or PSC blowers.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below ~60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **Thermostat set too low / fan set to AUTO with long run times** in humid conditions.
8. **Clogged condensate drain** — less a freeze cause than a symptom, but a backed-up drain plus ice makes a mess and can trip a float switch.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system in a safe state. **First, turn the AC OFF at the thermostat, but set the FAN to ON** — this circulates room-temperature air to thaw the coil.

- **Air filter:** Pull it. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Set mode to OFF/COOL as needed and FAN to ON to thaw. Don't set the temperature drastically lower than room temp.
- **Supply registers:** Walk the house and open every supply vent. Closing too many rooms starves the coil.
- **Return grilles:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or filters aren't blocking the return.
- **Breaker:** If the indoor unit is off, check the furnace/air-handler breaker and switch — the blower must run to thaw and to prevent re-freezing.
- **Condensate drain:** Look for standing water in the drain pan. If your unit has a float switch and it's tripped, clearing a clogged drain line with a shop vac may reset it.
- **Let it fully thaw** (1–3+ hours) before restarting cooling. Restarting on ice makes it worse.

Do **not** open the refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, bend coil fins, or chip ice off. Those need a licensed tech.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech won't just add refrigerant. Expect them to:

1. **Confirm the coil is thawed** and inspect for ice patterns (uniform vs. localized).
2. **Measure airflow** — check filter, blower speed/CFM, coil cleanliness, and static pressure across the coil.
3. **Inspect and clean the evaporator coil** if it's dirty.
4. **Test the blower** — motor amp draw, capacitor, and speed tap (or ECM programming).
5. **Take refrigerant readings** — superheat and subcooling against Goodman's spec for your model to see if the charge is correct.
6. **Leak-search if charge is low** — with electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test. Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.
7. **Verify the metering device** (TXV or piston) is operating correctly, since a stuck TXV also causes freezing.

If a quote jumps straight to "you need refrigerant" without any airflow or leak check, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm air at vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, run fan to thaw | Measure airflow & static pressure |
| Freezes again days after new filter | Dirty coil or blower issue | Open all vents; call pro | Clean coil, test blower/capacitor |
| Frost on outdoor line, weak cooling | Low refrigerant (leak) | Thaw, then call pro | Superheat/subcool test, leak search |
| Ice + water flooding furnace base | Frozen coil + drain clog | Shop-vac the drain line | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Coil freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Don't run cooling when cool out | Confirm no other faults |
| Little airflow, blower sounds slow | Failing blower motor / cap | Note behavior; call pro | Replace capacitor or motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250 (DIY with a shop vac: ~$0)
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (PSC):** $400–$700
- **Blower motor (ECM/variable):** $600–$1,200
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant recharge (after leak repair):** $200–$700 depending on type (R-410A) and amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $900–$1,500+, more if it triggers a system-match discussion

If your system is old and leaking, ask whether repairing the coil is worth it versus replacement — a tech should give you both numbers.

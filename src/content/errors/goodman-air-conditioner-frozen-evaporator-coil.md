## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a specific "frozen evaporator coil" fault code—there is no board self-diagnostic for it. Instead, homeowners discover it by symptoms: warm air from the vents, water pooling around the indoor unit, hissing, ice on the copper refrigerant lines, or a visible block of frost on the indoor coil (found inside the furnace/air handler cabinet).

Freezing happens when the evaporator coil gets colder than 32°F. That occurs when there is **not enough warm indoor air moving across the coil** to absorb the cold, or when the refrigerant pressure is abnormally low. Moisture in the air then condenses and freezes instead of draining away, and the ice snowballs until the coil is a solid block.

Because it involves refrigerant pressures and sealed-system diagnosis, we rate this **pro**. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out airflow causes yourself, but confirming a leak or low charge requires gauges and EPA certification.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or furniture over returns reduces airflow the same way.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat transfer.
4. **Low refrigerant charge / leak** — low pressure drops coil temperature below freezing. Requires a technician.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong fan speed** — the ECM or PSC blower isn't moving rated CFM.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — using the AC below about 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **TXV or metering device stuck** — an expansion valve feeding too much or too little refrigerant.
8. **Blocked or crushed ductwork** — collapsed flex duct or a stuck damper starving the return.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system in **COOL = OFF** so the coil can thaw:

- **Turn cooling off, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to OFF for cooling, or fan-only, to melt the ice safely. Never chip or scrape it.
- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of freeze-ups.
- **Open your vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and unblocked, and returns aren't covered by rugs, furniture, or filters stacked on filters.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it isn't set to an unrealistically low temperature and that the fan is set to AUTO or ON, not stuck.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor blower has no power, the furnace/air handler breaker may be tripped—reset it once.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A frozen coil often overflows the drain pan; clear standing water and confirm the drain line isn't clogged so it doesn't cause water damage as ice melts.
- **After a full thaw**, run the AC and watch: if the coil re-freezes within a few hours despite a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a technician.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should, after confirming the coil has thawed:

1. **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil and blower to confirm the system is moving rated CFM.
2. **Inspect the filter, coil, and blower wheel** for dirt and the ECM/PSC blower for correct speed taps.
3. **Attach gauges** to read suction and head pressure, then calculate superheat and subcooling to judge the refrigerant charge.
4. **Leak-search** with an electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen if the charge is low—**adding refrigerant without finding the leak is not a repair.**
5. **Check the TXV / metering device** operation and the outdoor coil/condenser fan.
6. **Verify the condensate drain and pan** so melting ice doesn't damage the furnace or ceiling below.

A good quote names the specific cause (airflow vs. charge vs. component). Be cautious of any tech who tops off refrigerant without a leak search or airflow check.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm air at vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Turn cooling off, thaw, replace filter, open vents | Measure static pressure, clean coil |
| Ice on the copper line outside | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw, then stop running AC | Gauge charge, leak-search, repair, recharge |
| Water pooling around furnace | Melting ice overflowing pan | Clear water, check drain line | Clear drain, confirm pan safety switch |
| Coil re-freezes fast after cleaning filter | Weak blower or stuck TXV | None—call a pro | Test blower speed/ECM, check metering device |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run cooling in cold weather | Advise / low-ambient controls if needed |
| Hissing or bubbling noise | Refrigerant leak | Shut cooling off | Locate and repair leak |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + diagnostic service call:** $90–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor / ECM module replacement:** $400–$900
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$750
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,800+ (higher on newer R-32 or matched sealed-system parts, and if partly out of warranty)

Goodman coils are often covered under the parts warranty if the unit is registered; you typically still pay labor and refrigerant. Confirm your warranty status before authorizing a coil swap.

## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a specific "frozen evaporator coil" fault code. Instead, this is a physical condition you diagnose by symptoms: ice or frost forms on the indoor evaporator coil (inside the furnace or air handler cabinet) and often on the copper refrigerant line running back to the outdoor unit.

A frozen coil almost always comes down to one thing: the coil is getting too cold because either **not enough warm indoor air is flowing across it** or there is **not enough refrigerant** to carry heat away properly. When the coil surface drops below freezing, condensation on it turns to ice, which then blocks airflow further and makes the problem snowball.

The result is weak or no cooling, higher energy bills, water leaking from the indoor unit when the ice melts, and — if ignored — the risk of liquid refrigerant returning to and damaging the compressor. That last risk is why this is rated a pro-level issue.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A blocked filter starves the coil of the warm return air it needs, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust, pet hair, and biofilm coat the coil fins and insulate them, causing the same airflow starvation as a dirty filter.
3. **Blower or airflow problems** — a failing blower motor, a slipping belt (older units), collapsed or crushed ductwork, or too many closed supply/return registers.
4. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — a leak lowers pressure in the system, which lowers the coil's boiling temperature and makes it freeze. This requires a licensed tech.
5. **Running the AC in cold weather** — using cooling when outdoor temps are below roughly 60°F can drop coil temperature enough to freeze.
6. **Faulty metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** — a stuck or failing expansion valve overfeeds or underfeeds the coil.
7. **Blower running slow or wrong speed tap** — an incorrectly set blower speed after a repair or install can undersupply the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do. **First, turn the thermostat from COOL to OFF and set the fan to ON.** Running just the fan (no compressor) helps thaw the ice safely.

- **Let the coil fully thaw.** Give it 1–3 hours with cooling off and the fan running. Do not chip, scrape, or pour hot water on the ice.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the #1 cause. Swap in a clean one of the correct size and MERV rating.
- **Confirm airflow at the registers.** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture, rugs, or closed doors.
- **Check the thermostat.** Verify it's set to a reasonable cooling temperature and not stuck calling for cold air in mild weather.
- **Check the breaker.** Make sure the AC breaker hasn't tripped (this can affect the outdoor unit's operation).
- **Clear the condensate drain.** When ice melts, the pan can overflow. Vacuum the drain line at the exit point and use drain tablets to keep it clear.
- **After thawing, restart cooling and watch.** If ice returns within a day, stop and call a technician — you likely have low refrigerant or a coil/airflow issue.

Do not open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bend coil fins yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work through the airflow-versus-refrigerant question systematically. Use this to sanity-check any quote:

- **Verify the coil is fully thawed** before testing — readings are meaningless with ice present.
- **Measure airflow** and inspect the blower, filter, and ductwork. Many will check static pressure to confirm the ducts aren't restricted.
- **Inspect and clean the evaporator coil** if fins are dirty.
- **Attach gauges and take pressure/temperature readings** to check the refrigerant charge and superheat/subcooling. This is how they distinguish an airflow problem from a low charge.
- **Leak search** if the charge is low — with electronic detectors, UV dye, or a nitrogen pressure test. A good tech finds and fixes the leak rather than just "topping off."
- **Test the metering device (TXV/orifice)** and blower motor speed settings.

Be wary of any tech who adds refrigerant without searching for a leak — that's a temporary patch on a system that's losing it.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter | Turn off cooling, thaw, replace filter | Confirm airflow, check static pressure |
| Ice returns after clean filter | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw and monitor | Clean coil, inspect blower |
| Ice on outdoor line + poor cooling | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water leaking from indoor unit | Melting ice overflowing pan | Clear condensate drain | Confirm no ongoing freeze cause |
| Coil freezes in cool/mild weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Turn off cooling in cold weather | Verify no other fault |
| Weak airflow, blower struggling | Failing blower motor | None | Replace/repair blower, set speed |
| Freezes soon after a recent service | Wrong blower speed or overcharge | None | Recheck charge and blower settings |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual prices vary by region, system size, and access.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Condensate drain cleaning (DIY):** $0–$25 for tablets and a shop-vac.
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor repair/replacement:** $300–$800 depending on ECM vs. PSC motor.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100–$350 as a standalone diagnostic.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+, heavily dependent on where the leak is and how much refrigerant is needed (R-410A pricing varies).
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+, higher if the coil is discontinued or hard to access.

If your Goodman system is old and the coil is leaking, weigh coil replacement against a full system replacement — a tech can advise based on age and refrigerant type.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code on your Goodman thermostat — it's a physical condition. The indoor coil (the "A-coil" or cased coil above your furnace or inside your air handler) drops below freezing and ice forms on it. Once iced over, the coil can't absorb heat from your home's air, so the system blows warm or weak air even though the outdoor unit is running.

The root problem is almost always one of two things: **not enough air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant in the system**. Both drive the coil temperature too low, and moisture in the air freezes onto it.

This applies to essentially all Goodman split-system air conditioners and heat pumps. What varies is the coil type and metering device (fixed orifice vs. TXV), which affects how a technician diagnoses charge.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature until ice forms.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and restrict airflow even with a clean filter.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — reduced pressure lowers the coil's boiling point below freezing. Requires a licensed tech.
4. **Blower problems** — a failing blower motor, wrong speed tap, or bad capacitor moves too little air.
5. **Closed or blocked returns and vents** — too many closed registers or a blocked return starves the coil of airflow.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — cooling when it's below ~60°F outside can freeze the coil.
7. **Failed or stuck TXV / metering device** — on TXV-equipped systems, a stuck valve overfeeds or starves the coil.
8. **Clogged condensate drain causing high humidity** — less common, but standing water and humidity can contribute.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system or touch electrical components inside the unit.

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to Off (cool) and the fan to On. This thaws the coil and prevents compressor damage. Wait until you're sure all ice is melted — this can take several hours.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. Note the size printed on the frame.
- **Open your supply and return vents.** Make sure at least 80% of registers are open and nothing (furniture, rugs) is blocking the return grille.
- **Check the breaker.** If the system tripped, that's a clue — reset it once, but don't repeat if it trips again.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** Standing water or a full pan suggests a clogged drain; you can pour drain tablets in the line, but don't disassemble anything.
- **Verify thermostat settings.** Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint, and batteries are fresh if it's battery-powered.

If the coil re-freezes after a clean filter and thaw, stop and call a pro — that points to refrigerant or blower issues.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech will confirm the cause before quoting refrigerant work:

- **Confirm full thaw**, then run the system and measure **temperature split** (return vs. supply air), which should be roughly 16–22°F.
- **Measure static pressure and blower airflow (CFM)** to rule out an airflow restriction or weak blower.
- **Inspect the coil and blower wheel** for dirt buildup.
- **Attach gauges and read superheat and subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct. On fixed-orifice systems they check superheat; on TXV systems they check subcooling.
- **Leak-check** with electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen if the charge is low — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means there's a leak to find.
- **Test the blower motor and capacitor** if airflow is weak.

Be wary of any tech who "just adds refrigerant" without leak-checking — that's a temporary patch, not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines / coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter restricting airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, check static pressure |
| Re-freezes after clean filter | Dirty coil or low refrigerant | Thaw again, call pro | Clean coil or leak-check and repair |
| Warm air, outdoor unit runs | Low refrigerant from leak | None — needs a pro | Leak detection, repair, recharge |
| Little air from vents | Weak blower / bad capacitor | Check filter and vents | Test blower motor and capacitor |
| Freezes in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Don't run cooling when cold | N/A — operating condition |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Clogged condensate drain | Add drain tablets | Clear drain line, treat pan |
| Uneven coil frosting | Stuck TXV / metering device | None — needs a pro | Test and replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Airflow fixes are cheap; sealed-system repairs are not.

- **New air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY (tablets) – $150–$300 professional clearing
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors cost more)
- **Refrigerant leak detection:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A costs vary)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ if the coil itself is leaking

Systems using older or phased-out refrigerant can push recharge costs higher. Always get the leak fixed rather than just topping off.

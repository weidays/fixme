## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numeric fault code — Goodman ACs don't flash a specific code for it. It's a physical condition: the indoor evaporator coil (the A-shaped coil in your air handler or above your furnace) drops below freezing and builds a layer of frost or solid ice. You'll often see ice on the copper lines outside, water pooling near the furnace, weak or no cold air, and sometimes the outdoor unit running non-stop while the house never cools.

Ice forms when the coil can't absorb enough heat from your indoor air. That happens for one of two big reasons: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant in the system** (charge/leak problem). Both make the coil surface temperature plummet below 32°F, and the humidity in your air freezes onto it.

Some Goodman systems with a communicating or higher-end board (GSXC, GSZC) may log a low-pressure or freeze-protection event, but base-model GSX/ASX units simply keep running until you notice the symptoms. Running a frozen unit risks compressor damage, so treat this as a "stop and diagnose" situation.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A blocked filter starves the coil of warm return air.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or furniture over returns chokes airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and cut its ability to shed cold.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. This is a pro repair.
5. **Failing or weak blower motor / wrong blower speed** — a slow ECM or PSC motor moves too little air.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating below ~60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/piston)** — restricts refrigerant flow and over-cools part of the coil.
8. **Clogged condensate drain (secondary effect)** — heavy ice melt can overwhelm the drain and trip a float switch.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do not open refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, or bypass any switch.

- **Turn the system off first.** Set the thermostat to **Off**, or to **Fan Only** — running the fan with the compressor off speeds up thawing. Never run the compressor while iced.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the #1 cause. Swap it for the correct size and a moderate MERV rating (MERV 8–11); very high-MERV filters can themselves restrict airflow.
- **Open all supply registers and clear return grilles.** Move rugs, furniture, and boxes off vents. Keep most registers open.
- **Let it thaw completely.** This can take 1–24 hours. Place towels to catch drip water near the furnace/air handler.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint, and replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor blower breaker is tripped, the fan can't move air across the coil.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Thawing produces a lot of water. Clear a visibly clogged drain line with a wet/dry vac and reset any tripped float switch by clearing the water.
- **After thawing, restart and watch.** If it cools normally and stays ice-free, the filter/airflow was the culprit. If it refreezes, call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just "add Freon." Here's what a proper diagnosis looks like so you can sanity-check the quote:

- **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — readings are meaningless on ice.
- **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil and check blower speed taps or ECM programming.
- **Inspect the filter and coil** for restriction and dirt buildup.
- **Attach gauges and read pressures**, then calculate **superheat and subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct, low, or overcharged.
- **Leak-search** with an electronic detector, UV dye, or bubbles if the charge is low — a recharge without finding the leak is a temporary fix.
- **Test the metering device (TXV/piston)** for proper operation.
- **Verify the condensate system and float switch** are clear.

Be cautious of any quote to add refrigerant without a leak search. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up" — if it's low, it leaked out and will leak again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents, thaw | Check static pressure, blower speed |
| Refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Shut off, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice on outdoor copper line-set | Low charge or airflow | Thaw, replace filter | Gauge readings, superheat/subcooling |
| Water pooling near furnace | Coil thawing / clogged drain | Clear drain, vac line | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Some rooms cold, coil partly iced | Dirty coil or bad TXV | Thaw, inspect filter | Clean coil, test metering device |
| Weak air even after thaw | Failing blower motor | None safe | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Don't run AC when cold out | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + restart (DIY):** $0
- **Service call / diagnostic:** $90–$200
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor (ECM/PSC) replacement:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type — R-410A vs newer A2L blends)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$800
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,800+
- **Compressor replacement (if slugging caused damage):** $1,200–$2,800+

Older R-22 Goodman systems are the most expensive to recharge because R-22 is scarce; a large leak on an aging R-22 unit often tips the decision toward replacement.

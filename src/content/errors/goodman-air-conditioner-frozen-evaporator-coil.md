## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a digital fault code — Goodman single-stage and most two-stage ACs don't display one for this. Instead, you notice it as ice or frost on the copper lines and the indoor coil, weak or no cold air, water pooling around the air handler or furnace, or a system that runs constantly without cooling.

The evaporator coil is the indoor coil where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. For it to work, warm air must flow across it continuously. When the coil gets too cold — either because not enough warm air is reaching it, or because the refrigerant is behaving abnormally — condensation on the coil freezes into ice. That ice then blocks airflow completely, making the problem snowball.

This is a **pro-level** problem because the two most serious causes (low refrigerant from a leak, or a failing compressor/metering device) legally and safely require an EPA-certified technician. You can safely handle the airflow-related causes yourself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow, even with a clean filter.
3. **Blower running too slow or a failing blower motor** — insufficient air across the coil (wrong fan speed tap, failing ECM motor, or a bad capacitor).
4. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — furniture, closed dampers, or undersized return ducting starve the coil of airflow.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — a real and common cause. Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature, freezing it. This needs a tech.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — operating cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature.
7. **Dirty or restricted condensate drain** causing a wet, humid cabinet, combined with any of the above.
8. **Failed metering device (TXV or piston/orifice)** or a failing compressor — least common, tech-only.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the **cooling turned OFF** at the thermostat while you let any ice thaw.

- **Turn cooling off, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to "Off" for cooling but switch the fan to "On." This melts the ice safely and dries the coil. Wait until all ice is gone (1–24 hours) before running cooling again.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure the setpoint is reasonable and the fan setting works. Replace batteries if it's a battery-powered stat.
- **Look at your vents.** Open all supply registers and make sure returns aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed doors. Blocked returns starve the coil.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor unit tripped, the blower may not run — confirm the furnace/air handler breaker is on.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan or a full float switch. Pour a cup of water through and confirm it drains. Drain tablets and a wet/dry vac on the drain line can clear a mild clog.
- **Don't** chip at ice, don't add refrigerant, and don't repeatedly cycle the unit trying to force cold air.

If the coil freezes again within a day of a clean filter and clear vents, stop and call a pro — that points to refrigerant or airflow problems you can't safely fix.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works airflow-first, refrigerant-second — so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the coil is thawed** and inspect for standing ice patterns (uneven frost can hint at a charge or metering issue).
2. **Inspect the filter and evaporator coil** for dirt; check blower wheel cleanliness and motor operation.
3. **Measure airflow** — static pressure across the system, blower speed tap/ECM programming, and duct restrictions.
4. **Connect gauges and take temperatures** to check superheat and subcooling. Abnormal readings point to undercharge, overcharge, or a bad metering device (TXV/piston).
5. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low — electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen/soap. **A refrigerant top-off without finding the leak is a red flag**; the coil will just refreeze.
6. **Verify metering device and compressor** performance if airflow and charge check out.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without checking your filter, coil cleanliness, and airflow deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on lines, weak airflow, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Turn off cooling, fan on, replace filter | None needed if it stays fixed |
| Clean filter but coil still freezes | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw, run fan; inspect coil | Professional coil cleaning |
| Blower weak or noisy | Failing motor / capacitor / wrong speed | Thaw and confirm fan runs | Replace capacitor/motor, set speed |
| Water around furnace, musty smell | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, use tablets | Clear line, service float switch |
| Refreezes fast, hissing, oily residue | Refrigerant leak / low charge | Turn off, call pro | Leak search + repair + recharge |
| Uneven frost, freezes even with good airflow | Faulty TXV/piston or compressor | Turn off, call pro | Replace metering device / compressor |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Low outdoor temp + marginal charge/airflow | Don't run cooling below ~60°F | Check charge and airflow margin |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your area, unit size, and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer R-32 models) affect price.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + restart (DIY):** $0
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250 (or DIY tablets ~$15)
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$500
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (ECM) replacement:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$500
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$3,000+ (often worth comparing to a full system replacement)

Diagnostic/service call fees ($90–$200) are common and often applied toward the repair.

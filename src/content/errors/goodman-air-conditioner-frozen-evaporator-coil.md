## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners generally do **not** display a dedicated "frozen evaporator coil" fault code — most Goodman AC condensers have no digital display, and the indoor board typically flashes codes for pressure switches, blower faults or communication, not ice buildup. "Frozen evaporator coil" is a **diagnosis by symptom**, not a code you'll read off a screen.

The evaporator (indoor) coil is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. It normally runs cold but above freezing. When something drops the coil below 32°F — too little warm air moving across it, or too little refrigerant — condensation on the coil turns to frost, then a solid block of ice. Once iced, the coil can't absorb heat, so you get **weak or no cooling**, and melting ice can **overflow the drain pan** and leak water into your furnace or air handler.

This is a **pro-level** problem: thawing it is easy, but figuring out *why* it froze usually requires gauges and airflow measurement.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharge drops coil temperature and pressure below freezing. Goodman systems don't lose refrigerant with normal use, so low charge means a leak.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust/biofilm on the fins insulates the coil and blocks airflow.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong blower speed** — not enough CFM across the coil (failing ECM module, bad capacitor on PSC blowers, or wrong speed tap after a repair).
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — starves the coil of return air.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~60°F outdoors) — low ambient temps push coil temperature below freezing.
7. **Blocked condensate drain** — a full drain pan and a clogged line can accompany and worsen icing; the float switch may also shut the unit down.
8. **Failed metering device (TXV/piston)** — feeds too little refrigerant to part of the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system **off** or in **fan only**:

- **Switch to FAN ONLY / cooling OFF** at the thermostat to stop the freeze from getting worse and let the coil thaw.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A gray, matted, or 3+ month old filter is your most likely culprit. Use the correct size and don't over-restrict with a very high MERV filter your system can't push through.
- **Open and unblock all supply vents and return grilles.** Move furniture, rugs, and boxes off them. Don't run with more than a couple of vents closed.
- **Check the thermostat** — make sure it isn't set to an unrealistically low temperature that keeps the compressor running nonstop.
- **Look at the outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass, and debris from the condenser fins so the system can shed heat.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** If there's standing water, clear the drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination and add drain-pan tablets. Don't ignore a tripped float switch.
- **Let it fully thaw** (1–24 hours) before running cooling again. Running the compressor against a block of ice can damage it.

If it freezes again within a day of a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a pro — you likely have low refrigerant or a blower/airflow problem that needs instruments.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should:

- **Confirm the coil is (or was) frozen** and let it thaw fully before testing.
- **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil and verify blower speed and amp draw.
- **Inspect the evaporator coil and filter** for dirt and restriction.
- **Connect gauges** and read suction/head pressures, then check **superheat and subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct — this is how they separate an airflow problem from a refrigerant problem.
- **Leak-search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or soap bubbles) if the charge is low, rather than just "topping off" the refrigerant.
- **Test the metering device (TXV/piston)** and the blower capacitor or ECM module if airflow tests poorly.
- **Verify the condensate drain and float switch.**

Be cautious of any quote that adds refrigerant without finding a leak, or replaces the compressor before proving airflow and charge are correct.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines / indoor coil, weak cooling | Dirty filter / low airflow | Fan only to thaw, replace filter, open vents | Measure static pressure, inspect coil |
| Re-freezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running cooling, call pro | Leak search, repair, evacuate & recharge |
| Coil dirty and matted with dust | Dirty evaporator coil | Light no-rinse cleaner if accessible | Professional coil cleaning |
| Weak airflow at vents, blower loud/failing | Bad blower motor/capacitor or wrong speed | Nothing safe | Test/replace capacitor, ECM module or set blower speed |
| Water leaking from air handler as ice melts | Overflowing drain pan / clogged line | Wet/dry vac the drain line, add tablets | Clear drain, flush, replace float switch |
| Freezes on cool nights only | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Don't run cooling in cold weather | Verify low-ambient limits, add controls if needed |
| Frost on only part of the coil | Failing TXV/metering device | Nothing safe | Test and replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and contractor):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + diagnostic service call:** $90–$200
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **ECM blower motor / module replacement:** $450–$1,000
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600, depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement (if leaking internally):** $1,200–$2,500+

A clean filter and open vents fix a large share of these cases for $0–$40. Refrigerant and coil work is where costs climb — and where a proper leak search matters.

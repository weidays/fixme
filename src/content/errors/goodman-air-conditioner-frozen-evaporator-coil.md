## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners and heat pumps do not display a fault code for a frozen evaporator coil — these systems have no error display, so "frozen coil" is a **symptom you diagnose by observation**, not a code the unit reports.

A frozen evaporator coil means ice has formed on the indoor cooling coil (the A-shaped coil inside your furnace or air handler cabinet). This happens when the coil surface drops below freezing and moisture in the air condenses and freezes on it instead of draining away.

The two root drivers are almost always the same: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant** in the system. Both cause the coil to get too cold. Once ice forms, it blocks airflow further, which makes the icing worse — a runaway cycle.

Common signs: warm air from the vents, hissing or gurgling, water pooling near the furnace/air handler, visible frost or ice on the copper lines outside, and a unit that runs constantly without cooling.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — reduces airflow across the coil the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharged systems run the coil colder than designed. Recurring freeze-ups almost always point here.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and grime insulate the coil and cut heat transfer.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / dirty blower wheel** — less air moved means colder coil. More common on older ECM or PSC blowers.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below about 60°F outdoor temperature drops coil temperature and promotes icing.
7. **Thermostat set too low or running nonstop** — extended low-temperature operation can freeze a marginal system.
8. **Restricted metering device (TXV or piston) or a kinked/undersized duct** — less common, but causes chronic freezing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do without tools or opening the refrigerant system:

- **Turn off cooling, set the fan to ON.** This is the single most important step. It thaws the coil and prevents compressor damage. Allow up to 24 hours if the coil is fully iced.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, dusty, or more than 60–90 days old, swap it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open all supply registers and check returns.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed vents aren't starving the system for air. Aim for at least 80% of vents fully open.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint (not colder than 68°F). Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Check the breaker.** After thawing, if the unit won't restart, verify the breaker and the furnace/air-handler switch are on.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it thaws. Make sure the drain pan and line aren't overflowing; place towels down and, if the line is clogged, clear it with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination.

Do **not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or bend the copper lines. If the coil refreezes after a clean filter and open vents, that's a pro-level refrigerant or airflow diagnosis.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will confirm the cause rather than just topping off refrigerant. Expect them to:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — measurements are useless with ice present.
2. **Measure static pressure and airflow** to rule out duct, filter, or blower restrictions.
3. **Inspect the blower wheel and evaporator coil** for dirt buildup.
4. **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to determine if the charge is correct. Low subcooling with high superheat suggests undercharge/leak; other patterns point to a metering device.
5. **Perform a leak search** (electronic sniffer, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low. A pro should never just "add a pound" without finding the leak.
6. **Check the TXV or piston** operation if airflow and charge are normal.

If a quote jumps straight to "recharge refrigerant" with no leak search and no airflow check, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, fan ON to thaw, open vents | Measure static pressure, clean blower |
| Coil refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Thaw, then call a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Frost on outdoor copper lines | Low charge or airflow | Thaw system, check filter | Gauge readings, subcooling test |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Thawing ice overflowing drain | Clear condensate line, place towels | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Long runtimes, never cools | Undercharge or dirty coil | Fan ON to thaw, inspect filter | Full charge and coil inspection |
| Icing only in cool weather | Running below ~60°F outside | Turn AC off when cool | Verify low-ambient limits |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and contractor):

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thaw + system check / diagnostic fee:** $80–$180
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Blower wheel cleaning or blower motor replacement:** $150–$700 (motor type dependent)
- **Evaporator coil cleaning (chemical):** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant recharge (R-410A):** $200–$700+ depending on amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$700
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,500+ (coil, refrigerant, and labor)

A one-time freeze fixed by a new filter can cost you nothing but a filter. Recurring freeze-ups tied to a refrigerant leak are the expensive scenario — and older R-22 systems can cost far more due to refrigerant availability.

## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a digital error code on most Goodman air conditioners — it's a physical condition you'll see or feel: a block of ice on the indoor coil (inside the air handler or on top of the furnace) and often on the copper refrigerant line running outside to the condenser.

The evaporator coil gets cold by design — that's how it removes heat from your home's air. But if it drops below freezing and stays there, humidity condenses and freezes into ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, cooling gets worse, and the ice grows. Eventually you get warm air, water leaking from the air handler as it melts, or a shutdown.

There are only two root categories: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant / wrong pressures** (refrigerant problem). Everything below sorts into one of those two.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of the warm return air it needs to stay above freezing.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too-few supply/return vents** — furniture over registers, closed doors, or an undersized return reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust caked on the coil fins blocks airflow even with a clean filter.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. If freezing recurs after thawing, this is the prime suspect.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the blower can't push enough air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating cooling below ~62°F outdoors can freeze the coil; not a fault, just misuse.
7. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/piston)** — feeds the wrong amount of refrigerant to the coil.
8. **Collapsed or disconnected ductwork** — chokes airflow, common in attics and crawlspaces.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do them **after** shutting the AC off to let the coil thaw.

- **Turn the thermostat to OFF (cooling) but the fan to ON.** This runs the blower without cooling and thaws the ice faster and safely. Wait 1–3 hours.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Walk your vents.** Open every supply register, move furniture and rugs off them, and open interior doors so return air can circulate.
- **Check the breaker.** If the outdoor unit tripped, the coil can freeze from imbalance — but only reset a breaker **once**. Repeated tripping means stop and call a pro.
- **Look for water at the air handler / furnace base.** Melting ice drains through the condensate pan and line. Make sure the condensate drain isn't clogged, or you'll get water damage as it thaws.
- **Check the thermostat batteries** if your model uses them, and confirm it's set to a reasonable temperature (not, say, 60°F on a cool day).

Do **not** chip, scrape, or blow-dry the ice — you can puncture the coil, which turns a filter swap into a coil replacement.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech works the airflow-vs-refrigerant fork:

1. **Confirms it's fully thawed** before any pressure readings — measuring on iced coils gives false numbers.
2. **Measures static pressure and airflow** across the coil and checks blower amperage, capacitor, and filter/duct condition.
3. **Connects gauges and takes superheat and subcooling readings.** Low pressures with high superheat point to low charge (a leak); other patterns point to a metering-device or airflow issue.
4. **Leak-searches** with an electronic detector or UV dye if the charge is low — reputable techs find and fix the leak rather than just "topping off" refrigerant.
5. **Inspects the coil itself** for dirt and the metering device (TXV/piston) for proper operation.

Red flag on a quote: if a tech wants to add refrigerant without doing a leak search, push back. Adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary, and now often illegal for certain refrigerants without repair, fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air + visible ice on coil/line | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Fan ON to thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, check blower amps |
| Freezes again days after filter change | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running the AC; call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water pooling at furnace/air handler | Ice melting + clogged drain | Clear visible drain clog, mop up | Clear condensate line, add safety switch |
| Weak airflow at every register | Failing blower or bad capacitor | Check filter first | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Ice only in cool weather | Running AC below ~62°F outside | Switch to fan or heat | Confirm no other fault |
| Clean filter but coil still dirty | Dust-caked coil fins | None (coil access needed) | Professional coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your price varies by region and contractor.

- **Filter replacement:** $15–$50 (DIY).
- **Thaw + diagnostic service call:** $75–$200.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Blower motor (ECM):** $400–$900 installed.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer A2L blends).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ installed; higher if the coil is out of warranty.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800 installed.

If the coil or compressor is failing and your unit is older, get a quote comparing repair to replacement — repeated freeze damage can shorten compressor life.

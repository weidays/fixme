## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners don't display a "frozen evaporator coil" error code — this is a **symptom diagnosis**, not a fault code on a board. You'll notice it as weak or no cold air, ice or frost on the copper lines or the indoor coil, water pooling around the air handler or furnace, or a compressor that runs constantly without cooling the house.

The evaporator coil is the indoor coil where refrigerant absorbs heat from your air. It normally runs cold but stays above freezing because a steady stream of warm household air flows across it. When that warm airflow drops — or when the refrigerant pressure drops too low — the coil surface falls below 32°F, condensation freezes, and ice snowballs. Once iced over, the coil can't transfer heat at all, so cooling stops.

**Bottom line:** a frozen coil is almost always a warning sign of an airflow problem or a refrigerant problem. Thawing it is easy; finding out *why* it froze is the real repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm air, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents, or dirty evaporator coil** — reduces airflow across the coil the same way a bad filter does.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge means low pressure, which lowers the coil's boiling temperature and freezes it. This is a professional refrigerant repair, not a DIY recharge.
4. **Failing or slow blower motor / capacitor** — if the indoor fan runs weak, it can't push enough air across the coil.
5. **Running the AC in cold weather (below ~62°F outdoors)** — low outdoor temps drop indoor coil temp; common when people run cooling on a cool night.
6. **Clogged condensate drain causing a safety float switch to cut the compressor** — less a freeze cause, more a related shutdown; but standing water and airflow issues often appear together.
7. **Stuck or failing metering device (TXV / fixed orifice)** — over-feeds or under-feeds refrigerant, causing localized freezing. Requires a technician.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no refrigerant handling, no opening the sealed system.

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** At the thermostat, set the system to OFF and the fan to ON. This thaws the coil without making more ice. Let it run until all frost is gone and the coil is dry (1–24 hours).
- **Replace the air filter.** Pull it out and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it with the correct size. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers aren't closed, and that return grilles and furniture aren't blocking airflow. Never close off more than a couple of vents.
- **Check the breaker and thermostat batteries.** Confirm the indoor unit has power and the thermostat is set correctly. If your thermostat takes batteries and they're weak, replace them.
- **Clear the condensate drain.** If you see water around the furnace/air handler, a shop vac on the outdoor end of the condensate line can pull out a clog. Drain tablets help prevent regrowth.
- **After thawing:** run cooling again. If it freezes a second time, stop and call a pro — the cause is internal.

**Do not** scrape or chip ice off the coil, add refrigerant, or bypass the condensate float switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the frozen coil, then hunts the root cause so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Thaws the coil fully** and inspects the filter, coil face, and blower wheel for dirt or restriction.
2. **Measures airflow** — checks blower speed, static pressure, and whether the blower motor and run capacitor are healthy.
3. **Connects gauges and takes temperatures** to measure **superheat and subcooling**. Low charge shows a specific pressure/temperature signature that points to a refrigerant leak versus an airflow problem.
4. **Leak searches** if the charge is low — electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test. A recharge without a leak fix is a red flag.
5. **Checks the metering device (TXV/orifice)** if pressures are odd but the charge and airflow look correct.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without checking your filter and airflow first, ask why.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil or copper lines | Frozen coil (any cause) | Set cooling OFF, fan ON; let it thaw fully | Confirm root cause after thaw |
| Weak airflow + dirty filter | Restricted filter | Replace filter, retest | Check blower & static pressure |
| Freezes again after clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Stop; call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Melt water + clogged drain | Clear condensate line with shop vac | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Coil dirty and matted | Restricted coil face | None (leave to pro) | Deep-clean evaporator coil |
| Weak fan, humming at startup | Blower capacitor/motor | Replace filter; call pro | Test/replace capacitor or motor |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Running AC below ~62°F out | Don't run cooling in cold weather | Usually no repair needed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges. Your price varies by region and contractor.

- **New air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY with a shop vac; $75–$200 as a service call.
- **Evaporator coil deep cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Blower run capacitor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Blower motor (ECM or PSC):** $400–$900+ installed, depending on type.
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $100–$350.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500+, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs older systems).
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$700.
- **Full evaporator coil replacement (if the coil itself leaks):** $900–$2,500+.

A simple filter-and-thaw fix is often free. Anything involving refrigerant is professional work that requires EPA certification.

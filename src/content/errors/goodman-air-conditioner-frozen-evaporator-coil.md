## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do **not** display a fault code for a frozen evaporator coil — there is no sensor that reports "coil iced over." Instead, you'll notice weak or no cooling, ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, and sometimes water pooling near the air handler or furnace when the ice melts.

A frozen evaporator coil happens when the indoor coil gets so cold that condensation on it freezes into ice. Two conditions cause this: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant** in the system. Both drop the coil temperature below freezing, and once ice forms it insulates the coil, making the problem snowball until cooling stops entirely.

This is a **pro-level** issue because while some causes (a clogged filter) are homeowner-fixable, repeated freezing usually points to a refrigerant leak or a blower/airflow fault that requires gauges and EPA-certified handling.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — the single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the coil of airflow, letting it freeze.
2. **Restricted airflow from a dirty evaporator coil** — dust builds on the coil fins over years and chokes airflow even with a clean filter.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. Goodman systems don't "use up" refrigerant, so low charge always means a leak.
4. **Blower motor problem** — a failing ECM/PSC blower, wrong fan speed setting, or seized bearings reduce airflow.
5. **Closed or blocked supply/return vents** — too many closed registers reduce total airflow across the coil.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — using cooling below ~60 °F outdoor temp can freeze the coil.
7. **Dirty or collapsed ductwork** — undersized or crushed returns restrict airflow long-term.
8. **Thermostat set to "fan auto" with a low-airflow issue** — makes freeze cycles worse; not a root cause but a contributor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — **do not open the sealed refrigerant system or handle refrigerant.**

1. **Turn off cooling, run the fan.** Set thermostat to **OFF** for cooling and **FAN: ON**. This thaws the coil in 1–4 hours. Never chip at ice.
2. **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, dusty, or you can't see light through it, swap it. Do this while the coil thaws.
3. **Open all supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow. Aim for at least 80% of vents fully open.
4. **Check the thermostat setting.** Confirm it's on Cool with a reasonable setpoint (not set 10°+ below room temp).
5. **Check the breaker.** Confirm the AC and air-handler/furnace breakers are on.
6. **Check the condensate drain.** A melting frozen coil dumps a lot of water — make sure the drain pan isn't overflowing and the drain line isn't clogged.
7. **Look at the outdoor unit.** Clear leaves and debris from around the condenser so it can reject heat.

After thawing and installing a clean filter, restart cooling. **If it freezes again within a day or two, stop and call a pro** — you likely have a refrigerant or blower issue.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech will:

- **Fully thaw the coil first** — you can't measure a frozen system accurately.
- **Measure airflow** across the coil (temperature split and static pressure) to confirm whether it's an airflow or refrigerant problem.
- **Attach refrigerant gauges** and check superheat/subcooling against Goodman's spec for your specific condenser and coil match.
- **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup and the blower wheel/motor for correct speed and operation.
- **Leak-test** with an electronic detector or UV dye if the charge is low — a legitimate recharge should always include finding the leak.
- **Check the metering device** (TXV or piston) for restriction.

Sanity check: if a tech says "just needs a top-off of refrigerant" without leak-testing, be skeptical. Refrigerant doesn't disappear on its own; a low charge means a leak that will recur.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow at vents | Dirty filter or coil | Thaw + replace filter | Clean evaporator coil, check blower |
| Ice on outdoor copper line (suction line) | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw only | Leak test, repair, recharge |
| Coil re-freezes after clean filter | Low charge or blower fault | Thaw, then call pro | Diagnose charge vs. airflow |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Melting ice overflowing pan | Clear condensate drain | Confirm and fix freeze cause |
| Freezes only on mild/cool days | Running AC below ~60 °F outdoors | Don't run cooling in cool weather | Verify no other fault |
| No cooling at all, coil solid ice | Advanced freeze, any cause | Thaw fully before service | Full diagnostic |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40 — often the entire fix.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Refrigerant leak detection:** $100–$350.
- **Refrigerant recharge (after leak repair):** $150–$600+ depending on refrigerant type (R-410A) and amount.
- **Leak repair (accessible joint or Schrader valve):** $150–$500.
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $900–$2,000+ including refrigerant.
- **Blower motor replacement (ECM):** $450–$900; PSC motor $200–$500.
- **Metering device (TXV) replacement:** $400–$800.

A frozen coil that turns out to be a dirty filter costs **$0–$40**. A frozen coil from a leaking evaporator coil can reach **$1,200+**. Most repeat-freeze jobs land in the $400–$1,200 range once diagnosed.

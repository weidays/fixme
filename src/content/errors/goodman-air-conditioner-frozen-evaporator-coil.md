## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault *code* — Goodman ACs won't flash a specific number for it. It's a physical condition: the indoor evaporator coil (the A-coil inside your air handler or above your furnace) drops below freezing and ice builds up on it. Once iced over, the coil can't absorb heat, so your home stops cooling even though the system is running hard.

The root cause is almost always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant** (a charge or leak problem). Both drop the coil temperature below the dew point and then below 32°F, and moisture freezes solid.

If your Goodman is running but not cooling and you see ice or water dripping, this page is for you. If there's no ice at all, see the related "Running But Not Cooling" article instead.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil of warm return air.
2. **Blocked return vents or closed supply registers** — furniture, rugs, or too many closed rooms cut total airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat transfer over time.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — a genuine leak lowers pressure and coil temperature. This is a pro repair, not a "top-off."
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / wrong fan speed** — reduced CFM across the coil. Common on aging ECM or PSC blowers.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~60°F outdoors) — the system isn't designed for it and can ice up.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — a full drain pan and backed-up water can contribute to and hide a freezing problem.
8. **Undersized or restricted ductwork** — chronic low airflow that keeps refreshing the freeze.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all safe for a homeowner. Do not open the sealed refrigerant system or handle refrigerant.

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the thermostat fan to ON.** This is step one. It thaws the coil safely and prevents compressor damage. Wait until all ice is gone (1–3+ hours).
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or older than 60–90 days, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of freeze-ups.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and unobstructed, and return grilles aren't covered by furniture or rugs.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool and a reasonable temperature — not set so low the system never cycles off.
- **Check the breaker.** After thawing, confirm the AC and air-handler breakers are on.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan or a wet floor near the air handler. Clearing a clogged drain line with a wet/dry vac is DIY-safe.
- **After thawing, restart in Cool** and watch it. If it cools normally and stays ice-free, the filter/airflow was your culprit. If it refreezes within a day, call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the steps helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the freeze is fully thawed** before testing — you can't measure anything on an iced coil.
2. **Checks airflow:** filter, coil cleanliness, blower speed/CFM, and static pressure across the ducts.
3. **Measures superheat and subcooling** with gauges to determine whether the refrigerant charge is correct. Low subcooling / high superheat points to undercharge or a leak.
4. **Leak search** if the charge is low — electronic sniffer, UV dye, or a nitrogen pressure test. A reputable tech will not just "add refrigerant" without finding the leak.
5. **Inspects the blower motor and capacitor** if airflow is weak.
6. **Verifies the metering device (TXV/piston)** if pressures are erratic.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without checking airflow or searching for a leak first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines/coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Turn off, thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, clean coil |
| Refreezes within a day of a new filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off, thaw, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water dripping, no cooling | Thawing coil / clogged drain | Clear drain line, mop up | Clear drain, confirm slope/trap |
| Weak airflow from vents even when clean | Failing blower / wrong speed | None safe | Test blower motor, capacitor, CFM |
| Coil ices only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run AC when cold out | Confirm no other fault |
| Coil dirty despite good filter | Old coil buildup | None (leave sealed system) | Deep-clean evaporator coil |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your area, model, and refrigerant type affect price.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw and restart (DIY):** $0
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $75–$250 (service call) or ~$15 DIY
- **Evaporator coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$400
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor replacement (ECM/PSC):** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$500
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ (R-410A/R-32 units vary)

A simple airflow fix costs nearly nothing. Refrigerant work is where cost climbs — because a leak means the coil will refreeze until it's actually sealed.

## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a numeric fault code for a frozen evaporator coil — most residential Goodman condensers and air handlers have no coil-icing diagnostic. You discover the problem by symptom: the system runs but blows warm air, you see frost or a solid block of ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, or you find water pooling around the air handler as the ice melts.

The evaporator coil (the indoor coil) is designed to run cold, but never below freezing. When something drops the coil temperature under 32°F, humidity from your indoor air freezes onto it instead of draining away. That ice then blocks airflow, which makes the coil even colder — a runaway cycle that ends with a coil-shaped block of ice and no cooling.

The two root categories are **not enough air moving across the coil** or **not enough heat in the refrigerant** (a low charge from a leak). Both starve the coil of the warmth it needs to stay above freezing.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter chokes airflow, the coil goes cold, ice forms.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — an undercharged system runs the coil below freezing. Goodman systems don't consume refrigerant, so low charge always means a leak.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and block airflow, mimicking a filter problem.
4. **Blower or fan problem** — a failing blower motor, wrong blower speed tap, or a bad ECM module moves too little air.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — reduces total airflow through the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating the system when outdoor temps are below about 60°F can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV or piston)** — meters too much refrigerant into the coil, over-cooling it. More common on TXV-equipped Goodman coils.
8. **Kinked or crushed refrigerant line / restricted filter drier** — restricts flow and drops coil pressure.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — they resolve a large share of icing calls for free.

- **Turn the system OFF, fan ON.** At the thermostat, set the mode to OFF and the fan to ON. This runs the blower without cooling so the coil thaws. Do not chip or scrape the ice.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or older than 1–3 months, swap it. A cheap pleated filter is the fix in a huge number of cases.
- **Open and unblock all supply vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, and closed dampers aren't choking airflow. Keep return grilles clear.
- **Check the thermostat setting.** Very low setpoints on humid days encourage icing. Set a reasonable temperature (72–75°F).
- **Check the breaker.** After a thaw, if the system won't restart, verify the AC breaker and the air handler switch are on.
- **Check the condensate drain.** As the coil thaws, water must drain. Confirm the drain line isn't clogged and the pan isn't overflowing. Add a condensate tablet to keep the line clear.
- **Let it fully thaw before restarting.** Wait until all visible ice is gone — this can take several hours to a full day.

If the coil refreezes after a clean filter and a full thaw, stop running the AC and call a pro. Continuing to run risks compressor damage.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified HVAC tech will confirm the cause with gauges and airflow measurements — not guesswork. Expect them to:

1. Verify the coil is fully thawed, then run the system and **connect refrigerant gauges** to read suction and head pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling.
2. **Measure airflow** — check filter, coil cleanliness, blower speed setting, and static pressure across the coil to rule out an airflow problem before touching refrigerant.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt and biofilm.
4. **Test the blower motor / ECM** and confirm it's running at the correct speed tap for your tonnage.
5. If the charge is low, **perform a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just topping off refrigerant.
6. Check the **metering device (TXV or piston)** for proper operation if pressures are off but airflow is good.

A good tech will not simply "add a pound of Freon" and leave. Adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary fix and, on R-410A systems, an expensive one. Insist on a leak diagnosis if the charge is low.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, ice on indoor coil, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | None if it stays fixed |
| Ice on the copper suction line at the condenser | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off and thaw; then call | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Coil refreezes within hours of a clean filter | Leak, dirty coil, or bad blower | Stop running; call a pro | Gauges, coil clean, airflow test |
| Water on floor near air handler | Thawing ice + clogged drain | Clear drain, add tablet | Flush drain, confirm root cause |
| Weak airflow from all vents even when thawed | Failing blower / wrong speed | Check filter and vents | Test/replace ECM, reset speed tap |
| Icing only when it's cool outside | Low outdoor temp operation | Don't run AC below ~60°F | Add low-ambient controls if needed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual price varies by region, tonnage, and whether your Goodman is still under its parts warranty (registered Goodman units carry a 10-year parts warranty; labor is separate).

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $100–$350.
- **Refrigerant recharge (R-410A):** $150–$600+ depending on how much is lost.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$800.
- **Blower motor or ECM module:** $400–$900.
- **Evaporator coil leak repair or coil replacement:** $900–$2,000+ (coil part may be under warranty; labor and refrigerant are not).

If a leak is found in the evaporator coil itself and the system uses older R-22, replacement of the coil or the whole system is often more cost-effective than repair — ask your tech to price both.

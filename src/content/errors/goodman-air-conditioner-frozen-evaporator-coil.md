## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code on your Goodman thermostat — it's a physical condition you'll see or feel: ice or frost on the indoor coil, on the copper refrigerant lines near your air handler or furnace, or reduced airflow paired with warm air at the vents.

The evaporator coil is the indoor part of your AC that absorbs heat from your home's air. It runs cold by design. When something makes it run *too* cold — usually poor airflow or low refrigerant — moisture on the coil freezes into ice instead of draining away. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil and blocks airflow, which makes it freeze even faster. The result is a runaway problem: your system runs constantly but cools less and less.

This is tagged **pro severity** because the two most common root causes (low refrigerant, blower/compressor faults) require licensed handling, and because running the unit frozen risks the compressor — the most expensive part in the system.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing. Cheap and DIY-fixable.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — closed registers, blocked returns, or a collapsed flex duct starve the coil of airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins act like the filter clog, insulating the coil and reducing airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — low pressure lowers the coil's boiling point, so it runs colder and ices up. Needs a technician; refrigerant is not DIY.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / wrong fan speed** — a weak ECM or PSC blower moves too little air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — running cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F can freeze the coil on many Goodman systems.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up onto the coil** — less common, but standing water can freeze and worsen icing.
8. **Failed metering device (TXV/orifice)** — a stuck expansion valve over- or under-feeds the coil. Pro diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner:

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan to ON.** Set the thermostat mode to *Off* (no cooling) but the fan to *On*. This thaws the coil safely with warm air while you wait — do not run the compressor.
- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first; it fixes a large share of freeze-ups.
- **Walk the house and open vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or curtains.
- **Check the breaker and thermostat.** Confirm the system has power and the thermostat is set to a realistic temperature (not, say, 60°F on a cool night).
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** If you see standing water or a full drain pan, the drain line may be clogged — a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain outlet can clear light clogs.
- **Let it fully thaw before restarting.** Only turn cooling back on once all ice is gone (1–3 hours). If it freezes again quickly, stop and call a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bend coil fins. Those aren't DIY tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech works from airflow outward before touching refrigerant:

1. **Confirms it's fully thawed** and inspects the filter, coil, and blower wheel for dirt or damage.
2. **Measures airflow / static pressure** across the coil to catch duct restrictions or a weak blower, and verifies the blower speed tap or ECM programming matches the system.
3. **Checks the blower motor** amp draw and operation — a failing ECM module or PSC capacitor is common.
4. **Attaches gauges and takes temperatures** to measure superheat and subcooling. Low charge with high superheat points to a leak; abnormal readings point to a TXV/orifice problem.
5. **Performs a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low — you should not pay for a "top-off" without a leak search.
6. **Inspects the condensate system** and metering device.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without checking the filter, coil cleanliness, and airflow first, get a second opinion — you may be paying to refill a leak that will just re-empty.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm air at vents | Dirty filter or blocked airflow | Turn cooling off/fan on, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, check blower speed |
| Coil re-freezes soon after thawing | Low refrigerant from a leak | Thaw fully, then stop and call a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice on the copper line outside | Low charge or restricted metering device | Shut off cooling to protect compressor | Superheat/subcooling test, TXV check |
| Weak airflow even with clean filter | Dirty coil or failing blower | Inspect blower wheel; replace filter | Clean coil, test/replace blower motor |
| Water pooling around air handler | Ice thawing or clogged condensate drain | Clear drain line with wet/dry vac | Clear/repair drain, add safety switch |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Don't run cooling in cold weather | Advise on low-ambient operation |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual price depends on region, access, and whether refrigerant is involved.

- **New air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200 by a pro; often DIY-free with a wet/dry vac.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400 depending on access.
- **Blower capacitor (PSC systems):** $150–$350 installed.
- **ECM blower motor or module:** $400–$900 installed.
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair:** $300–$1,000+ depending on leak location.
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $150–$600+ depending on system charge and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer refrigerants).
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800 installed.
- **Compressor replacement (if damaged by running frozen):** $1,200–$2,500+ — the reason you shut cooling off early.

The single most expensive outcome is a compressor killed by liquid slugging while the coil is iced. Turning cooling off at the first sign of ice is the cheapest thing you'll ever do for this system.

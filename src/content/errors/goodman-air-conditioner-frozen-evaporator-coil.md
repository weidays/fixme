## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a specific fault code for a frozen evaporator coil — it is a physical symptom, not an electronic error. The evaporator coil is the indoor coil (inside your air handler or above your furnace) that absorbs heat from your home's air. When something disrupts the balance of airflow and refrigerant, the coil surface drops below freezing and condensation turns to ice.

Once ice forms, it insulates the coil and blocks airflow further, so the problem snowballs. You'll notice weak or no cold air, higher humidity, water leaking from the indoor unit when the ice melts, and sometimes visible frost on the copper refrigerant line outside.

**Important:** Running the compressor while the coil is frozen can allow liquid refrigerant to return to the compressor ("slugging"), which can permanently damage it. Turn cooling OFF as soon as you suspect icing.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — The single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the coil of airflow, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Low charge lowers the pressure and temperature in the coil, causing ice. Goodman systems don't "use up" refrigerant, so low charge means a leak.
3. **Blocked or dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and biofilm on the coil fins restrict airflow just like a dirty filter.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong fan speed** — Insufficient air movement over the coil. Common with aging ECM or PSC blower motors.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — Reduces total system airflow.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — Operating below roughly 62°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil on systems without a low-ambient kit.
7. **Dirty or clogged condensate drain backing up onto the coil** — Less common but contributes to icing and water damage.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. They do **not** involve opening the sealed refrigerant system or bypassing any safety controls.

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** At the thermostat, set the system to OFF and the fan to ON. This runs the blower to melt the ice safely. Wait until you're sure it's fully thawed (up to 24 hours for heavy ice).
- **Check and replace the air filter.** Hold it to the light — if you can't see through it, replace it. Do this before restarting.
- **Confirm the thermostat settings.** Make sure it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint (not, say, 60°F on a mild day).
- **Look at your vents and registers.** Open all supply vents and make sure furniture, rugs, or curtains aren't blocking returns.
- **Check the breaker.** If the outdoor unit tripped, a frozen coil combined with a dead compressor can point to a bigger issue — note it for the technician.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** Look for standing water or a full drain line. You can pour a cup of water in to confirm it drains; use drain tablets to keep it clear.
- **Do not chip, scrape, or blow-torch the ice.** You can puncture the coil and turn a cheap fix into a coil replacement.

After a full thaw, put in a clean filter and restart. If it freezes again within a day or two, you have a deeper cause — call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified HVAC tech will work through the airflow-versus-refrigerant question systematically. Use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirm the coil is thawed** and inspect for water damage.
2. **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the system and check blower speed and motor operation.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil and filter** for restriction and biofilm.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and measure suction/discharge pressures, superheat, and subcooling — this reveals whether the charge is correct.
5. **If low on refrigerant, perform a leak search** using electronic detectors, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure testing. A recharge without finding the leak is a temporary fix, and a good tech will say so.
6. **Check the metering device (TXV or piston)** if pressures suggest a restriction.

A trustworthy quote should identify a *root cause* — "your filter was clogged" or "you have a leak at the coil" — not just "we added Freon."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, ice on indoor coil | Dirty filter | Replace filter, thaw fully, restart | Verify airflow, check blower speed |
| Frost on outdoor copper line, low cooling | Low refrigerant (leak) | Shut off, call pro | Gauge readings, leak search, repair & recharge |
| Coil ices even with clean filter | Dirty evaporator coil | Shut off, call pro | Chemically clean coil |
| Blower runs weak or not at all | Failing blower motor / wrong speed | Check breaker only | Test & replace motor, set speed |
| Ice returns quickly after thaw | Refrigerant leak or metering issue | Shut off, call pro | Diagnose TXV/piston and charge |
| Water pooling around air handler | Melted ice or clogged drain | Clear drain, thaw coil | Confirm no coil/pan damage |
| Icing on cool days only | No low-ambient control | Avoid running AC below ~62°F | Recommend low-ambient kit if needed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your price varies by region and contractor.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10 – $30
- **Thawing the coil (DIY):** $0
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150 – $400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $450 – $900 (ECM motors cost more than PSC)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150 – $450
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400 – $1,200+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing varies)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500 – $900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,500 – $2,900 if the coil itself leaks
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90 – $180 (often applied to the repair)

If your Goodman system is under 10 years old, ask whether the coil or compressor is still under Goodman's parts warranty — you may only pay labor.

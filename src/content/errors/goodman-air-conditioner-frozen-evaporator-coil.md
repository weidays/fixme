## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners don't usually flash a specific "frozen coil" error code — you'll notice it as **ice buildup on the indoor evaporator coil, the copper refrigerant lines, or the air handler**, along with weak or no cold air. On some communicating Goodman systems (ComfortNet / CoolCloud-enabled boards), you may see a low-pressure or airflow fault instead.

A frozen evaporator coil is a **symptom, not a root cause**. The coil freezes when it gets colder than 32°F, which happens when there isn't enough warm indoor air moving across it, or when the refrigerant charge is wrong. Once ice forms, it blocks airflow further, making the problem snowball.

Because the two most serious causes — low refrigerant and a failing blower — require sealed-system or electrical work, we rate this **pro-level**. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out airflow issues yourself first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — the single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the coil of warm return air, so it drops below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too few open registers restricts airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat transfer, promoting ice.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / wheel** — a slow or dust-caked blower can't move enough air. Common on aging ECM and PSC blowers.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — under-charged systems run cold enough to freeze. This is a sealed-system repair, never a DIY top-off.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — using cooling when it's below ~60°F outside can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — less common, but standing water and poor drainage can contribute to icing and water damage.
8. **Failed metering device (TXV / orifice)** — a stuck expansion valve mis-meters refrigerant into the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these in order. Stop and call a pro if the coil refreezes after you've corrected airflow.

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This stops making ice and lets the blower thaw the coil faster. Never chip or scrape ice off — you can puncture the coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray or clogged, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen-coil calls.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers aren't closed, and that return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs. Keep enough registers open per your system's design.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint, and that "Auto" fan mode isn't hiding a blower problem.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor blower (air handler/furnace) breaker is tripped, the fan can't run and the coil will freeze.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** If you see standing water, the drain may be clogged. Clearing a wet-vac accessible drain or adding drain tablets is safe.
- **Let it thaw fully — 1 to 24 hours** — before restarting cooling. Restarting on ice can slug the compressor.

Do **not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or bypass any safety controls. Refrigerant handling requires EPA certification.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech will confirm the *root cause*, not just thaw the coil. Expect them to:

- Verify the coil is fully thawed, then measure **airflow / static pressure** across the coil and check blower amp draw and speed setting.
- Inspect the **filter, coil cleanliness, and blower wheel** for dust loading.
- Attach gauges to read **suction and head pressure**, then calculate **superheat and subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct — this tells them whether it's a refrigerant problem.
- If charge is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic sniffer, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just topping off.
- Test the **metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** if pressures point to mis-metering.
- Confirm the **condensate drain** flows freely.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or airflow check is a red flag — you'll be back with a frozen coil in weeks.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Turn off cooling, replace filter, let thaw | Verify airflow after thaw |
| Weak airflow from vents, some closed | Blocked registers | Open vents, remove obstructions | Check total static pressure |
| Coil refreezes after filter change | Low refrigerant leak | Stop running, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Blower runs slow or noisy | Failing blower motor/wheel | Note behavior for tech | Test/clean/replace blower |
| Water around indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, check pan/float switch |
| Ice only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Avoid cooling in cold temps | Confirm no other fault |
| Coil dirty/matted with dust | Fouled evaporator coil | None — leave to pro | Professional coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Diagnostic/service call fees ($90–$200) often apply and may be credited toward a repair.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Clearing a condensate drain:** $0 DIY with a wet-vac; $100–$250 for a pro.
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$450 depending on access.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $300–$700 (PSC) to $600–$1,200 (ECM/variable-speed).
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs older R-22 is far more expensive).
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500+ (higher if under warranty parts but out-of-warranty labor).

If your Goodman is under its 10-year parts warranty, sealed-system parts may be covered — but **labor and refrigerant usually are not**. Ask before authorizing work.

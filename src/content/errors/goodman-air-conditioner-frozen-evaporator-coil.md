## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code — Goodman air conditioners don't flash a specific digit for it. Instead, you'll notice ice on the indoor coil or on the copper refrigerant lines, weak or no cold air, and sometimes water pooling around the air handler or furnace when the ice melts.

The evaporator coil is the indoor coil that absorbs heat from your home's air. It only works when enough warm air flows across it and the refrigerant charge is correct. When either drops too low, the coil surface falls below 32°F and condensation freezes into ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, airflow drops further, and the problem snowballs until the whole coil is a block of frost.

The freeze is a **symptom**. The real job is finding which of two problem families is behind it: **restricted airflow** or **low refrigerant**. Airflow issues are often DIY-fixable; refrigerant issues are pro-only because handling refrigerant requires EPA certification.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of the warm airflow it needs to stay above freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers, blocked returns, or furniture over vents chokes airflow just like a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust film on the coil fins insulate it and cut airflow, dropping surface temperature below freezing.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — undercharged systems run the coil too cold. On Goodman TXV systems this shows up faster than on piston (fixed-orifice) systems.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — a weak blower moves too little air. Common on single-speed PSC blowers with a tired run capacitor.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating cooling below ~60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil even on a healthy system.
7. **Kinked or crushed ductwork / collapsed flex duct** — restricts airflow to one part of the system.
8. **Stuck-closed TXV or metering device** — starves the coil of refrigerant flow (pro diagnosis only).

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — several are genuinely DIY.

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** At your thermostat, set the system to OFF and the fan to ON. This thaws the coil without adding more ice. Never chip or scrape the ice off.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Open and clear all vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by rugs, furniture, or curtains. Don't close vents to "redirect" air.
- **Check the breaker and thermostat.** Confirm the AC breaker is on and the thermostat is calling for cool (and, if it takes batteries, that they're fresh).
- **Check the condensate drain.** When the ice melts it can overflow a clogged drain pan. Look for standing water and make sure the drain line or condensate pump is clear.
- **Wait for a full thaw** (1–24 hours) before restarting cooling. Restarting on an iced coil just refreezes it.

If it freezes again within a day or two of a clean filter and open vents, stop running it and call a pro — that points to refrigerant or a mechanical fault you can't safely fix.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech works the airflow-vs-refrigerant fork in a defined order. Use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirms the thaw** and inspects the filter, coil, and blower wheel for dirt.
2. **Measures airflow / static pressure** to catch duct restrictions, undersized returns, or a weak blower.
3. **Checks blower motor and capacitor** — amp draw and capacitance against the rating on the label.
4. **Puts gauges on the system** to read suction/head pressures and calculate superheat and subcooling. This is where low charge shows up.
5. **Leak-searches** if the charge is low — electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test. A recharge without finding the leak is a temporary fix, and a reputable tech will say so.
6. **Inspects the metering device** (TXV or piston) on Goodman coils for correct operation.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without checking airflow first or searching for a leak — that's treating the symptom, not the cause.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Replace filter, open all vents, thaw | Verify airflow, static pressure test |
| Freezes again days after new filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Ice on outdoor copper line, warm air | Low charge or dirty coil | Thaw, replace filter | Gauge readings, subcooling/superheat |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Melted ice + clogged drain | Clear drain, check condensate pump | Flush drain, confirm pan slope |
| Fan runs but little air moves | Weak blower or bad capacitor | Check breaker; thaw | Test blower amps and capacitor |
| Coil freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoor | Don't run cooling in cold temps | Confirm charge is otherwise normal |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your area, system size, and whether it's a heat pump affect price.

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$400 depending on access.
- **Blower run capacitor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $450–$900 (single-speed) to $700–$1,200 (variable-speed/ECM).
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $200–$600+ depending on amount and refrigerant type.
- **Leak repair (accessible joint):** $200–$700; a leaking evaporator coil replacement runs **$1,000–$2,000+**.
- **TXV replacement:** $400–$800.
- **Condensate drain clear / new pump:** $75–$250.

If the coil itself is leaking and your system is older or uses R-22, ask whether a full replacement is more economical than a coil swap.

## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a specific "frozen evaporator coil" fault code — this is a **physical symptom**, not a board-generated error. You'll notice it as ice or frost on the copper lines or on the indoor coil (usually inside the air handler or above the furnace), weak or no cold air at the vents, and sometimes water pooling near the indoor unit as the ice melts.

A frozen coil happens when the evaporator gets **too cold and drops below freezing**, causing moisture in the air to freeze on it instead of draining away. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil and blocks airflow, which makes the problem snowball — the system runs harder and cools less.

The two root categories are almost always **not enough airflow across the coil** or **not enough refrigerant (low charge)**. Both drop coil temperature below freezing.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil overcool and freeze.
2. **Blocked return/supply vents or closed dampers** — reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust build a film that insulates the coil and chokes airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — a low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature, causing freeze-up. Common on older systems.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / wheel** — if the fan can't move enough air, the coil freezes even with a clean filter.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or failing thermostat / low fan speed setting** — occasionally the blower runs too slow for the coil.
8. **Dirty or clogged condensate drain** backing up isn't a freeze cause directly, but the resulting water often gets blamed on the ice melt.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are homeowner-safe. Do not open refrigerant lines, do not add refrigerant, and do not scrape ice off the coil.

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This thaws the coil safely. Wait until all ice is gone (1–3 hours) before doing anything else.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A clogged filter is the #1 cause. Match the size printed on the old filter frame.
- **Walk the house and open all supply vents and return grilles.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed doors aren't starving airflow.
- **Check the thermostat** — set to COOL, temperature a few degrees below room temp, and confirm the fan setting. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Look at the outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass, and debris from the fins so the system can reject heat properly.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan** for standing water once thawed; clear the line with a wet/dry vac if it's backed up.
- **Confirm the breaker** for both indoor and outdoor units is on.

If the coil freezes again within a day or two of a clean filter and clear vents, stop running it and call a pro — repeated freezing usually points to refrigerant or blower problems.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing anything — a frozen coil gives false readings.
2. **Measure airflow** — check filter, blower speed/amp draw, and inspect the evaporator coil for dirt.
3. **Attach gauges and check refrigerant charge** using superheat/subcooling — this reveals a low charge from a leak (R-410A or R-32 depending on your model).
4. **Perform a leak search** if the charge is low — electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test.
5. **Check the blower motor** amp draw and wheel condition if airflow is the suspect.
6. **Inspect the metering device (TXV or piston)** — a stuck TXV can starve the coil and cause freezing.

Sanity check: if a tech says "you just need refrigerant" without doing a leak search, push back. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up" — a low charge means there's a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, check blower speed |
| Refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, call pro | Leak search + repair + recharge |
| Ice + very little air even with new filter | Failing blower or dirty coil | Thaw, inspect filter | Test blower motor, clean coil |
| Freezing when it's cool outside | Running AC below ~60°F | Switch to fan only | Advise on low-ambient limits |
| Water on floor near air handler | Melted ice / clogged drain | Vac condensate line clear | Clear drain, confirm no leak |
| Coil freezes, high indoor humidity | Stuck TXV / metering device | Turn off, call pro | Replace/adjust metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Leak repair + recharge (R-410A):** $500–$1,200 depending on leak location and charge amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900
- **Full evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500 (higher on R-32 or newer sealed coils)

If your system is older, low on refrigerant with a leaking coil, and out of warranty, weigh a coil replacement against replacing the whole system — get a written estimate either way.

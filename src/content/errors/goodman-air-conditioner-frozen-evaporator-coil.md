## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a fault code on most Goodman air conditioners. It is a condition you can see: frost or ice forms on the indoor coil or on the copper suction line at the outdoor unit.

The coil freezes when its surface drops below 32°F. That happens for one of two reasons:

- **Not enough warm air crosses the coil.** This is an airflow problem.
- **The refrigerant inside runs too cold.** This is usually low charge from a leak or a refrigerant restriction.

Typical signs include:

- Weak or warm airflow from the vents
- An AC that runs constantly
- Water around the air handler or furnace as ice melts

**Reset behavior:**

- Standard Goodman condensers have no freeze-protection lockout, so they keep running and the ice keeps growing.
- Some models with a low-pressure switch, and communicating or inverter systems, may stop the compressor and log a fault. Whether it auto-resets depends on the model.

Severity is **pro**. You can thaw the coil and fix airflow basics yourself, but repeat freezing almost always needs a technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter.** This is the most common cause of restricted airflow and coil icing.
2. **Closed or blocked supply registers and return grilles.** Furniture, rugs or many closed vents reduce airflow across the coil.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil.** Dust and biological growth on the coil fins block airflow even when the filter is clean.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. Goodman evaporator coils and line sets can develop leaks, and these require a licensed tech.
5. **Blower problems.** A failing blower motor, a weak blower capacitor, a dirty blower wheel, or an incorrect fan-speed setting for cooling all cut airflow.
6. **Refrigerant metering restriction.** A sticking TXV or a clogged piston or filter-drier starves the coil.
7. **Running in cool weather or with the thermostat set very low.** Operating below about 60°F outdoors, or long run times with low indoor heat load, can ice a coil. This is especially true when another issue is already present.
8. **Restricted or undersized ductwork.** Collapsed flex duct or a return that is too small can cause chronic low airflow.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Thaw the coil first.**
   - Set the thermostat to **OFF** for cooling and the fan to **ON**.
   - Let it run until the ice is fully gone and the suction line at the outdoor unit is dry.
   - Never chip or scrape the ice.
2. **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it is gray, clogged or more than about 1 to 3 months old. Make sure the size matches and the airflow arrow points toward the blower.
3. **Open the registers and clear the returns.** Make sure most supply vents are open and nothing blocks the return grilles.
4. **Check the thermostat.**
   - Confirm it is set to COOL at a reasonable setpoint, around 72–78°F, not the lowest setting.
   - Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
5. **Watch the condensate drain.** As the ice melts, make sure the drain line and pan can handle the water.
   - Clear the drain line opening or vent tee if it is visibly clogged.
   - If your system has a float or safety switch that shut the unit off, the water level will need to drop before it restarts.
6. **Check the breaker and switches.** Reset a tripped AC or air handler breaker **once**. If it trips again, leave it off and call a technician.
7. **Seat the panels.** Make sure the air handler or furnace access panels are firmly in place, since missing panels disrupt airflow.
8. **Restart and observe.** After a full thaw, turn cooling back on and check the vents in 15–30 minutes. If you see frost on the suction line or the coil again within a day, turn cooling off and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Thaw first.** Pressures and temperatures can't be read correctly on an iced coil, so a good tech thaws it or asks you to.
- **Measure airflow.** The tech checks total external static pressure and the temperature drop across the coil, typically about 16–22°F. They also confirm the blower speed tap or ECM setting matches the cooling tonnage.
- **Inspect the blower.** This includes checking motor amps, testing the run capacitor (on PSC motors), and checking the blower wheel for dirt buildup.
- **Inspect the coil.** The tech looks for a dirty face, matted fins and oil spots that could indicate a leak.
- **Check the refrigerant charge.** They attach gauges and measure superheat (for piston systems) or subcooling (for TXV systems), following Goodman's charging chart on the unit.
- **Find the leak if the charge is low.** Methods include an electronic detector, bubbles, UV dye or a nitrogen pressure test. A proper repair fixes the leak. It should not just add refrigerant.
- **Test for restrictions.** Temperature drop across the filter-drier and TXV bulb mounting and operation are checked.

**Red flag:** a quote to simply "top off" refrigerant every season without a leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, filter visibly clogged | Restricted airflow | Thaw, replace filter, restart | Only needed if it refreezes |
| Weak airflow, many vents closed | Blocked registers or returns | Open vents, clear returns | Duct evaluation if airflow stays weak |
| Coil refreezes with clean filter | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw; keep filters current | Coil cleaning |
| Ice on suction line, long run times, hissing or oily spots | Refrigerant leak or low charge | Turn cooling off, fan on; call a pro | Leak search, repair, evacuate, weigh in charge |
| Little or no air from vents, humming at the air handler | Blower motor or capacitor failure | Turn system off; check breaker once | Test and replace capacitor or motor; clean blower wheel |
| Freezes after a new install or repair | Wrong blower speed or wrong charge | Note when it started; call the installer | Set fan speed, verify charge against Goodman's chart |
| Frost forms partway through the coil | TXV or metering restriction | Turn cooling off | Diagnose and replace TXV, piston or filter-drier |
| Water on the floor during thaw | Drain overwhelmed or clogged | Clear visible drain line, mop, monitor | Clear drain, check pan and float switch |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Low load or low ambient operation | Raise setpoint; avoid cooling below about 60°F outside | Check charge and airflow; consider low-ambient controls |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges for parts and labor. They vary by region and by whether parts are under warranty.

- **Air filter (DIY):** $5–$40
- **Service call and diagnosis:** $75–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $100–$400
- **Blower run capacitor:** $120–$300
- **Blower motor (PSC):** $350–$700
- **Blower motor (ECM or variable speed):** $600–$1,500
- **Blower speed adjustment:** $75–$200, often included in the diagnosis fee
- **Leak search:** $200–$500
- **Leak repair (braze or line set section):** $200–$1,000+
- **Refrigerant recharge (R-410A or R-454B):** $200–$800+, depending on pounds and current pricing
- **TXV replacement:** $400–$1,200, including recover, evacuate and recharge
- **Filter-drier replacement:** $200–$500
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800. Goodman's parts warranty often covers the coil if the unit is registered, but labor and refrigerant are still billed.
- **Duct repair or return upgrade:** $300–$2,500+

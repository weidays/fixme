## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a Goodman fault code — your control board won't flash a number for it. It's a physical condition: the indoor coil (the A-coil or slab inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) has dropped below freezing and condensation has turned to frost or a solid block of ice.

A coil freezes for one of two root reasons: **not enough warm air is moving across the coil**, or **there isn't enough refrigerant to carry heat away properly**. Either way, the coil surface gets cold enough to ice over, cooling collapses, and water often overflows the drain pan as the ice melts.

This is rated **pro** because the two most damaging causes — low refrigerant and compressor slugging — require EPA-certified refrigerant handling and can total your compressor if you keep running the system.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. A choked filter starves the coil of airflow, and the coil freezes.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or crushed ductwork reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and restrict air passage through the fins.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — a slow refrigerant leak drops coil temperature and pressure until it ices. Common on older Goodman R-410A systems.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the fan can't move enough air. Some Goodman air handlers use an ECM motor that can under-perform without throwing a code.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — standing water can back up onto the coil and freeze in extreme cases.
8. **Restricted metering device (TXV or piston)** — a stuck expansion valve starves the coil; a pro diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system **turned off at the thermostat** — never chip at ice.

- **Thaw it first.** Set the thermostat to **OFF** and the fan switch to **ON**. Let the fan run until all ice is melted (1–24 hours). Place towels near the air handler for meltwater.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray or you can't see light through it, swap it. Note the size printed on the frame.
- **Open your vents.** Walk the house and open all supply registers; make sure return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint (not 60°F) and, if battery-powered, that the batteries are fresh.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor unit or condenser tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Clear the condensate drain.** Use a wet/dry vac on the exterior drain line outlet to pull out clogs, and drop a drain tablet in the pan.

If the coil re-freezes within a day or two after you've done all of the above, the cause is almost certainly refrigerant, coil, or blower related — call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Goodman tech should:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — readings are meaningless on ice.
2. **Measure static pressure and airflow** across the coil and check the blower motor amp draw and capacitor.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup and the metering device (TXV/piston) for restriction.
4. **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to judge the refrigerant charge — this is the key step. Low subcooling or high superheat points to undercharge/leak.
5. **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low. Reputable techs find and fix the leak; they don't just "top off" the refrigerant.
6. **Verify the outdoor temperature** is warm enough to run a valid cooling test.

Sanity check: if a tech quotes a refrigerant refill without ever mentioning finding the leak, get a second opinion. Adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary fix that will freeze again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coil iced, filter gray | Clogged filter | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | None if it stays fixed |
| Weak airflow at vents, ice returns | Closed vents / duct restriction | Open all registers, clear returns | Inspect ductwork, static pressure |
| Ice + warm air, blower runs weak | Failing blower / bad capacitor | Thaw and observe fan speed | Test/replace ECM motor or capacitor |
| Coil re-freezes days after filter change | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn system off; call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water on floor near furnace | Condensate drain clog + thaw runoff | Vac drain line, add tablets | Clear drain, check pan/float switch |
| Ice only on cool nights | Running AC too cold outside | Don't run cooling below ~60°F | Confirm no other fault |
| Coil dirty, black fins | Dirty evaporator coil | Replace filter, schedule cleaning | Chemical coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your price varies by region, system size, and refrigerant type.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + restart (DIY):** $0
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250 (or DIY)
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **ECM/blower motor replacement:** $450–$900
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$500
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$500
- **Refrigerant recharge (R-410A):** $200–$700 depending on amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500 (parts, labor, refrigerant)
- **Compressor damage from slugging:** $1,500–$3,000+ — the reason you should never run a frozen system

The cheapest outcome is a $15 filter caught early. The most expensive is a wrecked compressor from running the unit while iced — so shut it down at the first sign of frost.

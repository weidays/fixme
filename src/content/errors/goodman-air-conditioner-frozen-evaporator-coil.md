## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a dedicated "frozen coil" fault code. Instead, this is a **symptom-based diagnosis**: ice builds up on the indoor evaporator coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet), and sometimes creeps out onto the copper refrigerant line running to the outdoor unit.

A coil freezes when the refrigerant inside it gets too cold — below 32°F — and moisture in the passing air freezes onto the metal fins. This happens for one of two reasons: **not enough warm air is moving across the coil**, or **the refrigerant charge or pressure is wrong**. Once ice forms, it blocks airflow further, so the problem snowballs quickly.

You'll usually notice weak or no cold air, higher humidity indoors, water pooling near the furnace when it thaws, or visible frost on the copper line at the outdoor unit. Because a diagnosis frequently involves refrigerant, this is rated **pro** — the airflow checks are DIY, but a leak or charge problem is not.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of airflow and drops its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — Too many closed registers, blocked returns, or crushed flex duct restrict airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — A slow leak lowers pressure in the coil, dropping its temperature until it ices over. Requires a technician to locate and repair.
4. **Failing or dirty blower motor / wheel** — A weak blower or a wheel caked with dust can't push enough air, even with a clean filter.
5. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Grime on the coil fins insulates them and restricts airflow, encouraging ice.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — Operating cooling when outdoor temps are below roughly 62°F can cause freezing on many systems.
7. **Stuck TXV / metering device or restricted line** — A metering valve that won't modulate properly causes localized freezing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — several fix the problem outright and cost nothing:

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan to ON.** At the thermostat, switch the system from Cool to Off, then set the fan from Auto to On. This runs the blower to thaw the ice safely. Wait at least a few hours (up to 24 for heavy ice) before doing anything else.
- **Replace the air filter.** Pull it out and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it with the correct size. This alone resolves a large share of frozen-coil calls.
- **Open your vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes. Aim to leave the vast majority of registers open.
- **Check the condensate drain.** As the ice melts, a lot of water is produced. Confirm the drain line and pan aren't overflowing. Clear a clogged drain with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination, and add drain tablets to the pan.
- **Check the thermostat and batteries.** Confirm it's set to Cool at a reasonable temperature (not, say, 60°F). Replace batteries if the display is dim.
- **Check the breaker.** If the outdoor unit or air handler tripped, a frozen coil could be a side effect — but don't repeatedly reset a breaker that keeps tripping. Call a pro.

**Do not** try to chip or scrape ice off the coil, and never handle refrigerant lines or add refrigerant yourself. After thawing, run cooling normally and watch whether it freezes again — repeat freezing means you need a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works airflow-first, then refrigerant:

1. **Confirms the coil is fully thawed** — gauges read wrong on an iced system.
2. **Measures airflow / static pressure** across the coil and checks the blower speed, wheel cleanliness, and duct restrictions.
3. **Inspects the coil and filter** for dirt buildup that a homeowner might miss.
4. **Connects refrigerant gauges** and checks superheat and subcooling to determine whether the charge is low, correct, or overcharged.
5. **Leak searches** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low — because simply "topping off" without finding the leak is a temporary fix.
6. **Tests the metering device (TXV)** and inspects for line restrictions if airflow and charge both check out.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without any airflow check or leak search, that's a red flag worth a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents, thaw with fan ON | Measure static pressure, clean coil |
| Frost on outdoor copper line | Low refrigerant | Thaw system, note if it recurs | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Coil freezes again after thawing | Failing blower or hidden leak | Verify filter is clean; note recurrence | Test blower motor, gauge refrigerant |
| Water pooling near furnace | Melting ice + drain issue | Clear condensate drain, use wet/dry vac | Clear/replace drain, verify float switch |
| Freezes only on cool days | Running AC in low temps | Avoid cooling below ~62°F outdoors | Confirm no other fault present |
| Warm air, coil dirty and iced | Clogged evaporator coil | Thaw, replace filter | Deep-clean or replace coil |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges — your price varies by region, system size, and access:

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thawing the coil:** $0 (DIY, just time)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250 professionally, or ~$15 DIY with tablets and a wet/dry vac
- **Blower motor cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (ECM) replacement:** $450–$900
- **Evaporator coil deep cleaning:** $200–$500
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,200 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500+ if the coil itself is damaged or leaking

Note: older Goodman systems on R-22 refrigerant cost significantly more to recharge, since R-22 is phased out and expensive — a leak repair may push you toward a system replacement conversation.

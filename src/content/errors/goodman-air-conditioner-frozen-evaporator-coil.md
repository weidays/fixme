## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is not a digital fault code — it is a physical condition. The indoor coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) gets so cold that moisture in the air freezes into ice on the fins and refrigerant lines. Goodman systems don't flash a specific "frozen coil" code; you diagnose it by what you see and hear.

The evaporator coil is designed to run cold, but airflow across it carries that cold away and keeps it above freezing. When either **airflow drops** or **refrigerant pressure drops**, the coil temperature falls below 32°F and condensation turns to ice. Once ice forms, it blocks airflow even more, so the problem snowballs fast.

Common warning signs: weak or warm air from the vents, ice visible on the copper lines near the outdoor unit, water pooling around the indoor unit after the ice melts, and a hissing or bubbling sound.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil and it freezes.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the fins insulate the coil and cut airflow across it.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — lower pressure means a colder coil. This is the most common *non-airflow* cause and requires a technician.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor** — a weak or dying indoor fan (or a bad capacitor/ECM module) doesn't move enough air.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below roughly 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil on many systems.
7. **Thermostat set too low** for extended periods, or a stuck contactor keeping the compressor running non-stop.
8. **Blocked condensate drain backing up** — less common as a freeze cause, but a full drain pan can be a symptom you'll notice at the same time.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner:

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to OFF for cooling, then set the fan to ON (not AUTO). This runs the blower to thaw the coil faster without running the compressor. Allow 1–3 hours.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A clogged filter is the number-one cause. Swap in a clean pleated filter (MERV 8–11 is plenty for most Goodman systems).
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and not blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes. Closing too many vents is a real cause.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to a reasonable temperature (68–72°F), and replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breaker.** If you've cycled the system, make sure the indoor and outdoor breakers are on.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan and a clogged drain line. A wet/dry vac on the exterior drain outlet can clear a clog. Drain tablets help prevent regrowth.

Do **not** attempt to add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or scrape ice off the coil. Let it melt naturally.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms it's actually frozen (not just weak cooling — see our *Running But Not Cooling* guide) and then works through causes systematically. Use this to sanity-check a quote:

- **Verifies the coil is thawed** before testing — you can't read pressures accurately on an iced system.
- **Measures airflow** across the coil and checks blower operation, capacitor, and ECM module.
- **Inspects the filter and coil** for restriction and cleanliness.
- **Connects gauges** to read suction/head pressure and measures **superheat and subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct.
- **Leak-searches** with electronic detector, UV dye, or bubble solution if the charge is low — a proper repair finds and fixes the leak, not just "tops off" the refrigerant.
- **Checks the metering device** (TXV or piston) for restriction.

Be wary of any tech who adds refrigerant without doing a leak search. Adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary fix that fails again — and repeated top-offs are more expensive than one leak repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow + ice on indoor coil | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents, thaw with fan ON | Verify airflow, clean blower |
| Ice on outdoor copper line set | Low refrigerant or airflow | Thaw system, check filter | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Coil refreezes after clean filter | Low charge or dirty coil | Thaw again, then call pro | Coil cleaning, charge check, leak repair |
| Water around indoor unit | Melted ice / clogged drain | Vac the drain line, add tablets | Clear drain, inspect pan |
| Blower runs weak or noisy | Failing motor / capacitor | None — call pro | Test/replace blower or ECM |
| Freezing in cool weather (<60°F) | Running AC too cold outside | Turn cooling off | Advise on operating limits |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + restart (DIY):** $0
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **ECM blower motor/module replacement:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100–$350
- **Refrigerant recharge (R-410A):** $200–$700 depending on amount
- **Refrigerant leak repair (coil or line):** $600–$1,500+
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800+ depending on model

If your Goodman system is out of warranty and the evaporator coil itself is leaking, get a quote for coil replacement versus system replacement — on older units the math sometimes favors replacement. Many Goodman coils carry a parts warranty if registered; confirm your coverage before paying for a new coil.

## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners don't display a specific "frozen evaporator coil" fault code — this is a physical condition you diagnose by symptom, not a board readout. It means ice has formed on the indoor evaporator coil (inside your air handler or above your furnace), or on the copper suction line running back to the outdoor unit.

Your evaporator coil is supposed to run cold — but above freezing. When something drops the coil temperature below 32°F, humidity condensing on it turns to ice instead of draining away. That ice blocks airflow, which makes the coil even colder, which makes more ice. Left running, the system pushes little or no cold air, and the refrigerant can flood back to the outdoor compressor as liquid — a leading cause of compressor failure.

This is filed as **pro severity** because the two most common root causes (low refrigerant and a failing blower) require a licensed technician, and running the system frozen risks a four-figure compressor repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Restricted airflow from a dirty filter** — the single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the coil of the warm return air it needs to stay above freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — low pressure lowers the coil's boiling point below 32°F. This is common on older or improperly charged systems and always indicates a leak that must be found and repaired.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and reduce airflow, dropping surface temperature.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / wheel** — a slowing blower or a wheel packed with dust moves too little air across the coil.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — reducing airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below roughly 60°F outdoors can push coil temperature under freezing on many systems.
7. **Stuck or failing metering device (TXV or piston)** — over-feeds or starves the coil; less common but real on aging units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do them **after** turning the thermostat to OFF for cooling so the coil can thaw.

- **Set the fan to ON:** With cooling OFF, switch the thermostat fan to ON. This melts the ice fastest. Give it 1–3 hours (up to 24 for heavy ice).
- **Change the air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clean filter fixes a surprising number of freeze-ups.
- **Check the thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and not to an unrealistically low temperature that makes the unit run nonstop.
- **Check the breaker:** Make sure both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor unit have power. A tripped indoor breaker stops the blower while the outdoor unit tries to run.
- **Open your vents:** Walk the house and open all supply registers and confirm return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or filters.
- **Check the condensate drain:** A frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it thaws. Make sure the drain pan isn't overflowing and the condensate line is clear. A shop vac on the outdoor drain outlet clears most clogs.
- **Let it fully dry, then test:** Only after the coil is completely thawed and dry, switch cooling back on and watch for normal cold airflow.

If it refreezes within a day or two, stop running it and call a technician — you likely have a refrigerant leak or airflow fault that DIY won't fix.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the root cause rather than just thawing it and leaving:

- **Verifies airflow** — checks filter, coil cleanliness, blower speed setting, and measures temperature split (return vs. supply air).
- **Connects gauges** — reads suction and head pressures against the Goodman charging chart for your model and refrigerant (R-410A or R-32). Low suction pressure with proper airflow points to undercharge/leak.
- **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to locate the leak. Refilling without fixing the leak is a temporary and non-compliant fix.
- **Inspects the blower** — tests motor amperage, checks the wheel for dust buildup, and confirms the correct blower tap/speed for the coil.
- **Checks the metering device** — evaluates TXV or piston operation if pressures are abnormal despite good airflow and charge.
- **Superheat/subcooling readings** — the definitive measurements that tell whether the system is properly charged.

Sanity check: if a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or airflow check, ask questions.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak warm airflow | Dirty filter / airflow restriction | Set fan ON, replace filter, open vents | Measure airflow, clean coil, set blower speed |
| Ice on outdoor copper line at compressor | Low refrigerant charge | Turn off, let thaw | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Coil refreezes within a day of thawing | Refrigerant leak or blower fault | Stop running system | Diagnose leak or blower, repair |
| Water overflowing pan after thaw | Melting ice + clogged drain | Clear drain with shop vac | Clear line, flush, add safety switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoor | Don't run cooling in cool temps | Confirm no other airflow/charge issue |
| Little airflow, dusty blower | Failing/dirty blower motor or wheel | Replace filter, then call | Clean/replace motor, capacitor, or wheel |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual price varies by region, model, and refrigerant type (R-32 systems can carry different service costs than R-410A).

- **DIY thaw + filter change:** $0–$40
- **Condensate drain clearing (pro):** $75–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning (pro):** $150–$400
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement (if damaged by running frozen):** $1,500–$3,000+

Fixing the airflow or leak cause early is far cheaper than replacing a compressor that failed from running iced up.

## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a specific fault code for a frozen evaporator coil — there is no blinking pattern or number for it. Instead, you notice the symptoms: warm or weak air from the vents, ice on the copper lines or on the indoor coil cabinet, water pooling around the air handler or furnace, and a system that runs constantly without cooling.

The evaporator coil is the indoor coil where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. It normally runs cold but above freezing. When it drops below 32°F, condensation on the coil turns to frost, then a solid block of ice. Ice insulates the coil, so cooling gets worse — a self-feeding problem. Left alone, it can starve the compressor of proper refrigerant flow and lead to a costly failure.

Two things cause a coil to get that cold: **too little airflow across the coil**, or **too little refrigerant in the system**. Everything below traces back to one of those two.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil overcool and freeze.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — closed registers, crushed flex duct, or a shut damper starve the coil of air.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins block airflow and heat transfer.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharged systems run the coil too cold. This is a repair, not a "top-off," and requires a technician.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the blower can't move enough air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — using cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain triggering a float switch** cycling behavior, or a stuck contactor/thermostat calling for cool continuously.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do them **after** turning cooling OFF and letting any ice melt fully.

- **Turn cooling off, fan to ON.** Set the thermostat mode to OFF (or Fan Only) and the fan to ON. This runs the blower without cooling, thawing the coil faster. Wait until all ice is gone — 1 to 3 hours typically.
- **Check the air filter.** Pull it. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of freeze-ups.
- **Open and clear vents.** Walk the house and confirm supply registers are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Confirm return grilles aren't blocked.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's set to a reasonable temperature and that "Fan ON" wasn't accidentally the only thing running, or that it isn't stuck calling for cool. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor or outdoor unit tripped, that can leave one running without the other. Reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan or a full float switch. Vacuum the drain line outlet with a wet/dry vac and add a cleaning tablet to keep it clear.

If the coil freezes again within a day of a clean filter and open vents, the cause is deeper — refrigerant, coil, or blower. Call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Goodman tech will confirm the coil is fully thawed, then:

- **Measure airflow and temperature split** across the coil (return vs. supply, typically looking for ~14–22°F drop) to separate airflow problems from refrigerant problems.
- **Inspect the coil, filter, and blower** for dirt and check blower speed and the run capacitor.
- **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to determine if the charge is correct. Low subcooling with proper airflow points to a leak.
- **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or bubble test) if the system is undercharged — because simply adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary fix and, on modern refrigerants, expensive to keep doing.
- **Check the metering device (TXV or piston)** and the condensate/float switch wiring.

A good quote should name the actual cause — not just "add refrigerant." If they propose recharging, ask where the leak is and whether they found it.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, weak airflow | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents, thaw coil | Verify airflow, clean coil |
| Coil re-freezes with clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, thaw, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Warm air + long run times, no ice yet | Dirty coil or weak blower | Change filter, check vents | Clean coil, test blower/capacitor |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Thawed ice or clogged drain | Vacuum drain, add tablet | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run cooling in cold temps | Confirm no other fault |
| Blower runs but very little air moves | Failing blower motor/capacitor | Nothing safe — call pro | Replace capacitor or motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Regional pricing varies.

- **Air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors on the high end)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000+ (higher if it's an R-410A/R-454B match with the condenser)

If your system is low on refrigerant, expect the leak repair — not the "top-off" — to be the real cost. Recharging a leaking system is temporary and, under EPA rules, not a proper fix.

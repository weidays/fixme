## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not usually flash a specific "frozen coil" error code — instead you notice weak or no cooling, ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, and water pooling when it thaws. A frozen evaporator coil means the indoor coil got cold enough for condensation on it to freeze into ice, blocking airflow and killing cooling capacity.

Two things cause ice: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant** (which drops coil temperature below freezing). Either way, once ice forms it snowballs — the coil gets colder, more ice forms, and cooling stops entirely.

This is rated **pro** because the most common non-DIY cause is a refrigerant leak, which by law requires an EPA-certified technician to diagnose and repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature until condensation freezes.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow, same effect as a dirty filter.
3. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — reduces the warm-air volume across the coil.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — lowers coil pressure and temperature. Requires a certified tech; you cannot add refrigerant yourself.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / capacitor** — not enough air being pushed across the coil.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — cooling below roughly 62°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Failed or stuck TXV / metering device** — overfeeds or restricts refrigerant, dropping coil temperature.
8. **Restricted or clogged condensate drain** causing water backup around a chronically cold coil (less common as a root cause).

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to "Off" for cooling but the fan to "On" to thaw the ice. Never run the compressor with a frozen coil.
- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many frozen-coil cases.
- **Check thermostat batteries and settings.** Confirm it's calling for a reasonable temperature, not set unrealistically low.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm the indoor (air handler/furnace) breaker is on — a dead blower means no airflow and guaranteed freezing.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and not blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed doors.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** After thawing, look for standing water; a clogged drain can overflow. Drain tablets can help keep it clear.
- **Let it fully thaw** (often several hours) before restarting cooling. Restarting on ice just refreezes.

If a fresh filter and full thaw fix it and it stays fixed, you're done. If it refreezes within a day or two, stop and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will confirm the coil is fully thawed, then:

- **Measure airflow and blower operation** — check the blower motor, capacitor, and static pressure across the coil.
- **Inspect and clean the evaporator coil** if it's dirty.
- **Attach gauges and check refrigerant pressures** and superheat/subcooling against Goodman's spec for your model — this is how they separate an airflow problem from a refrigerant problem.
- **Leak-search** with electronic detector or dye if refrigerant is low, since simply "topping off" a leaking system is illegal and temporary.
- **Test the TXV / metering device** for proper operation.

You can sanity-check a quote: a legitimate diagnosis includes both an **airflow check and a refrigerant-pressure check**. Anyone who adds refrigerant without finding the leak is not fixing the problem.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak cooling | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, thaw, run fan | Clean coil, check blower/static pressure |
| Refreezes days after new filter | Refrigerant leak | Thaw, then stop and call pro | Leak search, repair, EPA-compliant recharge |
| Ice on copper line at outdoor unit | Low refrigerant or restriction | Turn off, thaw | Gauge pressures, test TXV |
| Water pooling around indoor unit | Thawing ice / clogged drain | Clear drain, use tablets | Flush drain, confirm no coil freeze |
| Little/no airflow from vents | Blower motor / capacitor failure | Check indoor breaker | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Running AC when too cold out | Don't run cooling below ~62°F outside | Advise on low-ambient limits |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors cost more)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $100–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500 (coil part may be under Goodman warranty; labor and refrigerant usually are not)

If your system uses older R-22 refrigerant, recharge costs can run significantly higher, and a leaking coil is often worth replacing rather than repairing.

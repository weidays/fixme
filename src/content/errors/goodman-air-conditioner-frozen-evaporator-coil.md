## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a fault code that flashes on a board—Goodman air conditioners don't log it. It's a physical condition you diagnose by sight: a layer of frost or solid ice on the indoor coil (evaporator) and often on the copper refrigerant lines near the air handler or furnace.

The evaporator coil is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. That coil normally runs cold but stays above freezing because warm airflow keeps passing over it. When something drops the coil temperature below 32°F—too little airflow, too little refrigerant, or both—condensation on the coil freezes instead of draining away. Ice then insulates the coil, cooling collapses, and you get warm air from the vents even though the system is running hard.

The most damaging part is what happens to the outdoor unit: with the coil iced over, liquid refrigerant that should have boiled off inside can flood back to the compressor. That "slugging" can wreck a compressor, which is why this is rated **pro severity**—thaw it and find the cause before you run it again.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter.** The single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of airflow, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents.** Too many closed registers or blocked returns reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil.** Dust and biofilm on the coil fins act like insulation and restrict airflow through the coil itself.
4. **Low refrigerant charge (leak).** A leak lowers system pressure, which lowers coil temperature—classic freeze-up. Requires a licensed tech; refrigerant is not DIY.
5. **Failing blower motor or wrong fan speed.** A weak or miswired blower moves too little air across the coil.
6. **Clogged condensate drain or dirty blower wheel.** Restricts airflow or backs up water that then freezes.
7. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** (below ~60°F). Operating outside design conditions can freeze a coil.
8. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/orifice).** Meters refrigerant incorrectly, chilling the coil—diagnosed and replaced by a technician.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with cooling turned **OFF** and the thermostat **FAN set to ON** to speed thawing and protect the compressor.

- **Turn off cooling, run the fan.** Set the thermostat to OFF (or FAN ON, cool off) so the blower thaws the coil without the compressor running.
- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open your vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or filters stacked on top.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to cool with a reasonable setpoint (not way below room temp) and swap batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Look at the breaker.** If the indoor/outdoor unit tripped, reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** If the pan is full or water is pooling, the drain may be clogged. You can clear a visible clog or use a wet/dry vac on the drain line outlet.
- **Wait for full thaw before restarting.** Never run cooling while any ice remains. Let it thaw completely (1–3 hours, sometimes more), then restart and watch to see if it refreezes.

If it refreezes after a clean filter and open vents, you likely have a refrigerant or mechanical issue—call a technician. Do not open the sealed refrigerant system yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the root cause rather than just recharging refrigerant. Expect them to:

- **Thaw and inspect the coil** for dirt, damage, and ice pattern (uniform frost suggests airflow; frost starting at one point suggests a metering/charge issue).
- **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil and check the blower speed tap and wheel cleanliness.
- **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to judge refrigerant charge accurately—not just "add a pound."
- **Leak-search** with electronic detectors or dye if the charge is low; a proper repair means fixing the leak, not topping off yearly.
- **Check the metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** for correct operation.
- **Inspect the condensate system** and drain for restriction.

If a tech's first move is to add refrigerant without checking airflow or searching for a leak, be skeptical—that treats the symptom, not the cause.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil / lines, warm air from vents | Dirty filter or low airflow | Turn off cooling, run fan, replace filter | Measure static pressure, clean coil/blower |
| Coil refreezes after new filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running cool; call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Water pooling near air handler | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, vac the line | Clear drain, flush pan, check trap |
| Weak airflow at every register | Failing blower or wrong speed | Confirm vents open | Test blower motor, correct fan speed |
| Freeze-up only on cool nights | Marginal charge or low outdoor temp | Avoid running below ~60°F | Verify charge, check TXV operation |
| Frost starting at one spot on coil | Metering device / charge issue | None safe | Diagnose and replace TXV or orifice |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Your Goodman model and local rates will shift these.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$30
- **Coil thaw only (DIY, time + electricity):** $0
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors run higher)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. newer refrigerants)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$750
- **Full evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500+ if the coil itself is leaking

A frozen coil caused by a dirty filter is a $20 fix. A frozen coil caused by a refrigerant leak that already damaged the compressor can run into the thousands—which is exactly why you shut cooling off the moment you see ice.

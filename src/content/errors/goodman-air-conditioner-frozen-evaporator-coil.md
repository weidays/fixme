## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a fault code Goodman displays on a board — it is a physical condition you'll notice as weak cooling, ice on the copper lines, or water pooling near the indoor unit. The evaporator coil (the indoor A-coil or cased coil above your furnace or air handler) gets so cold that moisture in the air freezes on it instead of draining away.

Once ice forms, it insulates the coil and chokes airflow, so cooling drops off fast and the problem snowballs. Nearly every cause traces back to one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant pressure** to keep the coil above freezing. Because refrigerant work is involved in many repairs, this is rated a **pro** job — but several safe checks and the thaw itself are things you can do yourself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops the coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too few open registers starves the coil of return air.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil or blower wheel** — years of dust build a film that acts like insulation and reduces airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — a Goodman system low on charge runs colder coil temps and ices up; this is a pro repair.
5. **Failed or weak blower motor / wrong fan speed** — reduced CFM across the coil.
6. **Stuck or failing TXV or metering device** — meters too much refrigerant, over-cooling the coil (systems with a thermostatic expansion valve).
7. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — operating below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
8. **Clogged condensate drain** — standing water can back up onto the coil and refreeze in some cased-coil setups.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with cooling turned off. If the coil is iced, this is also how you thaw it.

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** At the thermostat switch cooling off but leave the fan running. Warm indoor air will thaw the coil over 1–3 hours. Do not chip or scrape ice.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or more than 60–90 days old, swap it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Open all supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking airflow. You want most registers open.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL and a reasonable setpoint; avoid setting it far below room temp, which forces long freezing runtimes.
- **Look for water and clear it.** Check the condensate pan and drain line. If it's full or slimy, that's a clue; a wet-dry vac on the drain outlet can clear a simple clog. Add pan tablets to prevent regrowth.
- **Check the breaker.** After a thaw, if nothing runs, verify the AC and air-handler breakers are on.
- **Feel the outdoor unit lines.** After thawing and restarting, the large insulated line should be cold and slightly sweaty — not frosted. Frost returning quickly means call a pro.

If the coil re-freezes within a day of a clean filter and open vents, stop running it and call a technician — you likely have a refrigerant or airflow problem you can't fix yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the root cause rather than just recharging refrigerant. Expect them to:

- Fully thaw the coil and inspect it and the blower wheel for dirt buildup.
- Measure static pressure and verify blower speed/CFM against the Goodman spec for your model.
- Connect gauges and check **superheat and subcooling** to determine whether the system is low on charge — and by how much.
- Perform a leak search (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) **before** adding refrigerant, since simply topping off a leaking system is a temporary fix.
- Test the TXV or metering device operation on systems that have one.
- Check the outdoor coil and condenser fan, since heat-rejection problems can indirectly affect coil temps.

Be wary of any quote to "just add a pound of Freon" without a leak test — a legitimate low-charge diagnosis includes finding where it went.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, weak airflow | Dirty filter / low airflow | Turn cooling off, fan ON to thaw; replace filter | Verify CFM, clean coil/blower |
| Warm air, coil freezes again fast | Low refrigerant (leak) | Shut off cooling; call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water pooling by indoor unit | Frozen coil melting + drain clog | Thaw, clear drain line | Clear drain, check pan/float switch |
| Cooling stops on mild/cool days | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run AC when it's cold out | Confirm no other fault |
| Coil freezes despite clean filter | Dirty coil or failing blower | Open all vents; call pro | Measure static pressure, test motor |
| Frost only on part of coil | Stuck TXV / metering issue | None — call pro | Test/replace TXV, verify charge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your actual price depends on region, system size, and whether refrigerant is involved.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + restart (DIY):** $0
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Evaporator coil / blower cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500+ (higher if the coil itself is leaking or if R-410A/R-454B refrigerant volume is large)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800 installed

Note: refrigerant prices and coil availability vary with the ongoing refrigerant transition, so quotes on older R-410A systems can trend higher.

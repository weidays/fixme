## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" isn't a digital fault code — most Goodman air conditioners won't flash a number for it. Instead, you'll notice ice or frost on the copper lines or the indoor coil, weak or no cold air, and sometimes water pooling around the air handler as the ice melts.

The evaporator coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) gets cold as refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. It's designed to stay just above freezing. When something disrupts the balance — too little airflow across the coil, or too little refrigerant inside it — the coil temperature drops below 32°F and condensation on it turns to ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, making the problem snowball until the whole coil is a block of ice.

This is a **pro-level** issue because the two most common root causes — a refrigerant leak or a failing blower — require licensed or specialized work. The thawing and basic airflow checks, however, are safe to do yourself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed return/supply airflow** — too many closed vents, a crushed flex duct, or furniture over a return register.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge makes the coil run colder than designed and it ices over. Requires a licensed tech (refrigerant handling is EPA-regulated).
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust build a film that acts like the filter clog, blocking airflow through the fins.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / capacitor** — the blower can't move enough air. Common on older ECM and PSC blowers in Goodman/Amana air handlers.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — using cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or failing TXV / metering device** — over-feeds or under-feeds the coil, causing icing. A pro diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **First, turn the thermostat to cooling OFF and the fan to ON** — this thaws the coil without stressing the compressor.

- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Check your registers and returns.** Open all supply vents and make sure return grilles aren't blocked by rugs, furniture, or a closed door.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and swap the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breaker.** If the air handler/furnace breaker is tripped, the blower isn't running — reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A frozen-then-thawed coil dumps a lot of water. Make sure the drain pan isn't overflowing and the drain line isn't clogged (a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain outlet clears many clogs).
- **Wait for a full thaw** — up to 24 hours — before restarting cooling. Running it while still iced can damage the compressor.

If the coil re-freezes after you've done all this, the cause is deeper (refrigerant or blower) and needs a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech will work through causes in order — you can sanity-check their quote against this:

1. **Confirm the coil thawed** and inspect for residual ice.
2. **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil and check the blower speed, capacitor, and motor amps.
3. **Inspect the filter and coil** for restriction, and clean the coil if it's dirty.
4. **Attach gauges** and read suction/head pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge the refrigerant charge against Goodman's spec for your model.
5. **Leak search** if the charge is low — with electronic detector, UV dye, or soap bubbles. A leak must be repaired *before* recharging; adding refrigerant to a leaking system is against EPA practice and just re-freezes.
6. **Check the metering device (TXV/piston)** if pressures are off but charge and airflow are correct.

Be cautious of any tech who "just adds a pound of Freon" without a leak search — that's a temporary fix for a leaking system.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, filter is filthy | Clogged filter starving airflow | Replace filter, thaw coil, restart | None needed if it stays fixed |
| Ice returns days after new filter | Dirty coil or weak blower | Verify vents open; thaw | Clean coil, test blower/capacitor |
| Ice + hissing/bubbling, weak cooling | Refrigerant leak (low charge) | Turn off, thaw | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice + water flooding around unit | Clog + thawed ice overflow | Clear drain line with vac | Clear drain, replace pan/switch |
| Blower runs weak or not at all | Failing motor or capacitor | Reset breaker once | Replace capacitor or blower motor |
| Coil freezes only on cool nights | Running AC in low outdoor temps | Don't cool below ~60°F outside | Add low-ambient controls if needed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Simple airflow fixes are cheap; refrigerant work is where costs climb.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10 – $30
- **Condensate drain clearing (DIY):** $0 – $25 for tablets/vac
- **Service call / diagnosis:** $80 – $180
- **Coil cleaning:** $150 – $400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Blower motor replacement (ECM):** $400 – $900
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100 – $325
- **Leak repair + recharge (accessible leak):** $500 – $1,200
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $900 – $1,600+, more if it's an R-410A phase-out or matched system issue
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350 – $700

If your Goodman system is older and the leak is in the coil itself, weigh coil replacement against overall system age.

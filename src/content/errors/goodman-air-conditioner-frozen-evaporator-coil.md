## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not typically flash a dedicated "frozen coil" fault code. This is a **symptom you see and feel**, not a board-driven code: a layer of frost or solid ice on the indoor evaporator coil (inside the air handler or above the furnace), weak or warm airflow at the vents, water pooling around the indoor unit as it thaws, and sometimes ice on the copper line running outside to the condenser.

A frozen coil almost always comes down to one of two root problems: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant** to absorb heat properly. Either way the coil surface drops below freezing and condensation turns to ice, which then blocks airflow further and makes the whole thing worse.

Running the system in this state risks liquid refrigerant returning to the compressor (slugging), which is a costly failure. That is why this is rated a **pro** issue once basic airflow checks are done.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — reduces total airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow between the fins.
4. **Blower problems** — failing blower motor, wrong fan speed tap, or a slipping belt (older units) starves the coil of air.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — a low charge drops coil pressure and temperature. Common on older systems or after prior service.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — using cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F can ice the coil.
7. **Failed metering device (TXV or piston/orifice)** — meters refrigerant incorrectly and can freeze the coil.
8. **Restricted return ductwork** — crushed or undersized returns starve airflow.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system **off** so it can thaw:

- **Turn off cooling.** Set the thermostat to OFF for cooling and set the fan to **ON** — running the blower with the compressor off speeds thawing and helps dry the coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it is gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Open and unblock all vents.** Make sure supply registers aren't closed and furniture or rugs aren't covering returns.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it isn't set unreasonably low and that batteries (if applicable) are good.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor blower isn't running at all, verify the air-handler/furnace breaker and switch are on — no blower means guaranteed freezing.
- **Check the condensate drain.** As the ice melts you'll get a lot of water. Make sure the drain line and pan aren't clogged so it doesn't overflow. Drop in a pan tablet if you have one.
- **Wait for a full thaw** (1–3 hours, longer for heavy ice) before restarting. Never chip at the ice with anything sharp.

If the coil freezes again within a day of a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a tech — that points to refrigerant or blower problems.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech will confirm the diagnosis rather than guess, so a quote should reflect steps like these:

- **Verify airflow:** inspect the filter, coil cleanliness, blower operation, and fan speed setting; may measure static pressure across the system.
- **Inspect the coil** directly for dirt and confirm it has fully thawed.
- **Attach manifold gauges** to read suction and head pressures, then calculate **superheat and subcooling** against Goodman's charging spec for that model.
- **Measure the temperature split** (return vs. supply air) — a healthy split is roughly 15–22°F.
- **Leak search** if the charge is low: electronic leak detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test. A recharge without finding the leak is only a temporary fix.
- **Check the metering device** (TXV superheat response or piston sizing).

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without checking airflow or looking for a leak first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / closed vents | Replace filter, open vents, thaw | Verify airflow, measure static pressure |
| Warm air, ice on outdoor line | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off, thaw, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Coil refreezes within a day | Blower fault or leak | Stop running AC, call pro | Test blower motor/speed, check charge |
| Water pooling indoors | Melting ice + clogged drain | Clear drain, add pan tablet | Clear/replace drain line, check float switch |
| Ice only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Stop cooling until warmer | Confirm no other fault |
| Dirty, matted coil visible | Neglected/dirty evaporator | Replace filter to prevent recurrence | Professional coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and unit variation applies):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + restart (DIY):** $0
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors on the higher end)
- **Blower capacitor / fan speed correction:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100–$350
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $200–$700 depending on charge amount and refrigerant type
- **Leak repair + recharge (combined):** $600–$1,200
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800
- **Evaporator coil replacement (if leaking/failed):** $900–$2,500+

Note: recharging without repairing the leak just buys time — the coil will freeze again once the charge drops.

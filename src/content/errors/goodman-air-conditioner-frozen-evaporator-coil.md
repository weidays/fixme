## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a digital fault code — Goodman condensers and air handlers don't flash a number for it. It's a physical condition you'll notice as ice on the indoor coil, on the copper refrigerant lines near your furnace or air handler, or sometimes at the outdoor unit's larger line.

The evaporator coil is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. It normally runs cold but above freezing. When something drops the coil temperature below 32°F — either too little warm air moving across it or too little refrigerant pressure — condensation on the coil freezes into ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, cooling drops off further, and the freeze accelerates until you get almost no cold air at all.

This is a **pro-level** problem because the two most common causes (low refrigerant and airflow restrictions inside sealed equipment) require gauges, recovery tools, or blower access most homeowners don't have. You can safely thaw it and rule out the easy stuff first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant / refrigerant leak** — low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This is a sealed-system repair; homeowners cannot legally or safely add refrigerant.
3. **Weak or failing blower motor / capacitor** — if the indoor blower can't move enough air, the coil freezes even with a clean filter.
4. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — reduces airflow enough to freeze the coil in some rooms' worth of shut registers.
5. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust bridging the fins blocks airflow through the coil itself.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — operating cooling below roughly 60–62°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Blocked condensate drain** — a backed-up drain can raise humidity and, on some setups, contribute to icing; more often it's a separate symptom of the same freeze meltwater.
8. **Stuck or failing metering device (TXV/piston)** — meters refrigerant incorrectly into the coil. Less common, requires a tech.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system **off** so it can thaw:

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** At the thermostat, set the system to Off (or Fan Only / Auto blower) so warm room air melts the ice without running the compressor.
- **Wait for a full thaw.** Give it 1–3 hours. Don't chip or scrape the ice — you can puncture the coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or older than 60–90 days, swap it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open all supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking airflow. Never run with more than a couple registers closed.
- **Check the thermostat** — confirm it's set to Cool at a reasonable setpoint (not far below 68°F, which encourages long freeze-prone run times).
- **Check the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and condenser; a tripped indoor breaker means the blower isn't running.
- **Look for water.** Thawing ice produces a lot of meltwater. Check the condensate pan and drain line — clear standing water with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain exit to prevent overflow and ceiling damage.
- **After thawing, restart cooling and watch.** If it freezes again within a few hours, stop and call a tech — that points to refrigerant or blower issues you can't safely fix.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety controls.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — readings on an iced system are meaningless.
2. **Measure airflow** — check filter, blower wheel cleanliness, blower motor amp draw and speed, and static pressure across the coil.
3. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and measure superheat and subcooling to determine if the charge is correct, low, or overcharged.
4. **Inspect for leaks** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low — adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.
5. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup restricting airflow.
6. **Test the metering device (TXV/piston)** if superheat readings are abnormal.
7. **Check the condensate drain** and float switch.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" with no leak search or airflow check, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil/lines, weak airflow | Dirty filter restricting airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Refreezes fast after thaw, low cooling | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw only; do not add refrigerant | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Little to no air from vents | Failed blower motor or capacitor | Check indoor breaker | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Ice with clean filter, older system | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw; note recurrence | Chemical coil cleaning |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~62°F outdoors | Avoid cooling on cold days | Confirm ambient limits, controls |
| Water overflowing near air handler | Blocked condensate drain | Clear drain with wet/dry vac | Clear line, test float switch |
| Uneven cooling, some vents closed | Restricted airflow from closed vents | Open all registers | Balance airflow, check ductwork |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and contractor):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40 — often the entire fix.
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180, sometimes waived if you approve the repair.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors cost more).
- **Evaporator coil cleaning (chemical):** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair:** $200–$1,500+ depending on leak location.
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $150–$600+ depending on amount and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer blends).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,500+; higher if the coil is out of warranty. Many Goodman coils carry a 10-year parts warranty to the original registered owner — labor is usually not covered.

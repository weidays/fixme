## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a digital fault code on Goodman equipment — most Goodman split-system air conditioners and heat pumps don't have a display that flashes "frozen coil." Instead, it's a physical condition you or a technician diagnose by symptoms: ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, weak or no cool air, and water pooling around the indoor unit as the ice melts.

The evaporator coil is the indoor part of your AC (inside your furnace cabinet or air handler). It's cold by design — cold refrigerant flows through it and pulls heat out of your home's air. When something drops the coil's temperature below 32°F, the moisture condensing on it freezes into ice instead of draining away. Once ice forms, it blocks airflow, which makes the coil even colder, which makes more ice. It snowballs fast.

Two things cause that temperature drop almost every time: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant in the system** (leak or undercharge). Telling those two apart is the whole job.

This is rated **pro** because confirming refrigerant charge, finding leaks, and any repair involving the sealed refrigerant system legally requires an EPA-certified technician. But several of the airflow causes are things you can safely check and fix yourself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — By far the most common. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow and drops its temperature below freezing. Cheap to rule out.
2. **Low refrigerant (leak or undercharge)** — The second most common cause. Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. Requires gauges and an EPA-certified tech; a leak must be found and repaired, not just topped off.
3. **Blocked return-air path** — Closed supply registers, blocked returns, shut interior doors, or collapsed/undersized ductwork all reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Years of dust and biofilm on the coil insulate it and restrict airflow between the fins.
5. **Failing or wrong-speed blower motor** — A weak, dirty, or improperly set blower (common after a furnace or board swap) moves too little air.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — Running cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil. Uncommon but happens in spring/fall.
7. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/piston)** — A faulty expansion valve overfeeds or underfeeds the coil. Less common; a technician diagnosis.
8. **Clogged condensate drain** — Won't cause freezing directly, but the water it backs up is often what makes homeowners first notice the problem.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the **cooling turned off** so the coil can thaw. Never scrape ice off with a tool — you can puncture the coil.

- **Turn the thermostat to OFF (cooling), then set the fan to ON.** Running the blower with no cooling melts the ice faster and dries the coil. Expect 1–24 hours to fully thaw.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Open all supply registers and clear returns.** Move furniture, rugs, or curtains off vents. Open interior doors so return air can circulate.
- **Check the breaker.** After a long freeze-up some units trip. Reset it once; if it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's calling for cool, batteries are good (if battery-powered), and the setpoint is realistic.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** If water is overflowing, clear the drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination. Standing water can trip a float switch and shut the system down.
- **After it's fully thawed, restart cooling and watch it.** If it cools normally and stays ice-free, a dirty filter was likely the culprit. If it ices up again within a day, you have a deeper problem — call a technician.

**Do not** open the refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. Refrigerant work requires EPA certification and the right tools.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech works the airflow-vs-refrigerant question in order. Use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirms it's fully thawed** — You can't measure a frozen system accurately. Some techs pre-book a return visit if it's still iced.
2. **Checks airflow first** — Inspects the filter, coil cleanliness, blower wheel, and blower speed setting. Measures temperature split (return vs supply air), which should be roughly 16–22°F on a healthy system.
3. **Attaches refrigerant gauges** — Reads suction and head pressure, calculates superheat and subcooling. Low suction pressure with high superheat points to low charge or a metering problem.
4. **Leak search if charge is low** — Electronic sniffer, UV dye, or bubble test. A responsible tech finds and repairs the leak rather than just topping off. Refilling a leaking system is a temporary, EPA-frowned-upon patch.
5. **Checks the metering device (TXV/piston)** — If airflow and charge are fine but the coil still freezes, the expansion valve is suspect.
6. **Inspects blower motor and control board** — Especially if the furnace/air handler was recently serviced and blower speed may be mis-set.

Be cautious of any tech who adds refrigerant without first ruling out airflow and without doing a leak search — that's a common way to run up a bill on a problem a $15 filter would have fixed.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked returns | Turn cooling off, replace filter, open vents, let thaw | Verify airflow, measure temp split |
| Ice on outdoor line + poor cooling that returns fast | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw system, stop running it | Gauge test, leak search, repair & recharge |
| Water pooling by indoor unit | Melting ice / clogged drain | Clear drain line with wet/dry vac | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Coil freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run cooling on cold days | Confirm charge is otherwise correct |
| Freezes again days after cleaning filter | Dirty coil, weak blower, or low charge | Confirm filter/vents are clear, then call | Clean coil, test blower, check charge |
| Freezes with clean filter and good airflow | Faulty TXV/metering device | None — needs sealed-system work | Diagnose and replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual price depends on region, unit age, and whether refrigerant work is involved.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $8–$30
- **Clear condensate drain (DIY):** $0–$20 with a wet/dry vac
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $75–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $200–$700+ depending on amount and type (R-32 on 2025+ units may differ from R-410A)
- **Leak repair (braze/component):** $200–$1,000 depending on location
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$800
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,800 (higher if the leak is age-related and the whole coil is corroded)
- **Compressor replacement (if a frozen coil slugged it):** $1,500–$2,800 — the expensive reason not to keep running a frozen unit

On an older unit, if the coil or compressor has failed and the system uses phased-out refrigerant, ask your tech to price full-system replacement before committing to a major repair.

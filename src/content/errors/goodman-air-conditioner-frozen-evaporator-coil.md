## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners don't display a specific "frozen coil" fault code — this is a physical condition, not a board alert. It means ice has built up on the indoor **evaporator coil** (the A-shaped coil above or beside your furnace/air handler).

The coil freezes when it gets **colder than it should** or **isn't getting enough warm air moving across it**. In simple terms: the coil is designed to sit just above freezing while it pulls heat out of your home's air. When either airflow drops or refrigerant pressure falls, coil temperature dives below 32°F, moisture condensing on it turns to frost, and the frost snowballs into a solid block of ice.

Ice on the coil blocks airflow even further, which makes the freeze-up worse — a runaway cycle. The result is little or no cold air, water leaking around the furnace when it melts, and, if ignored, a damaged compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A blocked filter starves the coil of airflow.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow through the fins.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — lowers coil pressure and temperature so it freezes. Requires a pro.
4. **Blower problems** — failing blower motor, wrong fan speed setting, or a slipping belt (older units) reduce airflow.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — reduces total airflow across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — running cooling when it's below ~62°F outside can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — on some setups a wet, backed-up pan and high humidity accelerate icing.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV or piston)** — feeds refrigerant incorrectly; a pro-level diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do **not** open refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan to ON.** At the thermostat, switch the system from Cool to Off, then set the fan from Auto to On. This melts the ice safely. Allow 1–3 hours.
- **Replace the air filter.** Pull it out — if you can't see light through it, it's the likely culprit. Install a fresh filter of the correct size and a reasonable MERV (8–11).
- **Check every supply and return vent.** Open closed registers and remove furniture, rugs, or boxes blocking them. You need airflow.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan under the coil, or water on the floor. Clear a visible clog at the drain line exit if you can reach it safely.
- **Check the thermostat setting.** Don't run cooling if outdoor temps are below about 62°F.
- **Check the breaker.** If the blower isn't running at all, confirm the furnace/air-handler breaker is on.
- **Only run cooling again once the coil is fully thawed and dry.** If it re-freezes within a day, stop and call a pro — that points to refrigerant or blower issues.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will confirm the freeze is gone before testing, then work through airflow and refrigerant systematically:

1. **Verify airflow** — inspect filter and coil, measure temperature split (return vs. supply air), and check blower speed and amp draw.
2. **Inspect the evaporator coil** — pulls the access panel to see if the coil fins are packed with dirt.
3. **Attach gauges and read pressures** — measures suction/discharge pressure and superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is low. This is where a refrigerant leak reveals itself.
4. **Leak search if charge is low** — electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test to locate the leak rather than just topping off.
5. **Check the metering device (TXV/piston)** and blower motor operation if airflow and charge both look off.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without checking the filter, coil, and blower — or without looking for the leak — deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice visible on coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn cooling off, fan on, thaw fully | Measure pressures & airflow to find root cause |
| Weak/warm air from vents | Dirty filter or iced coil | Replace filter; let thaw | Check temp split, blower speed |
| Water pooling near furnace | Melted ice from a freeze-up | Mop up, clear drain, thaw coil | Confirm cause so it doesn't recur |
| Coil re-freezes within a day | Refrigerant leak or blower fault | Stop running cooling; call pro | Leak search, charge correction, blower check |
| Coil dirty/black on fins | Dirty evaporator coil | Replace filter; don't scrub coil yourself | Professional coil cleaning |
| Freeze-ups only in cool weather | AC run below ~62°F outdoors | Don't run cooling in cool temps | Verify no other cause |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your price varies by region, refrigerant type, and accessibility.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain cleaning (DIY):** $0–$25
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors cost more)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$700
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500+ (higher if the coil is out of warranty; Goodman coils are often covered under a limited parts warranty if registered)

Note: on many newer Goodman units the coil part may be under warranty even when labor is not — ask the tech to check your unit's registration and serial number.

## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not usually flash a specific "frozen coil" fault code — there is no dedicated blinking-LED code for icing on most Goodman control boards. Instead, a frozen evaporator coil is a **physical condition** you discover: a block of ice on the indoor A-coil (in the furnace or air handler cabinet) and often the copper refrigerant line, paired with weak or no cold air at the vents.

The evaporator coil is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your indoor air. When something interrupts the balance between airflow across the coil and refrigerant flow inside it, the coil temperature drops below freezing, condensation turns to ice, and airflow collapses — which makes the ice grow faster. Left alone, the freeze can push liquid refrigerant back to the compressor and cause expensive damage.

Because the true cause ranges from a $0 filter swap to a refrigerant leak requiring EPA-certified repair, we rate this **pro** severity once the obvious airflow checks fail.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and drop its temperature.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — very common on older units; low charge over-cools the coil. Requires a certified tech.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — not enough air is moving across the coil.
6. **Running the AC when it is too cold outside** (below ~62°F) — running cooling on a cool night can ice the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — a secondary symptom that also signals the coil has been icing and dripping heavily.
8. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/piston)** — over-feeds or under-feeds refrigerant; a pro-level diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — several are free and may solve it entirely:

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the thermostat fan to ON.** This runs room-temperature air over the coil to thaw the ice safely. Never chip, scrape, or heat the ice.
- **Check the air filter.** If it is gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. Do this every 1–3 months.
- **Walk the house and open every supply vent and return grille.** Closing too many rooms starves the coil of airflow. Move furniture, rugs, and drapes off vents.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it is on COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and replace the batteries if it is battery-powered and the screen is dim.
- **Check the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and outdoor unit. A tripped breaker can leave the blower off while the compressor still tries to run.
- **Look at the condensate pan and drain.** If you see standing water, clear the drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination and add drain tablets.

After the coil is fully thawed and dry, restore normal cooling. If it freezes again within a day, stop and call a technician — the cause is likely refrigerant or blower related.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech will not just "add refrigerant." Expect them to:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before taking any measurements.
- **Measure airflow** — check filter, blower motor amps, capacitor, and static pressure across the coil.
- **Inspect and clean the evaporator coil** if it is dirty or biofilm-coated.
- **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to judge whether the charge is correct — not just guessing by pressure.
- **Leak-search** with electronic detectors or dye if the charge is low, because refilling a leaking system is only a temporary fix and is against EPA rules if the leak isn't addressed.
- **Test the metering device (TXV or fixed piston)** for correct refrigerant feed.

If a quote jumps straight to "you need refrigerant" without any airflow check or leak search, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / closed vents | Replace filter, open all vents, run fan to thaw | Verify airflow, check blower & static pressure |
| Freezes again days after filter change | Low refrigerant from leak | Thaw, then stop and call | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Ice on copper line + hissing outside | Refrigerant leak | Turn off, thaw, call | Locate leak, repair, evacuate & recharge |
| Coil freezes, blower sounds weak | Failing blower / bad capacitor | Thaw, listen for fan | Test motor amps, replace capacitor or motor |
| Water pooling near air handler | Backed-up condensate drain | Clear drain with wet/dry vac, add tablets | Clear line, check pan & float switch |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Running AC below ~62°F outside | Avoid cooling in cool weather | Confirm no other fault present |
| Coil ices with clean filter & open vents | Dirty coil or stuck TXV | None — call | Clean coil or replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and unit-size differences apply):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 professional.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors run higher).
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant leak search + minor repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-454B units may cost more).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ if the coil itself is corroded or leaking.
- **Compressor damage** (if the freeze was ignored and slugged the compressor): $1,500–$3,000+, sometimes making replacement more sensible.

Ask whether your Goodman still has active **parts warranty** — Goodman coils and compressors often carry long limited warranties, which cuts the part cost though not labor.

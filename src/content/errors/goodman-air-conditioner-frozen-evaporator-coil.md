## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a specific "frozen coil" fault code — this is a physical condition you diagnose by symptom, not by a board reading. A frozen evaporator coil means the indoor coil (inside your air handler or above your furnace) has dropped below freezing and ice has built up on it.

The evaporator coil is where warm indoor air gives up its heat to cold refrigerant. When something disrupts that balance — too little airflow across the coil or too little refrigerant in the system — the coil surface gets cold enough to freeze condensation into a block of ice. Once iced over, the coil can no longer absorb heat, so your Goodman runs constantly but blows warm or weak air, and you may see water pooling around the indoor unit as the ice melts.

This is rated **pro** because the two most common root causes — restricted airflow and low refrigerant — can damage the compressor if ignored, and refrigerant work is legally restricted to certified technicians.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow, letting it drop below freezing.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — Furniture over registers, closed dampers, or crushed flex duct reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and choke airflow, even with a clean filter.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature until it freezes. This is a repair, never a "top-off" without finding the leak.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor** — A weak or dying blower (ECM or PSC) moves too little air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — Operating cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temp can cause the coil to freeze.
7. **Stuck or failed expansion valve / TXV** — Meters refrigerant incorrectly, dropping coil temperature.
8. **Blocked condensate drain causing sensor or airflow issues** — Less common, but a flooded pan and secondary problems can accompany freeze-ups.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner and often solve the problem:

- **Turn off cooling first.** Set the thermostat to **Off** (not just up), then set the fan to **On**. Running the blower with the compressor off speeds the thaw and dries the coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This is the #1 fix.
- **Open and unblock all supply registers and return grilles.** Move rugs, furniture, and curtains. Closing off "unused" rooms often causes freeze-ups.
- **Check the thermostat** is set to a reasonable temperature and Cool mode, not stuck calling for cold air constantly.
- **Check the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and condenser. A tripped breaker on one but not the other can leave the fan or compressor mismatched.
- **Look at the condensate line and pan.** Clear any obvious clog with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain outlet, and pour a cup of the pan tablets if you have them.
- **Wait for a full thaw** (1–24 hrs) before restarting cooling. Never chip, scrape, or blow-dry the ice — you can puncture the coil.

If it freezes again within a day or two of a clean filter and open vents, stop running it and call a pro — you likely have a refrigerant or blower problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech will:

1. **Confirm the ice is fully thawed** before testing, then run the system while measuring airflow.
2. **Check static pressure** across the coil and blower to confirm the duct/coil isn't restricted.
3. **Inspect the coil and blower wheel** for dirt buildup.
4. **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to determine if the charge is correct — low readings point to a leak or a TXV problem.
5. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low. A reputable tech will not just add refrigerant and leave.
6. **Verify blower motor speed and amp draw**, especially on ECM motors, to rule out a weak blower.

Sanity check: if a tech says "you're low on refrigerant" but never mentions finding the leak, ask where the leak is. Topping off a leaking system is a temporary, wasteful fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines/coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Turn off cooling, replace filter, open vents, thaw | Measure static pressure, confirm airflow |
| Freezes again days after filter change | Low refrigerant from leak | Stop running AC, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Warm air, coil frozen, filter clean | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw, inspect for visible dirt | Professional coil cleaning |
| Weak airflow at all vents, no ice yet | Failing blower motor | Note noise/behavior for tech | Test blower amps/speed, replace if needed |
| Water around indoor unit | Melting ice + clogged drain | Vac the drain line, add tablets | Clear drain, find freeze root cause |
| Freezes only in mild/cool weather | AC run below ~60°F outdoor | Avoid cooling in cold weather | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200 pro, or DIY with a vac
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor replacement (PSC):** $300–$650
- **Blower motor replacement (ECM/variable-speed):** $600–$1,100
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV/expansion valve replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ if the coil itself is leaking

A simple filter/airflow fix is free-to-cheap. Costs climb fast once refrigerant or a coil is involved, especially on systems using R-410A or newer refrigerants.

## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners don't throw a dedicated "frozen evaporator coil" fault code — this is a **physical condition** a homeowner or technician diagnoses, not a message on a board or thermostat. You'll notice it as poor cooling, ice on the copper lines near the indoor coil, or water pooling around the furnace/air handler.

The evaporator coil is the indoor part of your Goodman system where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. When something disrupts that heat exchange — too little warm air flowing across the coil, or too little refrigerant inside it — the coil surface drops below freezing and condensation turns to ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, cooling collapses, and the problem snowballs.

This is rated **pro severity** because the two most common root causes (a refrigerant leak or a weak blower) require licensed diagnosis, and running the system frozen can damage the compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Starves the coil of warm return air.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharge lowers coil temperature and pressure, freezing the coil. Requires a licensed tech; refrigerant is never a DIY item.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil block airflow and heat transfer.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / capacitor** — reduced CFM across the coil. Common on older Goodman air handlers and furnaces.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — restricts airflow enough to freeze the coil.
6. **Collapsed or undersized ductwork / crushed return** — chronic low airflow.
7. **Running the AC in low outdoor temps** — using cooling below roughly 60°F outside can freeze the coil.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV / piston)** — over- or under-feeds refrigerant; a tech-level diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these before spending money on a service call:

- **Turn the system to Off, set the fan to On.** This is the most important step — it stops making ice and starts thawing the coil safely. Let it run for several hours.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's in Cool mode with a reasonable setpoint, and replace the batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim.
- **Check the breaker.** If the furnace/air handler breaker tripped, the blower can't run — but only reset a breaker once.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply and return registers aren't closed, covered by furniture, or blocked by rugs. Keep the return grille clear.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** A frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it melts. Make sure the pan isn't overflowing and the drain line isn't clogged. Drain tablets help prevent clogs.

If the coil thaws, you replaced the filter, and cooling comes back normally — you may be done. If it **re-freezes**, stop and call a pro; the root cause is deeper.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech will:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — you can't get accurate readings on ice.
2. **Measure airflow / static pressure** and inspect the blower motor, capacitor, and wheel for dust buildup.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt and the filter/duct path for restrictions.
4. **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to see whether the system is undercharged — a strong sign of a leak.
5. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector or dye) if the charge is low — Goodman won't cover a recharge without finding the leak first, and topping off a leaking system is a temporary fix.
6. **Check the metering device (TXV or piston)** for proper operation.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or blames the coil without checking airflow and filter, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm air from vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Set fan to On, replace filter, thaw fully | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Coil re-freezes after thawing | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running; call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow at every vent | Failing blower or capacitor | Replace filter first | Test blower motor & capacitor |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Melting ice overflowing drain | Clear/clean drain line, add tablets | Clear drain, check pan & switch |
| Ice on outdoor line set / AC won't cool below 60°F outside | Running AC in cold weather | Turn off cooling until warmer | Confirm charge if it persists |
| Coil dirty and matted | Dust/biofilm buildup | Light no-rinse coil cleaner if accessible | Deep coil cleaning, fin combing |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your price varies by region and contractor.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thawing the coil (DIY):** $0 — just time with the fan on
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90–$180
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors cost more)
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000 installed
- **Compressor damage from running frozen:** $1,200–$2,800+ (often makes replacement worth considering)

The takeaway: catch it early with a filter and a thaw, and it costs nearly nothing. Run it frozen for days and you risk the most expensive parts in the system.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is not a specific Goodman fault code — most Goodman air conditioners do not display a numeric code for it. Instead, it is a physical condition you'll see or feel: ice builds up on the indoor coil (inside your air handler or on top of the furnace) and often on the copper refrigerant lines outside.

The evaporator coil gets cold on purpose — that's how it pulls heat out of your home's air. But it's designed to stay just above freezing. When either **airflow across the coil drops** or **refrigerant pressure drops**, the coil temperature falls below 32°F, moisture in the air condenses and freezes, and the ice compounds until the coil is a solid block. Once it's iced over, your Goodman blows warm air and cooling grinds to a halt.

This is rated **pro** because most root causes — low refrigerant, a failing blower motor, or a metering-device problem — require gauges, sealed-system tools, or motor diagnosis. The one thing you can safely do yourself is thaw it and rule out airflow basics.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A blocked filter starves the coil of airflow, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. On Goodman R-410A and newer R-454B systems, this needs a licensed tech to find the leak, repair it, and recharge — DIY refrigerant work is illegal and unsafe.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and biofilm on the coil fins act like insulation and block airflow, mimicking a dirty filter.
4. **Failing or slow blower motor** — A weak ECM or PSC blower, bad capacitor, or wheel caked with dirt moves too little air. Common on older Goodman air handlers.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too-few supply/return vents** — Restricting airflow (furniture over vents, closed rooms) reduces load across the coil.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — Running cooling below ~60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil. Rare in summer, but happens in shoulder seasons or overnight.
7. **Faulty metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** — A stuck or failing expansion valve/orifice starves the coil. Requires a tech.
8. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — Less a cause of freezing and more a consequence; a frozen coil overflows a full drain pan.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **First, set your thermostat to OFF for cooling and switch the fan to ON** — this thaws the coil without running the compressor.

- **Air filter:** Pull it. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and set FAN to ON to speed thawing. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the indoor and outdoor breakers. Don't repeatedly flip them — one reset only.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house. Open all supply registers, remove furniture/rugs blocking them, and make sure return grilles aren't covered.
- **Condensate drain:** Check the drain pan and line for standing water. Meltwater from a thawing coil can overflow — place towels and, if you have a wet/dry vac, clear the drain line.
- **Look for ice:** Confirm ice on the coil or the larger copper line outside before assuming a freeze.

Let the coil thaw **completely** (1–3 hours) before running cooling again. If it re-freezes with a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a pro — you're likely dealing with refrigerant or blower issues.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Goodman tech should:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before taking any readings — measuring on ice gives false numbers.
2. **Check airflow** — inspect the filter, blower wheel, and coil; measure static pressure and blower speed. On ECM-equipped Goodman air handlers they may check the motor's programmed airflow settings.
3. **Attach gauges and read superheat/subcooling** — this tells them whether the charge is low, whether there's a leak, or whether the metering device (TXV/orifice) is failing.
4. **Leak-search** if charge is low — electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test. Recharging without finding the leak is a red flag; ask if they located it.
5. **Inspect the evaporator coil surface** and the condensate drain.
6. **Verify the refrigerant type** for your model — Goodman moved from R-410A to R-454B on newer units, and charging differs.

Sanity check: a good quote names the *root cause* (e.g. "1 lb low on R-410A due to a leak at the coil flare") — not just "needs freon." If refrigerant keeps being added year after year, you have an unrepaired leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, warm air, dirty filter | Clogged filter | Replace filter, thaw, restart | None if it holds |
| Re-freezes with clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, thaw, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow at all vents | Failing blower / dirty wheel | Check filter, then call pro | Test motor, capacitor, clean wheel |
| Ice on outdoor copper line | Low charge or metering issue | Shut cooling off | Gauge readings, TXV/orifice check |
| Coil freezes at night / cool weather | Low outdoor temp | Avoid AC below ~60°F outside | Confirm no other fault |
| Water overflowing air handler | Thawing coil + clogged drain | Vac out drain line, add tablets | Clear drain, check pan/switch |
| Only some rooms cold, coil frosts | Blocked vents / undersized return | Open all vents & returns | Assess ductwork airflow |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your Goodman's model, region, and labor rates will move these.

- **Air filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain cleaning (DIY):** $0–$25; pro service $75–$200
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (ECM/PSC) replacement:** $400–$1,000+
- **Coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-454B tends to cost more per pound)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500 (parts often warrantied on registered Goodman units; you still pay labor + refrigerant)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$1,000

If your Goodman is under its registered warranty, the coil or compressor part may be covered — but labor and refrigerant usually are not. Always ask.

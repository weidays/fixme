## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is not a fault code Goodman displays—it is a physical symptom. The evaporator coil (inside your indoor air handler or above your furnace, in a cased coil like a Goodman CAPF or CHPF) has dropped below 32°F and moisture in the air is freezing onto it as frost or a solid block of ice.

Cooling depends on warm indoor air constantly flowing across a cold coil. When either the airflow drops too low or the refrigerant charge is wrong, the coil gets colder than it should, condensation freezes, and the ice further blocks airflow—a runaway loop. Eventually you get **warm air from the vents, water on the floor, and a coil encased in ice**.

The two root categories are almost always **too little airflow** or **too little refrigerant**. Everything below sorts into one of those.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil and it ices over.
2. **Blocked return/supply airflow** — closed or blocked vents, collapsed flex duct, or a dirty blower wheel.
3. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — a slow leak lowers pressure, the coil runs colder, and it freezes. Requires a licensed tech.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust insulate the coil so it can't shed cold, and airflow suffers.
5. **Failing blower motor or wrong blower speed** — an ECM/PSC motor running slow or a mis-set tap starves airflow.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — using cooling below about 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **Thermostat set too low / continuous run** — extreme low setpoints keep the compressor running long enough to ice up.
8. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — pooled water at the coil can freeze; also trips some safety float switches.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with cooling turned **off** so the coil can thaw:

- **Turn the thermostat from COOL to OFF, and set the fan to ON.** Running the blower with cooling off melts the ice fastest and safely.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, that's likely your problem. Use the correct size and a MERV 8–11 pleated filter.
- **Open and unblock all supply vents** and make sure return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** If there's standing water, clear the drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination, and add drain tablets. A full pan can trip a float switch and stop cooling.
- **Check the breaker.** If the outdoor unit tripped, leave it off until the coil is fully thawed and the cause is understood—don't repeatedly reset it.
- **Give it time.** Wait 1–3 hours (longer for a solid block) for full thaw before restarting cooling.

If the coil re-freezes after a clean filter and clear vents, stop and call a pro—the remaining causes involve refrigerant or the blower.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Goodman tech should:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before taking any measurements—readings on an iced coil are meaningless.
2. **Measure airflow / temperature split.** They check the temperature drop across the coil (supply vs. return) and inspect the blower wheel, motor speed, and duct static pressure.
3. **Attach gauges and read pressures.** Low suction pressure with normal-to-high superheat points to low charge or airflow; they'll interpret superheat and subcooling together.
4. **Leak-search if charge is low.** Never just "top off" refrigerant without finding the leak—ask them to locate it (electronic detector, dye, or bubble test).
5. **Inspect the coil and drain.** For dirt buildup and drain restriction.
6. **Verify blower operation** on ECM/PSC motors and correct speed tap for your system size.

Sanity check: if a tech proposes adding refrigerant without mentioning a leak search, or replacing a coil before ruling out a dirty filter and airflow, ask why.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, visible ice on indoor coil/lines | Restricted airflow | Turn off cooling, fan ON, replace filter, open vents | Measure static pressure, clean coil/blower |
| Coil re-freezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | None—shut off cooling | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Water pooling near air handler/furnace | Melting ice + clogged drain | Clear drain with wet/dry vac, add tablets | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Ice only forms at night / cool weather | Low charge or low outdoor temp | Don't run AC below ~60°F outside | Verify charge, check outdoor conditions |
| Weak airflow from all vents | Dirty blower wheel or slow motor | Replace filter, unblock returns | Clean blower, test ECM/PSC motor & speed |
| Frost building slowly over days | Gradual refrigerant leak or dirty coil | Shut off cooling, call pro | Diagnose leak vs. dirty coil |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and system-size variation applies):

- **Filter replacement / thaw:** $0–$30 DIY.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 professional.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Blower motor cleaning or speed correction:** $100–$300.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400.
- **Leak repair + recharge (minor):** $400–$800.
- **ECM blower motor replacement:** $500–$1,000.
- **Evaporator coil replacement (major leak):** $1,000–$2,500+ depending on coil model (CAPF/CHPF) and refrigerant type.

Note: older Goodman systems using R‑22 are more expensive to recharge, and a leaking coil is often worth replacing rather than repeatedly refilling.

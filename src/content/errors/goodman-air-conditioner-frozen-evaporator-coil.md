## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a numeric fault code on most Goodman air conditioners—it's a physical condition you'll notice as ice or frost on the indoor coil, the copper refrigerant lines near the air handler or furnace, or dripping water once it starts to melt. On variable-speed and inverter Goodman models, a severe freeze may trigger a low-suction-pressure or airflow-related board fault, but standard single- and two-stage units simply keep running and stop cooling.

The evaporator coil is the indoor part of your system that absorbs heat from your home's air. It's meant to run cold—but above freezing. When something drops the coil temperature below 32°F, condensation on the coil turns to ice. That ice blocks airflow, which makes the problem worse in a spiral until the whole coil is encased and your home stops cooling.

The two root categories are almost always **not enough airflow across the coil** or **low refrigerant charge**. Fixing the ice without fixing the cause guarantees it comes back.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature until it freezes.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow, even with a clean filter.
3. **Blower problem** — a failing blower motor, wrong speed setting, or slipping belt (older units) reduces airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — undercharged systems run the coil colder than designed. This requires a pro.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too-few supply/return vents** — starving the system of return air.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Failed metering device (TXV/orifice)** — feeds too much refrigerant to the coil, causing localized freezing.
8. **Stuck contactor or thermostat wiring fault** keeping the compressor running non-stop.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do them before spending money on a service call.

- **Turn the AC to OFF, then set the thermostat fan to ON.** This runs the blower without the compressor and speeds up thawing. Never run cooling while the coil is iced.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Let it fully thaw.** Give it several hours (up to 24 for heavy ice). Place towels around the air handler—melting ice produces a surprising amount of water.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** A clogged drain can overflow as the coil melts. Clear standing water with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain outlet if you can reach it safely.
- **Open all supply registers and clear return grilles.** Move furniture, rugs, or drapes blocking vents. Closing too many rooms starves airflow.
- **Verify the thermostat isn't set unrealistically low** (e.g., 60°F) and that batteries are fresh if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor blower breaker tripped, the coil can freeze from no airflow while the outdoor unit runs.

After thawing and a filter change, run the system and watch: if it re-freezes within a day, you have a coil, blower, or refrigerant problem that needs a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Goodman tech will confirm the cause rather than just recharging refrigerant. Expect them to:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing—a frozen coil gives false readings.
- Measure **airflow / temperature split** across the coil (return vs. supply temperature) to spot restriction.
- Inspect the **evaporator coil and blower wheel** for dirt buildup.
- Check **blower motor operation and speed tap** (or ECM programming on variable-speed models).
- Take **refrigerant pressures and superheat/subcooling** readings to determine if the charge is correct.
- If refrigerant is low, **perform a leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or bubble test)—not just top it off.
- Test the **TXV or metering device** if pressures point that way.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or airflow check, ask why. Adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary fix and an EPA gray area.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter | Replace filter, thaw coil, restart | None needed if it stays fixed |
| Re-freezes after clean filter | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw; keep filter fresh | Deep-clean coil, check blower |
| Little to no air from vents | Blower motor / speed fault | Check breaker, fan-ON setting | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Freezes + hissing or oily residue | Refrigerant leak | Shut off, let thaw | Leak search, repair, evacuate & recharge |
| Ice only in mild/cool weather | Running AC too cold outside | Don't run AC below ~60°F outdoor | Verify controls, low-ambient limits |
| Freezes with clean filter & good charge | Bad TXV / metering device | None | Replace metering device, recheck charge |
| Water overflowing near furnace | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, vac line | Clear/repair drain, add safety switch |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US pricing including labor; your area and unit access will vary.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + diagnostic service call:** $90–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM/variable-speed higher)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$500
- **Refrigerant recharge (after leak fix):** $200–$700+ depending on refrigerant type and amount (R-410A/R-32 pricing varies)
- **Leak repair + evacuate + recharge (combined):** $600–$1,600+
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$1,000
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $900–$2,500+

If your Goodman coil itself is leaking and out of warranty, weigh coil replacement against overall system age—coils are a major cost driver.

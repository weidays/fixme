## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners don't display a specific "frozen coil" error code—there's no digital fault to read on a standard split system. Instead, a frozen evaporator coil is a **condition** you diagnose by symptom: weak or no cold air, a block of ice on the copper coil or the refrigerant lines near the indoor air handler, water pooling around the furnace when it melts, and the compressor running without cooling the house.

The evaporator coil (indoors, above or beside the furnace/air handler) is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your air. It normally runs cold but above freezing. When something drops the coil temperature below 32°F—too little airflow across it, or too little refrigerant inside it—condensation on the coil freezes, then snowballs into a solid block of ice. Once iced over, the coil can't transfer heat at all, so cooling stops entirely.

The real risk isn't the ice itself—it's what freezing implies. Low refrigerant means a leak, and running an iced coil can send liquid refrigerant back to the compressor and destroy it. That's why this is rated **pro**: the thaw is DIY, but the root cause usually isn't.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — Too many closed registers or blocked returns reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and grime insulate the coil and choke airflow across the fins, even with a clean filter.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature, causing freezing. This is a leak, not something that "runs low" normally.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — If the indoor fan spins too slowly, airflow drops and the coil freezes.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — Cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV / orifice)** — A restricted expansion valve or piston starves the coil and causes localized freezing.
8. **Clogged condensate drain** — Backed-up water can contribute to icing and, when the coil thaws, floods the pan.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do. **First, switch the thermostat to OFF for cooling and FAN to ON** to thaw the coil safely.

- **Thaw first:** Set cooling OFF, fan ON. Let all ice melt fully—1 to 3 hours. Do not chip or scrape the ice off the coil or lines.
- **Check the air filter:** Pull it out. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it with the correct size. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Open your vents:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or filters stacked up.
- **Check the thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and that it isn't calling for cooling on an unusually cold day.
- **Check the breaker:** After thawing, confirm the AC and air handler breakers are on.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan:** When the ice melts, look for standing water or overflow. You can vacuum the drain line with a wet/dry vac from the outside end.

If the coil freezes again within a day of a clean filter and open vents, **stop running it in cool mode and call a technician.** Do not open the sealed refrigerant system, and never handle refrigerant yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from airflow to refrigerant, in order:

1. **Confirms the thaw** and inspects the coil, filter, and blower.
2. **Measures airflow / static pressure** to catch a dirty coil, undersized ductwork, or a weak blower.
3. **Checks blower motor and capacitor** for slow or failing operation.
4. **Attaches gauges** to read suction/head pressures and calculates superheat and subcooling—this reveals low charge or a metering-device problem.
5. **If charge is low, performs a leak search** (electronic sniffer, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just "topping off." A responsible shop won't repeatedly add refrigerant to a known leak.
6. **Inspects the TXV or metering piston** if pressures suggest a restriction.

Sanity check: if a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or airflow check, ask why. Adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary bandage, not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, weak airflow | Dirty/clogged filter | Thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, check duct static |
| Coil refreezes with clean filter | Dirty evaporator coil | Open vents, thaw | Deep-clean coil, inspect blower |
| No cold air, hissing near coil | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off cooling, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice + slow/weak indoor fan | Failing blower or capacitor | Thaw, note fan speed | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Water flooding furnace area | Clogged condensate drain | Vacuum drain line | Clear drain, treat pan, check pump |
| Freezes only on cool days | Running AC below ~60°F outdoor | Don't run cooling when cold | Confirm no other fault |
| Ice on part of coil only | Stuck TXV / metering device | Turn off cooling, call pro | Replace TXV or piston |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Simple airflow fixes are cheap; sealed-system repairs are not.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150–$250 pro
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900+
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer R-32 units)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900
- **Full evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ (higher if the coil is out of warranty or the leak is in the coil itself)

Goodman coils often carry a limited parts warranty if registered—ask the tech to check your serial number, which can drop the parts portion to near $0 on a covered coil.

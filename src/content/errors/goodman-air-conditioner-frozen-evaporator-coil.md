## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" isn't a fault code your Goodman thermostat displays — it's a physical condition. The indoor evaporator coil (inside your air handler or above your furnace) drops below 32°F and the moisture on it turns to ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, cooling gets worse, and the block of ice grows until the coil is a solid brick.

You'll usually notice it as: warm air from the vents, water pooling around the furnace or air handler, a hissing or bubbling sound, or visible frost on the copper refrigerant line running to the outdoor unit.

The root cause is almost always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant** (a charge or leak problem). This is marked **pro** severity because confirming the cause often requires refrigerant gauges and leak detection — but several of the checks below are safe DIY.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of the warm return air it needs to stay above freezing.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — furniture over registers or too many closed rooms chokes airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins reduce heat transfer and airflow.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong blower speed** — a slow blower (bad capacitor, failing ECM, or wrong tap setting) moves too little air.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — a real and common cause; it lowers coil pressure and temperature until it ices. Requires a licensed tech.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — operating below roughly 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **Faulty TXV or metering device** — a stuck expansion valve or blocked fixed orifice over-cools the coil (varies by model — some Goodman units use a TXV, others a fixed piston).
8. **Blocked condensate drain triggering a float switch** — not a direct freeze cause, but a clogged drain and a wet freeze often show up together.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Do all of them with the system in fan-only mode first so the coil can thaw.**

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan to ON.** At the thermostat, set the mode to Off (or Fan Only) and the fan to ON. This thaws the ice without running the compressor. Wait 1–3 hours (up to 24 for heavy ice).
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of freeze-ups.
- **Open all supply and return vents.** Walk the house and confirm registers aren't closed or blocked by rugs, furniture, or drapes.
- **Check the thermostat batteries** if it's battery-powered and the display is dim or blank.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor blower isn't running, confirm the furnace/air-handler breaker and switch are on.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** If water is pooling, look for a full drain pan or a clogged drain line. You can clear standing water with a wet/dry vac and drop in a drain tablet — but do not disassemble the coil or its housing.
- **Look for water damage** near the unit and mop it up so a thawing coil doesn't flood.

If the coil freezes again within a day or two after a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a pro — you likely have a refrigerant or blower problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech should:

1. **Confirm it's fully thawed** before testing — readings on an iced coil are meaningless.
2. **Measure airflow / temperature split** across the coil and check blower speed and static pressure.
3. **Inspect the coil, filter, and blower wheel** for dirt.
4. **Connect refrigerant gauges** and read suction/head pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge the charge. Low readings point to a leak or metering issue.
5. **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low — recharging without finding the leak is a temporary fix and a red flag.
6. **Test the TXV or check the fixed orifice** if pressures suggest a metering fault.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" with no airflow check and no leak search, ask questions — repeated top-offs mask a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, ice on indoor coil, gray filter | Dirty air filter | Thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, check static pressure |
| Ice returns days after new filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Frost on line to outdoor unit, weak airflow | Slow/failing blower | Confirm blower runs | Test ECM/capacitor, correct speed tap |
| Coil ices only in cool weather | Running AC when too cold out | Don't run AC below ~60°F | Confirm outdoor lockout / low-ambient kit |
| Water around furnace, float switch tripped | Clogged condensate drain | Vac drain, add tablet | Clear/flush line, test float switch |
| Ice plus dirty visible coil fins | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw only; don't scrub coil | Professional coil cleaning |
| Normal filter/airflow but still freezes | Stuck TXV / blocked orifice | Call pro | Test/replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and access):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Service call / diagnostic:** $90–$180
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **ECM blower motor / module replacement:** $500–$1,000
- **TXV or metering device replacement:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 (higher if the coil itself leaks and must be replaced)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500

A frozen coil caused by a dirty filter or closed vents can genuinely cost **$0** beyond a filter. Refrigerant and coil issues are where the real money is — and why this is a pro-level problem.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is not a fault code Goodman displays on a control board — it's a physical symptom you'll notice as poor cooling, ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, and sometimes water pooling around the air handler or furnace as the ice melts.

The evaporator coil (indoor, inside your air handler or above your furnace) is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. That coil must stay above freezing. When something drops the coil temperature too low — usually restricted airflow or low refrigerant — condensation on the coil freezes into ice. The ice then blocks airflow further, making the problem snowball until the coil is a solid block.

Once frozen, the system stops cooling effectively and puts your compressor at risk. This is a **pro-level** problem: while thawing is DIY, most root causes (refrigerant leaks, blower faults) require a licensed technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air, so the coil temperature plummets below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — a low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This is the second most common cause and is *not* a DIY fix. Handling refrigerant requires EPA certification and the leak must be found, not just topped off.
3. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns reduce airflow across the coil.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust caking the coil fins insulate it and restrict airflow.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — if the indoor fan spins too slowly or not at all, airflow drops. Varies by model: PSC-motor air handlers behave differently than ECM/variable-speed units.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain (indirect)** — a plugged drain can trip a safety float switch, but standing water and high humidity can also worsen icing conditions.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

- **Turn off cooling first.** Set the thermostat to OFF (cool), then set the fan to ON. Running the fan without cooling helps thaw the coil safely.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Verify the thermostat.** Make sure it's not set unusually low and that it's actually calling for the fan.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm the air handler/furnace breaker and the outdoor unit breaker are both on.
- **Walk the vents.** Open all supply registers and clear anything blocking return grilles. Don't run with most vents closed.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** If you see standing water, the drain may be clogged. Clear the visible drain line opening and add drain tablets. Do not disassemble the pan or float switch.
- **Wait for a full thaw** (1-24 hours) before restarting cooling. If it refreezes, stop and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician should work through this so you can sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before taking any measurements — readings on an iced coil are meaningless.
2. **Measure airflow and static pressure** across the coil and check blower motor operation, capacitor, and speed settings.
3. **Inspect the filter and evaporator coil** for restriction and dirt buildup.
4. **Attach gauges and read pressures**, then calculate superheat and subcooling to determine if the charge is correct. A low charge with normal airflow points to a refrigerant leak.
5. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or bubble solution) if the charge is low — a legitimate shop will not just top off and leave.
6. **Check the metering device (TXV/piston)** if pressures suggest a restriction rather than a low charge.

Be wary of any quote that adds refrigerant without a leak search, or that recommends a full system replacement before ruling out a dirty filter or blower issue.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil or copper lines | Low airflow (dirty filter) | Replace filter, thaw with fan ON | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Coil refreezes after thawing | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw, then stop and call a pro | Leak search, seal, recharge |
| Weak airflow at vents | Slow/failed blower or capacitor | Check filter and vents | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Warm air, ice + water at furnace | Frozen coil overflowing | Turn off cooling, thaw fully | Diagnose root cause |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Stop cooling until warmer | Confirm no other fault |
| Coil visibly caked with dust | Dirty evaporator coil | None (behind panels) | Professional coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Regional labor and unit size affect pricing.

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thawing the coil:** $0 (DIY, just time).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250 by a pro; ~$15 for tablets DIY.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (PSC); $600–$1,200+ (ECM/variable-speed).
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type. R-454B and low-supply refrigerants can push this higher.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $1,000–$2,500+ installed, depending on cased vs uncased and refrigerant recovery.
- **Compressor damage from prolonged running while frozen:** $1,500–$3,000+ — the reason not to keep running an iced unit.

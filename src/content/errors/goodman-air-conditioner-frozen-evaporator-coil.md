## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a fault code Goodman displays on a control board—it's a physical condition you'll notice as weak or no cooling, ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, and often water around the furnace or air handler. The evaporator coil is the indoor part of your Goodman system that absorbs heat from your home's air. It runs cold by design, but it should stay above freezing.

When something drops the coil temperature below 32°F—usually too little airflow across it, or too little refrigerant inside it—moisture in the air freezes onto the coil. Ice acts as insulation, so cooling gets worse, more ice forms, and the whole coil can lock up in a solid block. This is a **pro-level** problem: the safe DIY steps (thaw and airflow checks) are limited, and if they don't fix it, the underlying cause needs gauges and refrigerant tools you shouldn't touch.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow, and it freezes.
2. **Blocked return air or closed vents** — Too many closed supply registers, crushed flex duct, or a blocked return grille reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and cut airflow. Common in homes without regular filter changes.
4. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — A refrigerant leak lowers pressure in the coil, which lowers its temperature and causes freezing. Requires a tech—no DIY.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — If the indoor blower can't move enough air, the coil freezes even with a clean filter.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — Operating cooling below ~60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil; not a fault, just misuse.
7. **Metering device / TXV problem** — A stuck or failing expansion valve floods or starves the coil. Diagnosis and repair are technician-only.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Turn the system to OFF and the fan to ON (fan-only)** first to start thawing.

- **Air filter:** Pull it out and look. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Set the mode to **Off** (or fan-only) to stop cooling while ice melts. Don't set cooling colder to "force" it—that makes freezing worse.
- **Supply and return vents:** Walk the house and open any closed registers. Make sure furniture, rugs, or curtains aren't blocking return grilles.
- **Breaker:** If the system tripped, that's a symptom—don't repeatedly reset it. Reset once; if it trips again, call a pro.
- **Condensate drain / pan:** A frozen coil dumps a lot of water as it thaws. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear the drain line with a wet/dry vacuum at the outdoor termination. A clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut the system down.
- **Let it thaw fully** (1–3 hours) before running cooling again. **Never chip or scrape the ice**—you can puncture the coil.

If the coil freezes again after a filter change and full thaw, stop and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech confirms the cause instead of just adding refrigerant. Expect them to:

- Verify the coil has fully thawed, then measure **static pressure and airflow (CFM)** across the coil to catch airflow restrictions.
- Inspect the filter, blower wheel, and evaporator coil for dirt; test the **blower motor and run capacitor**.
- Connect gauges and check **superheat and subcooling** against Goodman's spec for your model and metering device—this distinguishes low charge from an airflow problem.
- If charge is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector or dye) rather than just topping off. Note the refrigerant type—R-410A on most systems, R-454B on 2025+ units, which affects handling and cost.
- Check the **TXV or fixed orifice** operation if superheat/subcooling readings are abnormal.

Be cautious of any quote that adds refrigerant without a leak search, or replaces a coil without proving airflow is adequate.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil or copper line, weak cooling | Dirty filter / low airflow | Set to fan-only, replace filter, open vents, let thaw | Measure CFM, clean coil, verify airflow |
| Coil refreezes days after thaw | Low refrigerant (leak) | Thaw and stop running cooling | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Water pooling around air handler/furnace | Thawing ice + clogged drain | Vacuum drain line, clear pan | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Coil freezes, blower sounds weak/slow | Failing blower motor or capacitor | Fan-only, don't force cooling | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Freezing when it's cold outside | Running AC below ~60°F | Switch off cooling | Confirm no other fault |
| No airflow, blower won't start | Bad capacitor / motor / control | Reset breaker once only | Diagnose blower circuit |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your Goodman model, refrigerant type, and region change these.

- **Air filter (DIY):** $8–$30.
- **Condensate drain cleaning (DIY):** $0–$25 for a shop vac and tablets.
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90–$200, often credited toward the repair.
- **Blower run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Blower motor (ECM or PSC):** $400–$900 installed; ECM modules run higher.
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-454B costs more than R-410A).
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,600+; more if the system uses phased-out refrigerant and pairing gets complicated.
- **Compressor damage from running frozen:** $1,500–$2,800+ — the reason you should never run an iced system.

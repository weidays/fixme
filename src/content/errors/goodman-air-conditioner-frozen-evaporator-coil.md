## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" is not a fault code your Goodman displays — it is a physical symptom. The evaporator coil (the indoor coil inside your air handler or above your furnace) has dropped below freezing, and moisture from the air has turned to ice on it.

When ice builds up, airflow through the coil collapses, cooling stops, and you may see water dripping from the indoor unit as the ice melts. Left running, a frozen coil can send liquid refrigerant back to the outdoor compressor and cause expensive damage.

The core issue is almost always **too little heat reaching the coil** — either because airflow is restricted, or because the refrigerant charge is off. This page walks through both.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air, so it freezes.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — closed registers, blocked returns, or shut supply grilles reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and grime coat the fins over years, insulating the coil and dropping its temperature.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — a low charge causes the coil to run colder than designed. This is a pro repair, never a DIY top-off.
5. **Failing blower motor or wrong blower speed** — a weak or miswired blower moves too little air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating the system when outdoor temps are below roughly 60°F can drive coil temps below freezing.
7. **Dirty or crushed coil fins / restrictive ductwork** — undersized or collapsed ducts limit airflow across the whole system.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV/orifice)** — a stuck expansion valve overfeeds or underfeeds the coil. This is a technician diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Do not** open the refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any switch.

- **Turn off cooling first.** Set the thermostat to OFF (or HEAT off), then set the fan to **ON**. Running the blower with cooling off is the safe way to melt the ice.
- **Wait for a full thaw.** Give it 1–3 hours. Don't chip or scrape ice off the coil — you can puncture the fins.
- **Check the filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it with a clean one of the same size.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or filters stacked on top.
- **Check the condensate drain.** As ice melts, water must drain. If the pan is overflowing or the drain line is clogged, water will back up — clear the line and check for a tripped float switch.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm both the indoor and outdoor units have power at the breaker panel.
- **After thawing, restart and watch.** If it cools normally and stays ice-free, the filter or airflow was your culprit. If it freezes again, stop and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work through airflow first, then refrigerant:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before taking any readings — measurements on an iced coil are meaningless.
2. **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil and check blower speed settings against Goodman's spec for your model.
3. **Inspect the coil and blower wheel** for dirt buildup.
4. **Attach gauges and take temperature readings** to calculate superheat and subcooling — this tells them whether the charge is correct.
5. **Leak-search if the charge is low** using electronic detectors or dye, rather than just adding refrigerant. A recharge without a leak repair is a temporary fix.
6. **Check the metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** for proper operation.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or an airflow check, ask questions — that's a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil or copper lines | Restricted airflow | Thaw with fan ON, replace filter | Measure static pressure, clean coil |
| Warm air from vents, weak flow | Frozen coil blocking air | Turn off cooling, run fan to thaw | Confirm cause after thaw |
| Water pooling near indoor unit | Melting ice / clogged drain | Clear condensate line, check float switch | Inspect pan and drain slope |
| Coil re-freezes within a day | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Freezing plus loud/weak blower | Failing blower motor | None — call pro | Test motor, verify speed tap |
| Freezing only on cool evenings | Running AC below ~60°F | Avoid running AC in cold weather | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and system size):

- **Air filter replacement** — $0–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing** — $75–$200 if a pro does it.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning** — $150–$450.
- **Blower motor replacement** — $400–$700 (PSC) to $600–$1,200 (ECM/variable-speed).
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis** — $150–$450.
- **Leak repair + recharge** — $500–$1,200+, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A is pricier than legacy R-22 systems).
- **Evaporator coil replacement** — $1,200–$2,800+ if the coil itself leaks and is out of warranty.

If your Goodman is under its parts warranty, the coil or blower motor part may be covered — but you'll still pay labor and refrigerant. Register your unit and keep proof of professional installation to protect the warranty.

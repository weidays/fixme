## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a Goodman fault code — most Goodman condensers and air handlers have no diagnostic display for this. It's a physical condition: the indoor coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) drops below freezing and moisture in the air turns to ice on it.

An evaporator coil freezes for one of two root reasons: **not enough warm air moving across it**, or **not enough refrigerant to absorb heat properly**. Either way, the coil gets too cold, ice builds up, and airflow through the coil chokes off — which makes the freezing worse. Eventually you get warm air from the vents, water leaking around the indoor unit as ice melts, and, if ignored, a damaged compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Starved airflow lets the coil overcool and freeze.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — reduces the air volume across the coil.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and restrict airflow through the fins.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — less refrigerant means lower coil temperature and freezing, even with good airflow.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the fan can't move enough air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below about 60°F outdoors drives coil temps below freezing.
7. **Restricted or plugged condensate drain** — a full pan and stagnant humidity can accelerate icing on some setups.
8. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/piston)** — over- or under-feeding the coil. This is a technician-only diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This is step one. It stops further icing and thaws the coil safely. Give it 1–3 hours (up to 24 if fully iced).
- **Check and replace the air filter.** Hold it to a light — if you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Walk the house and open all supply registers.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed doors aren't starving return airflow.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL and a reasonable temperature, not set colder than the system can handle on a cool day.
- **Check the breaker.** After thawing, verify the AC breaker hasn't tripped.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** If you see standing water or an overflow near the indoor unit, the drain may be clogged — drain tablets can help, but standing ice-melt is normal right after a thaw.
- **Never chip, scrape, or hammer the ice.** You'll puncture the coil and turn a $150 job into a $1,500 one.

If the coil freezes again within a day or two of a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a pro — you likely have a refrigerant or blower problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just add refrigerant blindly. Expect them to:

- Let the coil fully thaw before testing (a frozen system reads inaccurately).
- Measure airflow — check filter, coil cleanliness, blower speed, and static pressure across the coil.
- Attach gauges to read suction/liquid pressures and calculate **superheat and subcooling** to judge the charge and metering device.
- Inspect the blower motor, wheel, and run capacitor.
- Perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or bubble test) if the charge is low — because topping off a leaking system is a temporary, repeat-visit fix.
- Confirm the metering device (TXV or fixed piston) is feeding the coil correctly.

If a tech's first move is to add refrigerant without checking airflow or searching for a leak, be skeptical of the quote.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines / indoor coil, warm air from vents | Restricted airflow or low charge | Filter off cooling, fan ON, replace filter | Measure airflow & charge, find root cause |
| Water leaking around indoor unit | Coil thawing / clogged condensate drain | Thaw fully, clear drain with tablets | Clear/replace drain line, check pan |
| Coil refreezes days after clean filter | Refrigerant leak | None — stop running it | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow at every vent | Dirty coil or failing blower | Replace filter, open vents | Clean coil, test blower motor & capacitor |
| Freezes only on cool days/nights | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Turn off cooling when it's cool out | Verify low-ambient suitability |
| Hissing/bubbling near indoor unit | Refrigerant leak at coil | None | Confirm leak, replace coil if needed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual price varies by region, model, and refrigerant type (older R-410A systems cost more to recharge as supply tightens).

- **New air filter (DIY):** $8–$30
- **Thawing + filter swap (DIY):** $0
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Evaporator coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$400
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Blower motor replacement:** $450–$900 (ECM motors run higher)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Minor leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,200
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $1,000–$2,500+ depending on unit and refrigerant

A repeatedly freezing coil traced to a leak is the most expensive path — which is why the safe checks above are worth doing first.

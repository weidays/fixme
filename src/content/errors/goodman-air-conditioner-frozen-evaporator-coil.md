## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is not a fault code on your Goodman thermostat or control board — it is a physical condition. The indoor coil (the "A-coil" inside your air handler or above your furnace) gets cold enough that condensation on it turns to ice instead of draining away as water.

Once ice forms, it blocks airflow, which makes the coil even colder, which makes more ice. The result is an AC that runs constantly but blows little or no cool air, often with water pooling around the indoor unit as the ice melts.

The two root problems are almost always **not enough airflow across the coil** or **not enough refrigerant in the system**. Both drop the coil temperature below 32°F while the humidity is still condensing on it.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil overcool and freeze.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers, blocked returns, or crushed flex duct starve the coil of airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat pickup, dropping its temperature.
4. **Failing or weak blower motor / wrong fan speed** — a slow blower moves too little air (common on ECM motors losing performance or a misconfigured multi-speed motor).
5. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — undercharge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This is a sealed-system repair, not DIY.
6. **Running AC in cool weather** — operating cooling below roughly 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil on many systems.
7. **Failed metering device (TXV or piston)** — over-feeds or under-feeds the coil, causing freezing.
8. **Clogged condensate drain** — indirectly, a backed-up drain plus high humidity can worsen icing, and the standing water is a symptom you'll notice.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — several fix the problem for $0.

- **Turn the system to OFF (cooling), fan to ON.** Let the coil thaw fully, usually 1–3 hours. Do not run cooling while iced.
- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone resolves many frozen-coil calls.
- **Open closed vents.** Make sure at least most supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or filters stuffed in the return.
- **Check the thermostat.** Set the fan to "Auto" for normal running after thawing, and set cooling to a reasonable temp (not lower than ~68°F). Replace thermostat batteries if applicable.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm both the indoor air handler/furnace breaker and outdoor condenser breaker are on.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the pan or a clogged drain line. A shop vac on the outdoor drain outlet can clear a partial clog. Empty and confirm any condensate pump runs.
- **Look for airflow at the vents after thawing.** Weak airflow everywhere points to a blower or duct problem.

**Do not** chip or scrape ice off the coil, do not add refrigerant yourself, and do not keep cycling a frozen system on and off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the root cause instead of just "adding a pound of Freon." Expect them to:

- **Verify the coil is fully thawed** before testing anything.
- **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil and check blower speed settings and amp draw.
- **Inspect the filter, coil, and blower wheel** for dirt.
- **Connect gauges and take temperatures** — measuring superheat and subcooling to judge whether the charge is correct or the metering device is misbehaving.
- **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or dye if the system is low on refrigerant. Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.
- **Check the TXV or piston** operation and the outdoor coil condition.

If a quote jumps straight to a refrigerant top-off with no leak search and no airflow check, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines / coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents, thaw | Verify airflow/static pressure |
| Water around indoor unit | Melting ice + clogged drain | Thaw, clear condensate drain | Clear drain, inspect pan/float switch |
| Coil freezes again after new filter | Dirty coil or slow blower | Thaw, confirm filter fits snug | Clean coil, test blower motor/speed |
| Warm air, hissing near coil | Low refrigerant / leak | Shut off, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Stop cooling in cold weather | Verify charge and metering device |
| Long run times, never fully cools | Undercharge or bad TXV | Thaw, note symptoms | Measure superheat/subcooling, replace TXV |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $75–$200 by a pro; ~$10 DIY with a shop vac and tablets.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400 depending on access (harder for cased coils).
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors cost more than PSC).
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair:** $200–$1,500+ depending on leak location; a leaking evaporator coil often means coil replacement.
- **TXV (metering valve) replacement:** $400–$800.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,800+, more if it's an older R-410A vs. newer refrigerant matching issue.
- **Refrigerant recharge (after leak repair):** $150–$600 depending on amount and refrigerant type.

If your Goodman system is under the original registered warranty, the coil part may be covered — but labor usually is not.

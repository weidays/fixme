## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code on your Goodman thermostat or control board — it's a physical condition you'll usually spot as ice on the indoor coil, the copper refrigerant lines, or water pooling near the air handler or furnace. Goodman systems don't have a dedicated sensor that flashes a "frozen coil" code, so you diagnose it by symptoms: warm air from the vents, ice you can see or feel, and a system that runs constantly without cooling.

The evaporator coil is the indoor part of your AC where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. It runs cold — but not below freezing under normal operation. When something drops the coil temperature below 32°F, condensation on the coil turns to frost, then a solid block of ice that blocks airflow entirely. Once that happens, the system can't cool at all, and melting ice overflows the drain pan.

This is rated **pro** because the two most common root causes — low refrigerant from a leak and a failing blower motor — require a licensed technician. The thawing and airflow checks are safe DIY.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air, letting it drop below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Undercharged R-410A (or R-32 on newer models) lowers coil pressure and temperature, causing ice even with a clean filter. This is a sealed-system repair.
3. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — Too many closed registers or blocked returns reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor** — A slowing ECM or PSC blower moves too little air across the coil.
5. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Years of dust build an insulating layer that mimics low airflow.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — Operating cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F can freeze the coil; most Goodman units are not rated for low-ambient cooling.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — Less common as a freeze cause, but a plugged drain worsens water damage once ice melts.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan to ON.** At the thermostat, set the system to OFF and the fan to ON. This thaws the coil safely and prevents compressor damage. Wait 1–3 hours (up to 24 for heavy ice).
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A clogged filter is the #1 cause. Swap in a clean one — if the old one is gray and matted, that was likely your problem.
- **Open all supply registers and clear returns.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed vents aren't choking airflow. Keep at least 80% of registers open.
- **Look at the thermostat setting.** Extremely low setpoints (60°F) during mild weather can overwork the system into a freeze.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** After thawing, confirm the drain line is clear and the pan isn't overflowing. Drain tablets help prevent algae clogs.
- **Verify the breaker.** If you shut the unit down at the breaker to let it thaw, confirm you've reset it correctly afterward.

Do **not** chip, scrape, or pour hot water on the ice, and do not open the sealed refrigerant system. If the coil re-freezes within a day of a clean filter and open vents, stop DIY and call a pro — that points to a leak or blower fault.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should confirm the freeze cause before quoting parts. Expect them to:

- **Confirm airflow first** — Measure static pressure and check the filter, blower speed, and coil cleanliness. A good tech rules out airflow before touching refrigerant.
- **Attach gauges** — Read suction and head pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. Low readings indicate undercharge or a leak.
- **Leak search** — If low on refrigerant, use an electronic detector or dye to find the leak. Simply "topping off" without finding the leak is a red flag.
- **Test the blower motor** — Verify the ECM or PSC motor spins up to spec and check the capacitor if it's a PSC unit.
- **Inspect the coil and TXV/metering device** — A stuck thermostatic expansion valve or restricted metering device can also cause icing.

Sanity check: a legitimate quote should name the *root cause* (e.g. "leak at the evaporator coil braze joint"), not just "add refrigerant."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm air from vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Set fan ON to thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, clean coil |
| Coil re-freezes days after clean filter | Refrigerant leak | Stop running unit, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow at every vent | Failing blower motor | Check filter and returns | Test/replace ECM or PSC motor & capacitor |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Melting ice + clogged drain | Clear drain, add tablets after thaw | Flush drain line, check pan/float switch |
| Ice only in cool weather | Low-ambient operation | Don't run AC below ~60°F outdoors | Advise low-ambient kit if needed |
| Fuzzy gray buildup on coil | Dirty evaporator coil | None (coil access needed) | Professional coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor):

- **Air filter replacement** — $10–$40 DIY. Zero if you already have spares.
- **Condensate drain clearing** — $0 DIY with a wet/dry vac; $75–$200 professionally.
- **Professional coil cleaning** — $150–$400 depending on access.
- **Refrigerant leak search** — $150–$450 as a standalone diagnostic.
- **Refrigerant recharge** — $200–$700 depending on R-410A/R-32 pricing and how much is added (recharging without repairing the leak is not a real fix).
- **Leak repair (line or joint)** — $500–$1,200 including recharge.
- **Evaporator coil replacement** — $900–$1,500+ installed; higher if the coil is under warranty on parts but you still pay labor.
- **Blower motor** — $300–$600 for a PSC motor; $600–$1,100 for an ECM module or full motor.
- **Blower capacitor (PSC units)** — $150–$300.

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type, and whether your Goodman is still under its parts warranty (many carry 10-year registered coverage — labor is usually separate).

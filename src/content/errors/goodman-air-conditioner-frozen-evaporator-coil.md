## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners don't display a specific "frozen evaporator coil" fault code — this is a **physical condition**, not a control-board error. You'll notice it as reduced or no cold air, visible frost or ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, and often water pooling near the indoor unit once it thaws.

The evaporator coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet, model numbers like CAPF, CHPF, or CAPT) is where warm indoor air passes over cold refrigerant. When the coil surface drops below 32°F for too long, humidity condenses and freezes into ice. Ice blocks airflow, which makes the coil even colder — a runaway loop that ends with a solid block of ice and zero cooling.

Two root categories cause it: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (airflow problem) or **refrigerant running too cold or too low** (refrigerant problem). Because diagnosing and correcting a refrigerant issue requires EPA-certified handling, this is rated **pro-level** once basic airflow checks are ruled out.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil of warm air, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns reduce airflow just like a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil fins and cut heat transfer.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — low pressure makes the coil run colder than designed. Requires a certified tech.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the indoor fan isn't moving enough air.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below ~60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil.
7. **Closed or stuck blower door / dirty blower wheel** — reduces total airflow through the system.
8. **Metering device (TXV or piston) fault** — overfeeds cold refrigerant to one part of the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — several fix the problem outright for $0.

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to *Cool → Off* and the fan to *On*. This thaws the coil safely while you inspect. Never run the compressor with an iced coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A gray, matted, or dusty filter is the most common culprit. Swap in a clean MERV 8 filter.
- **Open all supply registers and clear returns.** Make sure no rugs, furniture, or closed dampers are blocking airflow. Aim to keep the vast majority of vents open.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm both the indoor and outdoor units have power — a tripped breaker can leave the fan off.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** A frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it thaws. Clear the drain line with a wet/dry vac and drop in a drain tablet to prevent overflow and water damage.
- **Verify outdoor temperature.** If it's below ~60°F outside, the AC isn't meant to run in cooling mode.

If the coil refreezes after a clean filter and open vents, or you see oily residue near the coil or lines, stop and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech works from cheap to expensive, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the coil is thawed** before any pressure readings — frozen coils give false numbers.
2. **Measure airflow / temperature split.** They check the temperature difference across the coil (should be roughly 16–22°F) and inspect the blower motor, wheel, and filter.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup and check the blower capacitor and motor amp draw.
4. **Attach gauges and read superheat/subcooling.** This tells them whether the refrigerant charge is correct, low, or the metering device (TXV/piston) is malfunctioning.
5. **Leak search** if the charge is low — with electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test. A recharge without finding the leak is a red flag; the ice will return.
6. **Verify repair** by running the system and re-checking the temperature split and coil temperature.

Be wary of any tech who "just adds refrigerant" without a leak search — refrigerant doesn't get used up; if it's low, it's leaking somewhere.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter | Replace filter, thaw coil | Confirm airflow, clean coil |
| Coil freezes again after new filter | Dirty coil or low refrigerant | Verify all vents open | Coil cleaning or leak search + recharge |
| Water leaking near air handler | Thawed coil overflowing pan | Clear drain, vac drain line | Clear blockage, check drain slope |
| Ice on outdoor line (large copper) | Low refrigerant / TXV fault | Thaw and stop running | Superheat test, leak repair |
| Fan runs weak or won't start | Bad blower capacitor/motor | Check breaker | Test/replace capacitor or motor |
| Freezes only in mild weather | Running AC when too cold out | Switch off below 60°F | Confirm no other fault |
| Oily film near coil or lines | Refrigerant leak | Do not run; call pro | Electronic/UV leak detection |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and tonnage):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor replacement (ECM):** $450–$900
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$350
- **Refrigerant recharge (R-410A):** $200–$700 depending on amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$750
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $900–$1,500+
- **Full coil + refrigerant on older systems:** can approach $2,000+

If your system is old and out of warranty and needs a coil plus refrigerant work, ask about replacement — the repair can be more than half the cost of a new system.

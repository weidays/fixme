## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners don't display a specific "frozen evaporator coil" error code the way a furnace board flashes a fault. Instead, this is a **symptom-based problem**: ice or frost builds up on the indoor evaporator (A-coil), on the copper suction line, or at the outdoor unit. You'll notice weak or no cold air, higher humidity indoors, water pooling near the indoor unit as ice melts, and sometimes a hissing or bubbling sound.

A frozen coil is a **downstream symptom, not the root cause**. Something is making the coil colder than 32°F while moisture from the air condenses on it and freezes. The two big drivers are **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant** (a leak or charge problem). Either way, running the system frozen risks liquid refrigerant returning to the compressor and causing damage — which is why this is rated a pro-level issue.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops the coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked return or supply airflow** — closed/covered vents, collapsed flex duct, or a dirty blower wheel starving the coil of warm air.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust caked on the fins insulates the coil and reduces heat pickup.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — very common second cause. Low pressure drops the coil's boiling temperature well below freezing.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / wrong blower speed tap** — the coil gets cold air but not enough volume.
6. **Thermostat set too low / running in very cool weather** — running cooling below about 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or failing TXV / metering device** — meters too little refrigerant into the coil.
8. **Clogged condensate drain causing a safety float switch trip** — not a freeze itself, but often discovered while investigating.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Turn cooling OFF first and let the ice fully thaw** before testing.

- **Set the thermostat mode to OFF and the fan to ON.** Running just the blower melts the ice safely in 1–3 hours without straining the compressor.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A gray, matted filter is the #1 cause. Slide in a clean one of the correct size.
- **Confirm supply and return vents are open and unblocked** — no rugs, furniture, or closed dampers restricting airflow.
- **Check the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and the outdoor unit; a tripped blower breaker can stop airflow while the coil keeps cooling briefly.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan** for standing water or overflow. Clear the drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination, and consider drain tablets.
- **After thawing, restart cooling and watch.** If frost returns within a day, stop running it and call a pro.

Do **not** open the refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bend the coil aggressively, or bypass the condensate float switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just add refrigerant. Expect them to:

- **Confirm the coil has fully thawed** and inspect for water damage or drain issues.
- **Measure static pressure and airflow (CFM)** across the coil, and check the blower speed tap and blower wheel cleanliness.
- **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup and bent fins.
- **Attach gauges and read pressures, then calculate superheat and subcooling** — this tells them whether the system is undercharged, overcharged, or has a metering problem.
- **Leak-check** with electronic detector or UV dye if the charge is low, so they fix the leak rather than just topping off refrigerant.
- **Verify the metering device (TXV or piston)** is feeding the coil correctly.

If a quote jumps straight to "just needs refrigerant" without a leak search or airflow check, get a second opinion — low charge without a leak diagnosis usually means you'll be paying again next season.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Turn off, thaw, replace filter, open vents | Measure airflow, clean blower wheel |
| Frost on copper suction line at outdoor unit | Low refrigerant / airflow | Thaw system, check filter | Gauge readings, leak search, repair & recharge |
| Coil refreezes after new filter | Refrigerant leak or bad blower | Stop running, call pro | Superheat/subcooling test, leak check |
| Water pooling near air handler | Ice melting or clogged drain | Clear drain with wet/dry vac | Fix drain slope, verify float switch |
| No airflow at vents at all | Blower motor / breaker | Check breaker, thaw | Test blower motor, capacitor, control board |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Stop cooling in cool weather | Confirm no low-ambient control needed |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; regional variation is wide):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180
- **Coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor (ECM or PSC):** $400–$900
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500 (higher if the coil is out of warranty)

Goodman coils often carry a **10-year parts warranty** if registered — confirm your serial number and registration before paying for a new coil, as that can cut the cost significantly.

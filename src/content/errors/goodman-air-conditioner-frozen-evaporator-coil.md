## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a fault code — Goodman air conditioners and heat pumps don't display an error for it. It's a physical condition you'll notice as poor or no cooling, a block of ice on the indoor coil or the copper lines near your furnace/air handler, and sometimes water on the floor as it melts.

The evaporator coil freezes when the refrigerant inside it gets too cold and the coil surface drops below 32°F. Moisture from your indoor air then condenses and freezes into ice. That ice blocks airflow, which makes the problem worse in a runaway cycle until the coil is a solid block.

Two broad conditions cause this: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant / wrong pressures** (a refrigerant problem). Airflow issues are far more common and often DIY-fixable. Refrigerant issues require an EPA-certified technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm airflow and it freezes.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers, blocked returns, or furniture over vents chokes airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm buildup on the coil insulates it and reduces heat transfer.
4. **Failed or slow blower motor / wrong blower speed** — a weak ECM or PSC blower, or a bad capacitor, moves too little air.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — low pressure lowers coil temperature and causes icing. Requires a certified tech; refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means a leak.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — indirectly, a water backup on a coil that's already cold can accelerate icing (and cause water damage).
8. **Metering device (TXV/piston) fault** — a stuck valve underfeeds or overfeeds the coil. Diagnosis and repair are a tech job.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system **off** — never run a frozen system.

- **Turn the thermostat to OFF or FAN ONLY.** Let the coil thaw completely (1–24 hours). Running FAN speeds this up.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of freeze-ups.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and returns aren't covered by rugs, furniture, or filters stacked on top of each other.
- **Check the breaker.** After thawing, confirm the AC and air handler breakers are on and not tripped.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** Clear standing water; if you have a wet/dry vac, you can suck out the drain line at the outdoor termination. Drop a drain tablet in the pan.
- **Confirm outdoor temperature.** Don't run the AC when it's below about 60°F outside.
- **Feel the copper lines** near the indoor unit after thawing. If they re-frost within an hour of running with a clean filter, stop and call a pro — that points to refrigerant or blower issues.

If it re-freezes after a clean filter and clear vents, you have a deeper cause. Stop running it and book a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just add refrigerant. Expect them to:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing.
- Inspect and measure **airflow** — filter, coil cleanliness, blower speed, and static pressure across the system.
- Check the **blower motor and capacitor** for correct operation and speed.
- Connect gauges to read **suction and head pressures**, then calculate **superheat and subcooling** against Goodman's spec for your model. This tells them whether the charge is correct or the metering device is faulty.
- If charge is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen/soap) rather than just topping off. Adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a stopgap, not a fix.
- Inspect the **TXV or piston** if pressures point to a metering issue.

If a quote jumps straight to "you're low on refrigerant, we added some" with no leak search and no airflow check, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, weak/no cooling | Dirty air filter | Replace filter, thaw, run FAN | — |
| Freezes again after clean filter | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw, verify vents open | Professional coil cleaning |
| Warm air, coil re-frosts fast | Low refrigerant (leak) | Shut off, thaw | Leak search + repair + recharge |
| Weak airflow from vents | Failing blower / bad capacitor | Check breaker after thaw | Test/replace blower or capacitor |
| Water on floor near furnace | Melting ice + clogged drain | Clear drain, vac line, tablets | Clear/repair condensate system |
| Freezes on cool days only | Running AC below ~60°F | Turn off AC in cold weather | Verify low-ambient controls |
| Some rooms hot, coil ices | Too many vents closed | Open registers and returns | Assess duct/airflow balance |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing / tablets:** $0–$40 DIY; $75–$200 for a service call.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor replacement (ECM):** $400–$900+.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100–$350 (sometimes credited toward the repair).
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $150–$600+ depending on refrigerant type and amount. Older R-410A systems cost more as supply tightens.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,500+, sometimes more if it's under warranty on parts but not labor. Confirm your Goodman registered warranty.

Most airflow-driven freeze-ups are resolved for under $250. Refrigerant and coil repairs are where costs climb.

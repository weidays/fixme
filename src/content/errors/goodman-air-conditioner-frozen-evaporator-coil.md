## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is not a specific error code on Goodman equipment—it's a physical condition your system displays. The evaporator coil sits inside your indoor air handler or furnace cabinet. When it gets colder than 32°F, the moisture condensing on it freezes into a layer of ice.

Once ice forms, airflow across the coil drops, which makes the coil even colder, which makes more ice. It's a runaway problem. You'll notice weak or no airflow from your vents, warm air even though the system is "running," and often water pooling around the indoor unit once the ice starts to melt.

The two big root causes are **not enough air moving across the coil** and **not enough refrigerant in the system**. Both drive the coil temperature below freezing. Figuring out which one is happening is the whole diagnosis.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow and drops its temperature below freezing.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and block air, same effect as a dirty filter.
3. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — Reduces total airflow enough to freeze the coil, especially in zoned homes.
4. **Failing or slow blower motor / wheel packed with debris** — Less air moved means colder coil. Some Goodman variable-speed (ECM) blowers will ramp but still under-deliver if the wheel is caked.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This requires an EPA-certified tech; it is never a top-down first guess.
6. **Running the AC when outdoor temps are too low** — Cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor can freeze the coil. Common in early spring or with over-aggressive night settings.
7. **Failed metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** — A stuck expansion valve floods or starves the coil and can cause icing. Model-dependent; newer Goodman units mostly use TXVs.
8. **Blower not running while compressor runs** — A wiring, relay, or control board fault that lets the compressor cool an idle coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Do not** open the refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

- **Turn off cooling first.** Set the thermostat to **OFF for cooling** but set the **fan to ON**. This runs the blower and thaws the coil without making more ice. Give it 1–3 hours (up to 24 if heavily iced).
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Walk the house and open supply vents and return grilles.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't strangling airflow. Keep at least 80% of vents open.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to a reasonable temperature (not 60°F) and that the fan setting is correct.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm both the indoor and outdoor units have power at the panel.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** As the coil thaws, a lot of water will flow. Make sure the drain line is clear and the pan isn't overflowing. Drain tablets help prevent future clogs.
- **After a full thaw**, restart cooling and watch it. If it re-freezes within a day, stop and call a pro—that points to a coil, blower, or refrigerant problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech works airflow-first, refrigerant-second:

1. **Confirms the coil is fully thawed** before testing—you can't measure a frozen system accurately.
2. **Measures airflow / temperature split.** Return-vs-supply temperature difference (should be roughly 16–22°F). A high split with weak flow points to airflow restriction; a low split points to charge or metering issues.
3. **Inspects the filter, blower wheel, and evaporator coil** for dirt or debris and checks blower motor operation and amp draw.
4. **Attaches gauges and takes superheat/subcooling readings.** These numbers tell them whether the charge is low, the metering device is faulty, or the system is fine. This is the step only a certified tech should do.
5. **Leak searches if the charge is low**—with electronic detector or dye. A pro won't just "top off" refrigerant without finding the leak; that's a red flag in a quote.
6. **Checks control wiring** to confirm the blower runs whenever the compressor does.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without airflow checks or a leak search, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, ice on copper lines | Dirty filter or coil | Replace filter, set fan to ON to thaw | Deep-clean coil, verify airflow |
| Warm air from vents, system "running" | Frozen coil / low airflow | Thaw with fan-only, replace filter | Measure temp split, inspect blower |
| Water around indoor unit | Coil thawing, drain clogged | Clear drain line, use drain tablets | Confirm no chronic freeze cause |
| Re-freezes within a day of thawing | Low refrigerant or bad metering device | Stop cooling, call pro | Gauge readings, leak search, TXV check |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Low charge or low outdoor temp cooling | Raise night setpoint, stop overnight cooling | Verify charge and outdoor lockout |
| Blower doesn't spin with compressor | Relay, board, or wiring fault | None—call pro | Test blower, relay, control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Simple airflow fixes are cheap; refrigerant and coil work are not.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Thaw + diagnostic service call:** $75–$200.
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$450 depending on access.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM/variable-speed motors run higher).
- **Blower wheel cleaning:** $150–$350.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900.
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant amount.
- **Full evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$1,600+, sometimes more if the coil is out of warranty or hard to access.

Goodman's registered warranties often cover parts (including coils and compressors) for the original owner—labor is usually not covered. Ask the tech to check your unit's warranty status by serial number before you pay for a coil.

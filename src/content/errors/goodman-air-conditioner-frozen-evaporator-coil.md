## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners don't flash a dedicated "frozen coil" fault, so there's no code to read on a control board. Instead, this is a **physical condition**: the indoor evaporator coil (inside your air handler or above your furnace) has dropped below freezing and accumulated ice.

That ice acts like a blanket. Once the coil is coated, it can no longer absorb heat from your home's air, so you get **little or no cooling** even though the outdoor unit is running. As the ice melts, you may also see water pooling around the air handler or overflowing the condensate pan.

Two things cause a coil to freeze: **not enough warm air moving across it**, or **not enough refrigerant flowing through it**. Everything below sorts into one of those two buckets.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of the warm return air it needs to stay above freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed airflow** — too many closed supply registers, collapsed flex duct, or a dirty blower wheel reduce airflow across the coil.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil fins, mimicking low airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — a leak lowers pressure at the coil, dropping its temperature below freezing. This is the most common *non-airflow* cause and requires a tech.
5. **Failing blower motor or capacitor** — a weak or dead ECM/PSC blower moves too little air. On variable-speed Goodman air handlers (AVPTC/AWUF), an airflow fault can cause this.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — running cooling below ~60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain (secondary effect)** — a backed-up drain plus a marginal system can worsen icing and cause water damage as it thaws.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV/orifice)** — a stuck expansion valve underfeeds the coil. Diagnosis and repair are tech-only.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **First, turn the thermostat to OFF for cooling and set the fan to ON** — this stops the freezing and speeds thawing.

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Supply registers** — open any closed vents and clear furniture, rugs, or drapes blocking them. Restore airflow throughout the house.
- **Return grille** — make sure the large return-air grille isn't blocked or clogged.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to COOL with a temperature well below room temp, and swap batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker** — confirm both the indoor air-handler/furnace breaker and the outdoor condenser breaker are ON.
- **Condensate drain and pan** — check for standing water. If the pan is full, the drain may be clogged; a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain line can clear a simple blockage.
- **Let it fully thaw** — wait until *all* ice is gone (3–24 hours) before restarting cooling. Don't chip at the ice.

If the coil freezes again within a day of a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a pro — that points to refrigerant or airflow hardware.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just add refrigerant. Expect them to:

1. Confirm the coil is fully thawed, then run the system and measure **temperature split** (return vs. supply air) — should be roughly 16–22°F.
2. Check **airflow**: inspect filter, blower wheel, blower motor amps, and static pressure across the coil.
3. Attach gauges to read **suction and head pressure** and calculate **superheat/subcooling** to see if the charge is correct — this distinguishes an airflow problem from a refrigerant problem.
4. If charge is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just topping off.
5. Inspect the **TXV/metering device** and evaporator coil condition.

A quote that jumps straight to "you need refrigerant" without checking airflow and finding a leak is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil/lineset, weak cooling | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents, thaw fully | Check static pressure, blower amps |
| Refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Shut cooling off, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice + water around air handler | Frozen coil melting + clogged drain | Vac the drain line, thaw | Clear drain, verify pan/float switch |
| Coil freezes only at night/cool days | Running AC when too cold out | Don't cool below ~60°F outdoor | Confirm system operating limits |
| Warm air, blower barely blowing | Weak blower motor/capacitor | Verify breaker on, then call pro | Test/replace blower motor or cap |
| Dirty coil visible, poor airflow | Insulating dust on coil | No-rinse coil cleaner (if accessible) | Deep coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and system):

- **New air filter / thaw:** $0–$40 DIY
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Evaporator coil professional cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement (PSC or ECM):** $400–$900 (variable-speed modules run higher)
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ (R-410A costs vary; newer R-454B systems differ)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$1,000

If the system is aging and the coil leaks, ask whether coil replacement or full replacement makes more financial sense — especially with the R-410A-to-R-454B refrigerant transition.

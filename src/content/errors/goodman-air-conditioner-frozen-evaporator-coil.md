## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a fault code Goodman displays on a board — it's a physical condition. Ice forms on the indoor evaporator coil (the A-shaped coil in your air handler or above your furnace) when the coil surface drops below 32°F. Instead of cooling your air, the coil turns into a block of ice, and you'll notice weak or warm airflow, water around the air handler, or the system running nonstop without cooling.

The root cause is almost always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (airflow problem), or **not enough refrigerant** (charge or leak problem). Both make the coil too cold, and moisture in your air freezes onto it.

This is filed as **pro severity** because while the *thawing* is DIY, the underlying cause frequently requires a technician — especially anything involving refrigerant, which is illegal for homeowners to handle without EPA certification.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the number-one cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow, dropping its temperature until it freezes.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — closed registers, furniture over vents, or crushed ductwork reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow through its fins.
4. **Low refrigerant charge or a leak** — low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. Common on older units or after a slow leak develops.
5. **Failing blower motor or wrong fan speed** — a weak or wrongly-configured indoor blower (common after a board or motor swap) doesn't move enough air.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — using cooling when it's below ~62°F outdoors can freeze the coil even on a healthy system.
7. **Thermostat set too low / running constantly** — extended runtime with marginal airflow tips a borderline system into icing.
8. **TXV or metering device failure** — a stuck expansion valve overfeeds or underfeeds the coil (less common, needs a tech).

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these only after **turning cooling OFF and letting the coil fully thaw** (set the thermostat to Off, fan to ON to speed melting; place towels to catch drips):

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Supply and return vents:** Open every register, move furniture and rugs off vents, and make sure return grilles aren't blocked.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's not set unreasonably low, and that the fan setting is correct. Don't run cooling if outdoor temps are below ~62°F.
- **Breaker:** After thawing, confirm the air-handler and condenser breakers are on. Don't repeatedly flip breakers to "reset."
- **Condensate drain:** A thawed coil dumps a lot of water. Check the drain pan and line for standing water or overflow, and clear the line with a wet/dry vac if it's backed up.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure the condenser fan spins and the coil isn't buried in leaves or debris.

If the coil re-freezes after a fresh filter and open vents, stop running it and call a pro — you likely have a refrigerant or airflow-mechanical issue.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will:

1. **Confirm airflow first** — inspect the filter, coil cleanliness, blower operation, and static pressure across the system before touching refrigerant.
2. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt, biofilm, or bent fins.
3. **Check blower speed/configuration**, especially on ECM motors, which are dip-switch or board-programmed on many Goodman air handlers.
4. **Attach gauges** to read suction pressure and superheat/subcooling, and measure the temperature split across the coil.
5. **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low — recharging without finding the leak just delays the same failure.
6. **Verify the metering device (TXV/piston)** if pressures point to it.

Sanity check: any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without first checking your filter, coil cleanliness, and blower is cutting corners. Refrigerant should only be added *after* a leak search when charge is confirmed low.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, ice on indoor coil/lines | Dirty filter | Replace filter, thaw fully | Verify airflow, check static pressure |
| Some rooms warm, ice at air handler | Blocked/closed vents | Open all registers, clear obstructions | Inspect ductwork for collapse/leaks |
| Recurs after new filter | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw, then call pro | Deep-clean coil, check fins |
| Ice + hissing/bubbling, poor cooling | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw, shut off, call pro | Leak search, repair, evacuate & recharge |
| Blower runs weak or not at all | Blower motor / wrong speed | Confirm fan set to ON | Test motor, correct ECM configuration |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~62°F | Don't run cooling in cool temps | Verify low-ambient suitability |
| Water overflow after thaw | Clogged condensate line | Clear line with wet/dry vac | Clear drain, add pan safety switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10 – $40
- **Thaw + restart (DIY):** $0
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75 – $250
- **Evaporator coil cleaning (professional):** $150 – $450
- **Blower motor replacement (ECM):** $400 – $900
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150 – $450
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $400 – $1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant amount (R-410A/R-32 pricing varies)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350 – $750
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900 – $2,500+

Note: if your Goodman system is 10+ years old and needs a coil or major refrigerant repair, ask your tech to compare repair cost against replacement — matched-system economics often favor replacing at that point.

## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a specific "frozen evaporator coil" fault code — this is a physical condition, not a control-board error. You'll notice it as ice or frost on the indoor coil, on the copper refrigerant lines near the air handler or furnace, or a solid block of frost at the outdoor unit's service valves.

The evaporator coil freezes when it gets too cold and moisture in the air condenses and freezes on it instead of draining away. Two things cause that: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant pressure** to keep the coil above freezing. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, cooling drops off, and the problem snowballs.

This is rated **pro** because the two most damaging causes — low refrigerant and blower failure — require licensed diagnosis, and running a frozen unit risks a compressor failure. You can safely handle the airflow-related causes yourself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too-few supply/return vents** — reduces the air volume crossing the coil.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust buildup on the fins insulates the coil and blocks airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — lowers coil pressure and temperature. Common on older Goodman systems. Requires a technician.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the indoor fan isn't moving enough air. Varies by model (PSC vs ECM motors).
6. **Clogged condensate drain** — a full drain pan or backed-up line can trigger a safety float switch or contribute to ice/water problems.
7. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~62°F outside) — the coil freezes because ambient air is already cool.
8. **Stuck or incorrectly set thermostat** — extended run times with poor airflow.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system **off** first if the coil is already iced:

- **Turn the AC off, fan to On.** Set the thermostat to Off (or Cool set high) and the fan switch to On. This melts the ice without running the compressor. Wait until all ice is gone — 2 to 8 hours.
- **Replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the number-one cause. Check the size printed on the frame and swap in a clean one.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and returns aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed doors. Keeping too many rooms closed off starves the system of return air.
- **Check the thermostat batteries and settings.** Confirm Cool mode and a reasonable setpoint (don't set it below 68°F).
- **Check the breaker.** If the unit tripped, that's a sign of an electrical problem — reset once, and if it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** Look for standing water under the air handler. You can gently clear the drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination and add drain tablets.

If the coil freezes again within a day of a clean filter and open vents, the cause is internal — refrigerant, blower, or coil. Stop running it and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just "add refrigerant." Here's what a proper diagnosis looks like:

1. **Confirm airflow.** They measure static pressure and check the blower speed, filter, and coil cleanliness first — many "low charge" claims are actually airflow problems.
2. **Attach gauges and read pressures.** They compare suction/head pressure and superheat/subcooling against Goodman's spec for your model and refrigerant (R-410A on most units, R-32 on newer ones).
3. **Leak search if charge is low.** Refrigerant doesn't get "used up" — if it's low, there's a leak. They'll use electronic detectors or dye to find it before recharging. Recharging without fixing a leak is a red flag.
4. **Test the blower motor and capacitor** with a meter, and inspect the ECM module on variable-speed models.
5. **Check the condensate float switch and drain** to rule out a safety cutoff.

Sanity check: if a tech quotes a recharge without mentioning a leak search or airflow test, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak cooling | Dirty filter / low airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter, open vents | Static pressure test; coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 DIY.
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY (shop vac + tablets) to $75–$200 professional.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Blower motor (PSC):** $400–$700. **ECM/variable-speed motor:** $600–$1,200.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A and R-32 pricing differ).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ if the coil itself is leaking.
- **Compressor replacement** (if the unit was run frozen and burned out): $1,500–$2,800+ — the reason to shut a frozen unit down promptly.

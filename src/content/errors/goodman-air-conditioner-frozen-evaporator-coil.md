## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" isn't a Goodman fault code — it's a physical condition your air conditioner (or heat pump in cooling mode) develops when the indoor coil gets cold enough for condensation to turn to ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, cooling gets worse, and the problem snowballs until the whole coil is a block of frost.

On Goodman split systems the evaporator coil sits in the cased coil above (or below) the furnace/air handler. When it freezes, you'll typically notice weak or no cool air, water pooling around the indoor unit as the ice melts, and sometimes visible frost on the copper refrigerant line outside.

The root cause is always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant** (a leak or charge problem). This page is marked **pro** because most durable fixes — refrigerant leaks, blower failures, metering device faults — require a licensed technician with gauges and EPA certification.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air, so it drops below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — an undercharged system runs the coil too cold. Recurring freezing after cleaning points here.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the fins insulate the coil and cut airflow, just like a dirty filter.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / wheel** — a slow or dirty blower wheel moves too little air across the coil.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — restricting airflow throughout the house.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating cooling below about 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **Faulty metering device (TXV or piston)** — over-feeds or under-feeds the coil. Less common but a real culprit.
8. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — occasionally contributes and always makes the water mess worse.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, handle refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat system to Off and the fan to On. This thaws the coil safely. Give it several hours — up to a day for heavy ice.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the number-one cause. Swap in a clean one and note how dirty the old one was.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint, and replace batteries if it's battery-powered and showing a low icon.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm the AC and air handler/furnace breakers are on. A tripped breaker can leave the blower off while the coil freezes.
- **Open all supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't choking airflow. Leave interior doors open.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** Look for standing water or overflow. Clear a visible clog at the pan and confirm the drain line is dripping outside when running.
- **Look at the outdoor line.** Frost on the larger copper line at the outdoor unit confirms a coil freeze upstream.

If the coil re-freezes within a day or two of a clean filter and full thaw, stop running cooling and call a pro — you likely have a refrigerant or airflow fault that needs tools.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Goodman tech will work the airflow-vs-refrigerant fork methodically:

1. **Confirm and complete the thaw** so readings are valid — you can't diagnose a solid block of ice.
2. **Inspect airflow first:** filter, coil cleanliness, blower wheel condition, and static pressure across the system.
3. **Attach gauges** to read suction and head pressures and calculate **superheat and subcooling** — the definitive test for refrigerant charge.
4. **Leak search** if charge is low: electronic sniffer, UV dye, or bubble test at joints and the coil. Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a temporary and non-compliant fix.
5. **Test the blower motor** amp draw and speed tap, and check the metering device (TXV bulb, piston sizing).
6. **Verify the condensate path** and float switch operation.

Sanity check: any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or an airflow inspection is cutting corners. Refrigerant loss means there's a leak somewhere.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak air + ice on indoor coil | Dirty filter / airflow | Replace filter, thaw with fan on | Clean coil, check static pressure |
| Coil refreezes days after thaw | Low refrigerant / leak | Stop cooling, call pro | Gauge readings, leak search, recharge |
| Frost on outdoor copper line | Undercharge or airflow | Thaw fully, check filter | Superheat/subcooling test |
| Water pooling at air handler | Melting ice / drain clog | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, check float switch |
| Low airflow at every vent | Weak/dirty blower wheel | Confirm vents open, breaker on | Test blower motor, clean wheel |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Switch to fan/heat mode | Confirm no other fault present |
| Freezes with clean filter | Metering device (TXV) | Call pro | Test/replace TXV or piston |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Your area and model affect the total.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Thaw + system check (service call):** $90–$180.
- **Evaporator coil deep cleaning:** $200–$500.
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$300.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900.
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A).
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,600+; more if it's under a sealed-system warranty claim with labor.
- **Compressor damage from running frozen:** $1,500–$2,800+ — the reason you shut cooling off early.

Systems still using older R-22 refrigerant are far more expensive to recharge; on those, replacement often makes more economic sense than a big leak repair.

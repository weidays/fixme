## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a "frozen evaporator coil" fault code. This is a **physical symptom**, not a board message: ice builds up on the indoor evaporator coil (inside your furnace or air handler) and often creeps back onto the copper refrigerant line.

A coil freezes when it gets **colder than it should while too little heat reaches it**. That happens two ways: not enough warm indoor air is moving across the coil (an airflow problem), or the refrigerant pressure inside the coil has dropped too low (a refrigerant problem). Either way, condensation on the coil freezes instead of draining away, and the ice snowballs.

Once ice forms, cooling collapses — the system runs constantly but the house stays warm, and you may see water pooling near the indoor unit as the ice melts.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil of warm air and it ices over.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers, blocked returns, or a collapsed flex duct starve airflow the same way.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil so it can't shed cold, and they choke airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — a leak drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. This is the most common *serious* cause and requires a technician.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the fan can't move enough air across the coil.
6. **Clogged condensate drain causing a float switch trip / cycling** — some setups short-cycle and never move enough air.
7. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating below roughly 60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil on many systems.
8. **Thermostat set very low with an oversized system** — extended low-temperature runtime encourages icing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — several fix the problem for free.

- **Turn the system to thaw mode.** Set the thermostat to **OFF (or Cool off)** and the **fan to ON**. Let the blower run to melt the ice before you troubleshoot or restart. Do not chip or scrape the ice.
- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone resolves many frozen coils.
- **Open your vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or filters stacked on top.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool, at a reasonable setpoint (not, say, 62°F), and swap the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breaker.** A tripped breaker on the air handler or furnace can stop the blower while the outdoor unit keeps trying. Reset once; if it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan or a full float switch. A clogged drain line can be cleared with a shop vac at the outdoor termination, but if it keeps clogging, have it serviced.

If the coil ices up again within a day or two after all of these check out, the cause is almost certainly refrigerant or the blower — call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the *category* of problem before quoting parts:

1. **Thaws the coil fully** and inspects the filter, blower wheel, and coil face for dirt.
2. **Measures airflow** — checks blower speed/amps, capacitor, and static pressure across the coil to rule out an airflow restriction.
3. **Connects refrigerant gauges** and measures superheat and subcooling. Low suction pressure with high superheat points to undercharge/leak.
4. **Performs a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or bubble test) if the charge is low — this is where the real cost lives.
5. **Inspects the metering device** (TXV or piston) which, if stuck, mimics low charge.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search is a red flag — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so if it's low, there's a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, gray filter | Clogged filter | Replace filter, run fan to thaw | Confirm airflow restored |
| Ice returns after new filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow at all vents | Dirty coil or blower | Thaw, inspect filter | Clean coil, service blower/capacitor |
| Water around indoor unit | Melting ice / clogged drain | Clear drain, run fan | Clear line, check float switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC too cold outside | Don't run AC below ~60°F | Advise on low-ambient controls |
| Outdoor unit runs, house warm | Coil iced, compressor at risk | Shut off now, thaw | Full charge & airflow diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Vent/duct correction:** $0 DIY to $300+ if ductwork is modified.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Blower capacitor:** $150–$400. **Blower motor:** $450–$900.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450.
- **Recharge (refrigerant):** $200–$700+, depending on system and refrigerant type (R-410A vs newer blends).
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $900–$1,800+; higher if the coil is under warranty on parts but you pay labor, or if the whole cased coil is replaced.

If a Goodman coil is still under the registered parts warranty (often 10 years to the original owner), you typically pay labor only — confirm registration and dates.

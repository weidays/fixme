## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners don't display a numeric fault code for a frozen evaporator coil — this is a **physical symptom**, not a board error. The evaporator coil is the indoor coil inside your air handler or furnace cabinet. When it ices over, you'll see frost or a solid block of ice on the copper coil, on the refrigerant lines, or dripping water where ice has melted.

A coil freezes when its surface temperature drops below 32°F. That happens for one of two reasons: **not enough warm indoor air is moving across the coil**, or **too little refrigerant is inside it** (which causes the pressure and temperature to drop abnormally low). Either way, the ice blocks airflow, cooling collapses, and — most dangerously — liquid refrigerant can return to the compressor and damage it.

This is rated **pro** severity because the two most common root causes (a refrigerant leak or a failing blower) require gauges, leak detection, and EPA-certified refrigerant handling. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out the simple stuff, but the repair itself usually needs a technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air, dropping the coil temperature until it ices.
2. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — the second most common cause. Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. If your system was fine for years and now freezes, suspect a slow leak.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and block airflow, mimicking a dirty filter.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong blower speed** — an ECM or PSC blower that isn't moving enough air will freeze the coil. Failing capacitors on PSC blowers cause this.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — reducing airflow below the design minimum.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature drops pressures and freezes coils.
7. **Clogged condensate drain (secondary)** — some systems shut off on a float switch, but standing water and high humidity can worsen icing.
8. **Metering device (TXV/piston) failure or a kinked line** — restricts refrigerant flow and causes localized freezing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — they're all safe and may solve the problem or save you a service call.

- **Turn the system to OFF (cooling), fan to ON.** Running the blower without cooling thaws the coil fastest and safely. Wait 1–4 hours until all ice is gone. Never chip or scrape ice off the copper.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. Note the size printed on the frame.
- **Check the thermostat.** Set it to a normal temperature (not extremely low) and make sure "Cool" and "Auto" fan are selected once thawed. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Walk your vents.** Open any closed supply registers and clear furniture, rugs, or boxes off return grilles. Restricting return air is a top freeze cause.
- **Check the breaker.** If the outdoor unit tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan or a float-switch cutoff. You can clear a clogged drain line with a wet/dry vacuum at the outdoor termination and add drain tablets.
- **Watch the drain water as it thaws** — a lot of water is normal; place towels or a shop vac to prevent overflow.

After thawing and replacing the filter, run cooling and watch. **If it freezes again, stop and call a technician** — repeated freezing means a leak or airflow fault that DIY won't fix.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech should:

1. **Confirm the ice is fully thawed** (or thaw it) before testing — you can't read pressures accurately on an iced coil.
2. **Measure airflow** — inspect the filter, coil cleanliness, and blower operation; check blower amp draw and capacitor on PSC motors, or programmed speed on ECM blowers.
3. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and read suction/head pressures and superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is low.
4. **Leak-search if charge is low** — electronic sniffer, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test. **Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag** — it will just leak out again.
5. **Test the metering device (TXV/piston)** if airflow and charge are both good but freezing persists.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant, $400" without a filter/airflow check or a leak search is worth questioning.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm air from vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, clean coil |
| Coil freezes again days after thawing | Refrigerant leak (low charge) | Thaw and stop running cooling | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice on the large copper line outside | Low charge or metering issue | Turn off cooling, fan on | Gauge readings, TXV/piston test |
| Weak airflow even with clean filter | Failing blower or capacitor | Confirm filter is clean | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Water overflowing from air handler | Melting ice + clogged drain | Vacuum drain line, add tablets | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Freezing only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoor | Don't run AC in cold weather | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and system size):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement (PSC):** $150–$350
- **Blower motor replacement (ECM):** $500–$1,000
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100–$350
- **Leak repair + recharge (small leak, accessible):** $400–$1,200 depending on refrigerant type and amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,800 (higher on R-410A systems and if the coil is discontinued)
- **Compressor replacement (if freeze damaged it):** $1,500–$2,800+ — the reason you must not run a frozen system

Systems using older R-410A or transitional refrigerants cost more to recharge. If your Goodman is out of warranty and needs a coil *and* compressor, ask whether replacing the system is more economical.

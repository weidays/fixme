## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a digital fault code—Goodman air conditioners don't flash a specific number for it. Instead, it's a physical condition: the indoor coil (inside your air handler or above the furnace) drops below 32°F and moisture in the air freezes into a layer of frost or solid ice.

Once ice forms, it insulates the coil and blocks airflow, so the system cools less and less until it barely moves air—and the ice keeps growing. You'll often notice weak or warm air from the vents, water pooling near the furnace/air handler as it thaws, or ice visible on the copper lines outside.

The root problem is almost always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (airflow) or **not enough refrigerant in the system** (a leak or undercharge). Both let the coil get too cold.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant charge / refrigerant leak** — Low charge causes low pressure and a super-cold coil. This is a pro repair; refrigerant handling is EPA-regulated.
3. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — Too many closed registers or blocked returns reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and hurt heat transfer, promoting freeze-up.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / capacitor** — If the indoor blower runs slow, airflow drops and the coil freezes.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — Operating cooling when outdoor temps are below ~62°F can freeze the coil on many systems.
7. **Clogged condensate drain triggering a float switch** — Not a freeze cause directly, but often discovered at the same time when water backs up.
8. **Thermostat set too low or fan set wrong** — Extreme low setpoints and a poorly matched fan mode can contribute.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Do not** open the refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This is step one for any frozen coil—it thaws the ice using room-temperature air. Leave it for 1–3 hours until no frost remains.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. A $10 filter fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open all supply registers and clear returns.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or vent covers aren't blocking airflow. Keep most vents open.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool, fan on Auto for normal use, and the setpoint isn't unreasonably low. Replace dead batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breaker.** If the system tripped during the freeze, reset the breaker once. Do not repeatedly reset it.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** Look for standing water or a full pan near the indoor unit. Clearing a clogged drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination point is safe DIY.
- **Look outside.** Ice on the large copper line near the outdoor unit confirms a frozen coil. If it keeps returning after a clean filter and full thaw, you likely have a refrigerant or airflow problem that needs a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work through the airflow-vs-refrigerant question in a logical order—here's what a fair diagnosis looks like:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing anything, since a frozen coil gives false readings.
2. **Measure airflow and static pressure** to check the blower, ductwork, and filter condition.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt, and the blower wheel/motor for slow operation or a weak run capacitor.
4. **Attach gauges and read pressures** and superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is low.
5. **Leak-search** if the charge is low—dye, electronic detector, or soap bubbles—because simply "topping off" refrigerant on a leaking system is a temporary, non-compliant fix.
6. **Verify the metering device (TXV/piston)** is operating correctly, as a stuck TXV can also cause freezing.

If a tech quotes a refrigerant recharge without mentioning a leak search, ask why—repeatedly adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm air from vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Turn cooling off, fan on, replace filter | Check static pressure, clean coil |
| Ice on large copper line outside, refreezes after thaw | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw fully, then call a pro | Leak search, repair, EPA-compliant recharge |
| Water pooling near air handler | Thawing coil + clogged drain | Clear drain line with wet/dry vac | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Weak airflow even with clean filter | Failing blower or capacitor | Confirm fan runs; then call a pro | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Coil freezes in cool weather | Running AC below ~62°F outdoor | Wait for warmer temps or use fan only | Confirm low-ambient limits, adjust use |
| Coil looks dirty/matted | Dirty evaporator coil | None—don't spray uncertified cleaners | Professional coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your price varies by region, system age, and refrigerant type (R-410A systems cost more to recharge; older R-22 units cost much more).

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$30
- **Condensate drain cleaning (DIY):** $0–$25
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge (R-410A):** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,800 installed
- **Compressor replacement (if damaged by running frozen):** $1,300–$2,800+

The takeaway: catching a frozen coil early and shutting it down protects the compressor—the most expensive part in the system.

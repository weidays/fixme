## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a fault code on a display — Goodman air conditioners don't flash a specific error for it. It's a physical condition your indoor coil reaches when its surface temperature drops below freezing and moisture in the air freezes into ice on the fins.

The evaporator coil sits inside your indoor unit (in the furnace cabinet or air handler). Under normal operation, warm indoor air blows across the cold coil, refrigerant absorbs the heat, and condensation drips into the drain pan. When something disrupts that heat exchange — too little airflow or too little refrigerant — the coil gets *too* cold, condensation freezes instead of draining, and ice snowballs until airflow stops almost entirely.

Common symptoms you'll notice: weak or no airflow from vents, the system running constantly without cooling, water leaking around the indoor unit as ice melts, or visible frost on the copper refrigerant line outside near the condenser.

This condition can damage your compressor if ignored, which is why we rate it **pro** severity even though the first steps are DIY.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of the warm airflow it needs, letting it drop below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant charge / leak** — Undercharged systems run the coil colder than designed. On Goodman units with a TXV, low charge can still freeze the coil; on piston-metered systems it's even more sensitive.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and biofilm insulate the fins, blocking heat transfer even with a clean filter.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor** — Reduced airflow from a worn blower, wrong speed tap, or failing ECM module.
5. **Blocked or closed ducts / vents** — Too many closed registers or crushed ductwork chokes airflow to the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — Operating below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature drives the coil temperature too low.
7. **Faulty metering device (TXV/piston)** — Less common; a stuck metering device over-feeds or under-feeds refrigerant.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system or add refrigerant yourself.

- **Turn cooling OFF first.** Set the thermostat to COOL OFF and the fan to **ON**. Let the ice fully thaw (1–24 hours). Never chip at ice with a tool.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. Note the size printed on the frame.
- **Check every supply and return vent.** Open all registers and remove furniture, rugs, or boxes blocking them. Air needs a full path.
- **Inspect the condensate drain.** As ice melts, a lot of water flows out. Make sure the drain pan and line aren't overflowing; a wet/dry vac on the drain line clears a clog.
- **Verify the thermostat setting.** Don't set it below about 68°F, and avoid running the AC when it's cool outside.
- **Check the breaker.** After thawing, if the unit won't restart, confirm the breaker for the AC and air handler is on.

If the coil re-freezes within a day of a clean filter and clear airflow, stop running it and call a technician — that points to refrigerant or blower problems.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm and thaw** the coil, then inspect the filter, coil cleanliness, and blower wheel.
2. **Measure airflow** — static pressure readings across the system reveal duct restrictions or a weak blower.
3. **Attach gauges** to read suction and head pressure, then calculate **superheat and subcooling**. This is how they distinguish a low charge from an airflow problem.
4. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low — electronic sniffer, UV dye, or bubble test. A proper repair fixes the leak *before* recharging, not just tops off refrigerant.
5. **Test the metering device and blower motor** if airflow and charge check out.

Be cautious of any tech who simply adds refrigerant without a leak search and airflow test — that's a temporary patch, not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, ice on indoor coil | Dirty filter | Replace filter, thaw coil, run fan | Verify airflow with static pressure test |
| Frost on outdoor copper line, warm air inside | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off, thaw, call pro | Leak search, repair, evacuate & recharge |
| Coil re-freezes after clean filter | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw, replace filter | Chemically clean coil |
| Little airflow even with clean filter | Failing blower / wrong speed | Confirm fan runs | Test/replace blower motor or ECM module |
| Water pooling at indoor unit | Melting ice + clogged drain | Vac out drain line | Clear drain, check pan & pump |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Stop running AC when cool | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor). Regional pricing and unit size vary these.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $8–$40
- **Thaw + service call diagnostic:** $75–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Condensate drain clearing / new pump:** $75–$400
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,000+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Blower motor / ECM module replacement:** $300–$900
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$700
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $800–$1,500+ (higher if the coil is warranty-only labor)

A frozen coil that turns out to be a compressor casualty from prolonged running can push costs well past $1,500 — the reason to shut the unit off at the first sign of ice.

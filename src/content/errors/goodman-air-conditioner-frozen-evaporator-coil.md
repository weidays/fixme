## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" is a condition, not a fault code Goodman displays on a
screen. Most residential Goodman split systems have no display that flags this
directly — you notice it because cooling drops off, a block of ice forms on the
indoor coil or refrigerant lines, or water pools around the air handler as the ice
melts.

The evaporator coil is the indoor part of your AC that absorbs heat from your home's
air. It runs cold — but it should stay above freezing. When something drops the coil
temperature below 32°F, condensation on the coil turns to frost, then to a solid ice
block. Ice insulates the coil, so cooling gets worse, which makes it colder, which
makes more ice. It snowballs until the system barely cools at all.

The two families of root causes are **low airflow** across the coil and **low
refrigerant charge**. Everything below traces back to one of those.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow
   drops coil temperature until it freezes.
2. **Closed, blocked or too few supply/return vents** — furniture over returns, shut
   registers, or crushed flex duct starve the coil of airflow.
3. **Dirty blower wheel or dirty evaporator coil** — years of buildup reduce airflow
   and heat transfer.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — a very common cause of *repeat* freezing.
   Goodman coils and line sets can develop slow leaks; low charge over-cools the coil.
5. **Failing blower motor or wrong blower speed** — a motor that won't run at full
   speed reduces airflow. Some Goodman air handlers use ECM motors set by dip switches
   or board settings, which vary by model.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** (below ~60°F outside) — this drops suction
   pressure and freezes the coil. Rare but real on cool summer nights.
7. **Stuck-closed metering device (TXV or piston)** — restricts refrigerant flow and
   freezes the coil; a technician-level diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system in **fan-only or OFF** — never keep it in cooling while iced.

- **Switch to fan-only to thaw first.** At your thermostat, set mode to OFF or FAN
  ON, cooling off. Let the coil thaw completely (1–24 hours). Running the compressor
  frozen can wreck it.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray or you can't see light through
  it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen-coil cases.
- **Open and unblock all vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return
  grilles aren't covered by rugs, furniture or closed doors.
- **Check the condensate line and drain pan.** Melting ice produces a lot of water —
  confirm the drain isn't overflowing indoors.
- **Verify the thermostat setting.** Set to a reasonable temperature (around 72–75°F);
  extremely low setpoints run the system nonstop.
- **Check the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and outdoor unit if the blower
  won't run at all.

If the coil refreezes after a clean filter and open vents, stop running it in cooling
and call a technician — you're likely into refrigerant or blower territory.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the cause rather than just topping off refrigerant. Expect them to:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing.
- Measure **airflow / temperature split** (return vs supply air temperature) to see if
  it's an airflow problem.
- Inspect the **filter, blower wheel and evaporator coil** for restriction.
- Check **blower motor operation and speed setting** (ECM settings vary by Goodman
  model).
- Attach gauges to read **suction and head pressures** and calculate **superheat and
  subcooling** to judge the refrigerant charge.
- **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low — a topped-off
  system that's leaking will just freeze again.
- Verify the **metering device (TXV/piston)** is feeding correctly.

Be cautious of any quote that adds refrigerant without a leak search — that's a
temporary fix that leaks money and refrigerant.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak cooling | Dirty filter / low airflow | Fan-only to thaw, replace filter, open vents | Measure airflow, clean coil/blower |
| Ice on outdoor line (suction line) | Low refrigerant or restricted metering | Thaw, then stop running in cool mode | Leak search, charge, TXV/piston check |
| Water pooling near air handler | Melting ice overwhelming drain | Thaw slowly, clear condensate line | Confirm no leak, verify drain slope |
| Coil refreezes within a day of cleaning | Refrigerant leak or blower fault | None — call a pro | Superheat/subcool test, leak repair |
| Blower not running at all | Motor, capacitor or board fault | Check breaker | Test blower motor, capacitor, control board |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Low outdoor temp | Don't run AC below ~60°F outside | Confirm low-ambient limits |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges. Actual prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Air filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Coil / blower cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **ECM or PSC blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 installed, depending on model.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$500.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+, higher if it's the coil itself
  or the outdoor unit. R-32 vs R-410A systems and the amount of refrigerant affect cost.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ installed, more if under-warranty parts
  labor still applies.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900 installed.

Many Goodman units carry a 10-year parts warranty if registered — the coil part may be
covered even when labor isn't, so ask the tech to check the model and serial.

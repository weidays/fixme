## What this code means

Goodman air conditioners do not display a specific "frozen evaporator coil" fault code — this is a **physical condition**, not a board error. You'll notice it as a solid layer of ice or frost on the indoor coil (the A-coil above your furnace or in the air handler), on the copper refrigerant lines, or dripping water where there shouldn't be any.

The evaporator coil is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your indoor air. When something disrupts that balance — too little warm air moving across the coil, or too little refrigerant — the coil surface drops below freezing and condensation turns to ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil and makes the problem snowball: less cooling, more freezing.

This is filed as **pro severity** because the two most common root causes (a refrigerant leak or an airflow/blower fault) usually require a licensed technician. There are safe things you can check first, though.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. A choked filter starves the coil of warm return air, dropping coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant charge / refrigerant leak** — less refrigerant means lower coil pressure and temperature. This is common and requires EPA-certified handling.
3. **Restricted airflow from a dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust build a film that insulates the coil.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / capacitor** — if the indoor fan spins slowly, airflow drops.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — reduces total system airflow.
6. **Dirty blower wheel** — caked debris on the fan blades kills airflow just like a dirty coil.
7. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** (below ~60°F) — the system can't maintain proper coil temperature.
8. **Failed or stuck metering device (TXV or orifice)** — less common; behavior varies by model.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the system OFF first.** Set the thermostat to **OFF** and the fan to **ON**. Running the blower with cooling off is the fastest safe way to thaw the coil.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Open all supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking airflow. Never close more than a couple of registers.
- **Look at the breaker.** If the outdoor unit tripped, that's a symptom worth noting — but don't repeatedly reset a breaker that keeps tripping.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it thaws. Make sure the drain pan isn't overflowing and clear the drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination if it's slow.

Do **not** open the refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, or bend/scrape ice off the coil. Let it melt naturally.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path, and you can sanity-check their quote against it:

1. **Confirm and fully thaw the coil** before testing — you can't read pressures accurately on a frozen system.
2. **Verify airflow**: check the filter, inspect the evaporator coil and blower wheel, and measure temperature split across the coil (typically ~16–22°F on a healthy system).
3. **Test the blower motor and capacitor** if airflow is weak.
4. **Connect gauges and read refrigerant pressures** against the charging chart for your specific Goodman model, and check superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is low.
5. **Leak-search** if refrigerant is low — with electronic detector, dye, or soap bubbles. Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.
6. **Inspect the metering device (TXV or orifice)** if pressures point that way.

Be wary of any tech who "tops off" refrigerant without discussing a leak search — refrigerant doesn't get used up; if it's low, it leaked out.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter | Replace filter, run fan to thaw | Verify airflow, check coil |
| Coil re-freezes days after thawing | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw and note recurrence | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Frost on lines, warm air from vents | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw; call for cleaning | Deep-clean coil |
| Little to no airflow at vents | Blower motor/capacitor fault | Check breaker; thaw | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Water pooling near furnace/air handler | Thawed coil overflow | Clear condensate drain | Clear/replace drain, pan float |
| Freezing only in cool weather | Running AC below 60°F outside | Stop running AC when cold out | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges. Prices vary by region and by whether it's a straightforward fix or a leak buried in the coil.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (more for ECM variable-speed motors)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$500 depending on method
- **Minor leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,200
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+
- **Refrigerant recharge alone (R-410A):** $150–$600 depending on amount

If your Goodman uses R-410A and is still under the sealed-system parts warranty, the coil part may be covered — but labor and refrigerant usually aren't. Ask for warranty details before authorizing work.

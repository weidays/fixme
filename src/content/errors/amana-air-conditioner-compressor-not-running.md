## What this code means

"Compressor not running" is a symptom, not a numbered Amana fault code. The indoor blower runs and often the outdoor fan spins too, but the compressor, which is the large sealed unit inside the outdoor cabinet, never starts or keeps shutting off. Without it, refrigerant doesn't circulate and the air coming out is room temperature.

How it shows up depends on your model:

- **Single-stage Amana units** usually have no diagnostic display. You recognize the problem by sound and feel. The fan runs but there's no compressor hum, or you hear a hum or click with no start.
- **Two-stage and variable-speed/communicating Amana units** (for example inverter models paired with a ComfortBridge or communicating thermostat) have a control board. That board can flag compressor faults such as high or low pressure lockouts, overcurrent, or temperature faults, through LED flashes or thermostat messages. Exact codes and reset rules differ by model, so the board's fault chart on the service panel is the authoritative source.

**Reset behavior:**

- The compressor's internal overload resets by itself once the compressor cools. This often takes 1 to 3 hours, sometimes longer.
- Pressure-switch lockouts on board-equipped models may reset automatically after a set number of retries. Some hold until power is cycled.
- Check your unit's documentation. Don't assume one behavior applies to every model.

If the outdoor fan is also dead, run the thermostat, breaker and disconnect checks below first. If both the fan and compressor are still off after that, go to the Amana AC Outdoor Fan Not Spinning page. This page focuses on cases where the compressor specifically isn't running.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** This is the most common cause on single-stage Amana condensers. A weak or open dual capacitor leaves the compressor humming without starting, or not starting at all. Outdoor heat speeds up capacitor failure.
2. **Contactor not pulling in, or burned contacts.** The contactor is the relay that switches power to the compressor. Pitted contacts, a failed coil, or insects jammed in the contactor can stop the compressor from getting power.
3. **Internal thermal overload open.** The compressor overheated and shut itself off. Common triggers are a dirty outdoor coil, low refrigerant charge, or a weak capacitor, and it often happens on hot afternoons.
4. **Pressure switch lockout.** Many Amana units have low- and/or high-pressure switches. A low charge (leak) or a blocked outdoor coil can trip them, and board-equipped models may lock out after repeated trips.
5. **Low-voltage control problem.** Possible faults include a broken thermostat wire to the outdoor unit, a blown 3A low-voltage fuse at the furnace or air handler board, or a thermostat that isn't calling for cooling. An opened condensate float switch also breaks the cooling call.
6. **Inverter or control board fault (variable-speed models).** On variable-speed models, the inverter drive or outdoor control board can fail or report a fault that stops the compressor.
7. **Failed compressor.** The cause may be shorted or open windings, a grounded winding, or a seized compressor. This is the least common cause, but the most expensive, so get it confirmed before you accept a replacement quote.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat.** Set it to Cool, with the temperature 3 to 5°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the screen is blank or dim. Wait at least 5 minutes, because most systems have a built-in restart delay.
- **Air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter can cause low pressure and freeze-ups that lead to compressor trips.
- **Breakers and disconnect.** Check the AC breaker in the main panel and the outdoor disconnect switch next to the unit.
  - If the breaker is tripped, reset it **once**.
  - If it trips again, stop. See "Breaker keeps tripping."
- **One power reset for a locked-out unit.** Turn the thermostat Off, switch off the outdoor disconnect or breaker for 5 minutes, then restore power and set the thermostat back to Cool. Do this once only.
- **Let it cool.** On a very hot day, turn the system off for 1 to 3 hours so a tripped overload can reset, then try once more. A hot compressor sometimes needs longer.
- **Outdoor coil airflow, from the outside only.** Look for leaves, cottonwood, grass clippings, or anything stacked against the unit. Clear debris by hand from around the outside of the cabinet. Don't open the unit.
- **Vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return grilles are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate.** Check only what you can see without removing any panels: the secondary (emergency) drain pan, if your system has one, and the end of the condensate drain line where it exits. Water standing in the secondary pan can mean the float switch has tripped. Clearing a clogged condensate line at its exit can restore the cooling call. Don't remove panels to reach the primary pan at the indoor coil. That's technician work.
- **Panels.** Confirm the indoor unit's access panels are seated firmly.

If the compressor still won't start, or you hear humming or clicking with no start, **turn the system off** and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will usually work through these steps:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling.** They'll measure 24V at the outdoor contactor coil. If it's missing, they'll trace the thermostat wiring, the low-voltage fuse, and any pressure or float switches.
2. **Check the contactor.** They'll verify that it pulls in and measure line voltage across the contacts to look for pitting or voltage drop.
3. **Test the capacitor.** They'll measure capacitance (µF) against the rating on the label. The reading should be within the tolerance printed on the capacitor label.
4. **Check compressor windings.** They'll measure resistance between Common, Start, and Run, check for an open overload, and do an insulation test to ground.
5. **Measure amp draw.** If the compressor tries to start, they'll compare locked-rotor amps with running amps. They may try a hard-start kit to see whether the compressor will start.
6. **Check refrigerant pressures and temperatures.** They'll check superheat and subcooling to confirm the charge if pressure switches or overheating are involved. A low charge means there's a leak that needs to be found.
7. **Read board fault codes** on two-stage and inverter models, using the Amana fault chart for that model.

**Red flag:** A tech who says "the compressor is bad" without showing winding readings or amp-draw results. Ask for the numbers.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor fan runs, compressor silent, warm air | Failed capacitor or open overload | Turn off for 1–3 hours, then try once | Test and replace capacitor; check overload |
| Loud hum or click outside, nothing starts | Capacitor failure or locked compressor | Turn system off immediately | Test capacitor and windings; try hard-start kit |
| Neither outdoor fan nor compressor runs | No 24V signal, failed contactor, tripped breaker | Check thermostat, batteries, breaker, disconnect; if still dead, see the Outdoor Fan Not Spinning page | Test contactor, low-voltage fuse, wiring |
| Compressor runs a few minutes then stops | Overheating or pressure switch trip | Clear debris around unit; replace filter | Check charge, clean coil, find leaks |
| Stops on hot afternoons, restarts later | Overload from dirty coil or weak capacitor | Clear area around condenser | Clean coil; test capacitor and charge |
| Thermostat or board shows fault/lockout (variable-speed) | Pressure, inverter, or sensor fault | One power reset | Read fault code; repair per Amana chart |
| Water in secondary (emergency) drain pan, AC won't start | Condensate float switch open | Clear condensate line at its exit | Clear drain; check float switch |
| Breaker trips when compressor tries to start | Shorted or grounded compressor, wiring fault | Reset once; don't keep resetting | Megger/winding tests; repair wiring or replace compressor |

## Repair costs

These are typical US prices, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $80–$200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Hard-start kit | $150–$350 |
| Low-voltage fuse or thermostat wire repair | $100–$300 |
| Pressure switch replacement | $200–$500 |
| Refrigerant leak search and repair | $250–$1,500+, plus refrigerant |
| Refrigerant recharge (R-410A, or R-32 on newer units; check the outdoor unit's nameplate) | $200–$800+, depending on amount |
| Outdoor control or inverter board (variable-speed) | $500–$1,500 |
| Compressor replacement, under parts warranty | $1,200–$2,800 (labor, refrigerant, components) |
| Compressor replacement, out of warranty | $1,800–$3,500+ |
| Full condenser replacement | $3,500–$7,500+ |

Check your Amana warranty registration before approving a compressor replacement. Many units have long parts warranties, and some have unit replacement coverage.

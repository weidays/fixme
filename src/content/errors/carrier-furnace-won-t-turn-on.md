## What this code means

"Won't turn on" isn't a flash code — it's what you experience when the furnace shows no response at all: no display, no blower, no inducer hum, and no ignition sequence when the thermostat calls for heat. On a Carrier furnace, this points to a break somewhere in the power chain rather than a single sensor fault.

Carrier furnaces need three things to even begin a heat cycle: 120V line power reaching the unit, 24V control (low) voltage from the transformer, and a closed blower-door safety switch. If any of those is missing, the control board stays dark and the furnace does nothing. Because there's no diagnostic LED to read when the board has no power, this is a process-of-elimination problem.

Note that a totally dead furnace is different from one that clicks, hums, or flashes a code — if you see any of that, check the related pages below, since those point to specific documented faults.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Tripped breaker or furnace disconnect switch off** — The wall-mounted switch near the furnace (looks like a light switch) or the breaker at the panel is the single most common cause of a total no-start.
2. **Dead thermostat batteries or thermostat fault** — Battery-powered Carrier and third-party thermostats go blank when batteries die and stop sending the heat call.
3. **Open blower-door safety switch** — If the lower access panel isn't seated fully, the door interlock switch stays open and cuts all power to the board. Very common after filter changes or service.
4. **Blown low-voltage fuse on the control board** — A wiring short or thermostat short can blow the small automotive-style fuse on the control board, killing 24V control power. This is technician territory.
5. **Failed transformer** — No 24V output means the board can't power up even with good line voltage.
6. **Failed control board (circuit board)** — Corrosion, surge damage, or age can kill the board entirely so it never powers up.
7. **Loose or corroded wiring / bad connection at the board or transformer** — Especially in older or damp installs.

Whether power must be cycled or the unit auto-recovers depends entirely on the root cause — a blown fuse or failed board will not recover until repaired.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform on a dead furnace:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to HEAT and the setpoint is above room temperature. If the screen is blank, replace the batteries with fresh ones.
- **Furnace power switch:** Find the switch on or near the furnace cabinet (often looks like an ordinary light switch) and make sure it's ON.
- **Breaker:** At your electrical panel, find the furnace breaker. If it's tripped (middle position), flip it fully OFF, then back ON — once.
- **Access panel:** Make sure the lower blower-door panel is fully seated and pushed in flat. A panel that's slightly ajar holds the safety switch open and keeps the furnace dead. Seat it firmly.
- **Air filter:** Check and replace a visibly dirty filter. Read the size printed on the edge of the filter you pull out and buy that size. It won't usually cause a total no-start, but it rules out airflow issues cheaply.
- **Supply/return vents:** Confirm registers aren't all closed or blocked.
- **Condensate line (high-efficiency models):** If there's a float switch and the drain is clogged, some units will refuse to start. Check for standing water in the drain pan.

If the furnace is still dead after these, stop. Do not open the cabinet, do not bypass the door switch, and do not repeatedly cycle power — call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works the power chain in order, and you can sanity-check their quote against this:

1. **Confirm 120V line voltage** at the furnace disconnect with a meter.
2. **Test the door interlock switch** — verify it closes when the panel is seated; a stuck or broken switch gets replaced.
3. **Check the low-voltage fuse** on the control board; if blown, they'll find the short that caused it before replacing (a fuse that blows again means a wiring or component fault upstream).
4. **Measure transformer output** — should read ~24V. No output means a failed transformer.
5. **Verify 24V reaching the board and the thermostat call** at the R and W terminals.
6. **Inspect the control board** for corrosion, burnt traces, or surge damage; confirm it powers up and responds to a call.
7. **Check wiring and connections** throughout the low-voltage circuit.

A thorough tech won't jump straight to "you need a new board" without ruling out the cheap causes first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blank thermostat screen | Dead batteries or no 24V | Replace thermostat batteries | Test transformer, board fuse, wiring |
| Thermostat lit, furnace dead | Open door switch, blown fuse, bad board | Reseat access panel firmly | Test/replace door switch, fuse, board |
| No display, breaker tripped | Short circuit or overload | Reset breaker once | Find and repair the short |
| Furnace switch off | Switch bumped/left off | Turn furnace switch ON | — |
| Nothing after reset & battery swap | Blown fuse, transformer, or board | Stop — call a pro | Full power-chain diagnosis |
| Standing water in drain pan | Clogged condensate float switch | Clear visible drain blockage | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor:

- **Breaker reset / furnace switch on:** $0 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Reseating access panel:** $0 (DIY)
- **New thermostat (if faulty):** $30–$120 DIY, or $150–$350 installed
- **Blower-door safety switch:** $150–$300 installed
- **Low-voltage fuse (plus finding the short):** $100–$300 depending on the underlying cause
- **Transformer:** $150–$350 installed
- **Control board:** $400–$1,200 installed — basic single-stage boards land near the bottom, while communicating and variable-speed boards (Infinity and similar) sit at the top of the range or above it
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180, often credited toward the repair

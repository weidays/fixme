## What this code means

"Won't turn on" is not a specific Lennox fault code — it describes a furnace that shows **no response at all** when there's a call for heat: no display lights, no inducer hum, no ignition sequence. On Lennox integrated-control furnaces this points to a break somewhere in the power or control chain: incoming line voltage, the 24 VAC control circuit, the thermostat, or the control board itself.

If your furnace **does** show a lit board, a blinking light, or an alphanumeric code on a display but still won't fire, you likely have a specific fault instead rather than a no-power problem — see the related codes at the bottom, especially **Won't ignite**.

One important caveat before you go code-hunting: Lennox does not use a single diagnostic convention across its equipment. Older SureLight-style boards signal faults with **blink patterns and Watchguard indications**, while newer alphanumeric and iComfort-communicating controls report **E-codes on a display**. The same number does **not** always mean the same thing from one control generation to the next. Always read the code against the **code legend printed on your furnace's door label, or the service manual for your specific control board** — not against a generic list.

A total no-start does **not** auto-reset on its own if the cause is a blown fuse or failed component — the unit stays dead until the underlying problem is corrected.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **No power to the furnace** — tripped breaker, blown circuit fuse, or the furnace service switch (the light-switch-looking one on or near the cabinet) turned off.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, blank screen, wrong mode/setpoint, or a loose wire at the thermostat base.
3. **Blown control board fuse** — Lennox boards carry a small automotive-style blade fuse, typically a 3-amp, that blows when a low-voltage wire shorts; check the rating printed on the board itself, since values can differ. When it blows, the board goes completely dark.
4. **Open blower-door safety switch** — if the front panel isn't fully seated, the door interlock switch cuts all power and the furnace acts totally dead.
5. **Failed low-voltage transformer** — no 24 VAC means the board and thermostat get no power.
6. **Failed integrated control board** — the board itself is dead or won't power up even with good incoming voltage.

Causes 3–6 are all **inside the cabinet** and are technician work.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Heat** and the setpoint is several degrees above room temperature. If the screen is blank, replace the batteries.
- **Breaker:** Find the furnace breaker in your electrical panel. If it's tripped, flip it fully **Off**, then back **On**, once.
- **Furnace switch:** There's a standard wall switch on or beside the furnace that looks like a light switch. Make sure it's **On** — these get bumped off surprisingly often.
- **Blower door / front panel:** Make sure the furnace's front access panel is fully seated and pushed in flush. A door that isn't closed all the way holds the safety interlock open and the furnace won't run.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter won't usually cause a total no-start, but replace it if it's dirty — it rules out repeated safety trips.
- **Vents & registers:** Confirm supply and return registers are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate (high-efficiency models):** Check that the condensate drain line isn't clogged or that a float switch hasn't tripped from standing water.

If the furnace is still dead after these, stop — the remaining causes are inside the cabinet.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A tech works from the outside in, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify incoming 120 VAC** at the furnace disconnect with a meter, confirming the breaker and switch actually deliver power.
2. **Check the door interlock switch** to confirm it closes when the panel is seated.
3. **Test the control board fuse** (typically a 3-amp blade fuse — the rating is printed on the board) — if it's blown, they hunt for the shorted low-voltage wire that caused it before simply replacing it.
4. **Measure the transformer output** (should read ~24 VAC) to confirm the control circuit is powered.
5. **Confirm the thermostat call reaches the board** at the R and W terminals.
6. **Inspect the control board** for burn marks, and confirm it powers up and responds. If everything upstream is good and the board is dead, the board is condemned.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Totally dead, no lights | Tripped breaker / off switch | Reset breaker once; confirm furnace switch on | Verify 120 VAC at unit |
| Dead only when panel on | Open blower-door switch | Reseat front panel fully | Test/replace door interlock switch |
| Blank thermostat | Dead batteries | Replace batteries | Check thermostat wiring |
| Board completely dark, power confirmed | Blown board fuse | None — do not open cabinet | Find short, replace fuse |
| No 24 VAC anywhere | Failed transformer | None | Test/replace transformer |
| Everything upstream good, board dead | Failed control board | None | Replace integrated control board |
| Standing water / float trip | Clogged condensate line | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor unless noted):

- **Breaker reset / switch flip / reseat panel:** $0 DIY.
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 DIY.
- **Diagnostic service call:** $90–$180, often credited toward the repair.
- **Blown control board fuse (plus finding the short):** $150–$300 — the fuse is cheap; the labor is in tracing the fault.
- **Blower-door interlock switch:** $120–$250.
- **Low-voltage transformer:** $150–$300.
- **Integrated control board:** $400–$1,200 depending on model and whether it's a variable-speed or communicating board.

Prices vary by region and by Lennox series — variable-speed Signature (SLP/SL) and communicating boards run higher than Merit-series parts and commonly land in the $700–$1,200 range installed.

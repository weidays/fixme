## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code on your Carrier thermostat or control board — it's a physical condition you'll usually spot as ice or frost on the indoor coil, the copper refrigerant lines near your furnace or air handler, or as water pooling on the floor after the ice melts. Your system may still run but blow warm or weak air.

The evaporator coil freezes when its surface temperature drops below freezing. That happens for one of two big reasons: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant pressure** (a charge or metering problem). Both push the coil temperature too low, condensation freezes into ice, and the ice then blocks airflow completely — making the problem worse fast.

Because the two root causes point in opposite directions (too little air vs. too little refrigerant), and because low refrigerant means an EPA-regulated leak repair, this is rated **pro**. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out the easy stuff, but the fix often needs a licensed technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of warm return air.
2. **Restricted airflow** — closed or blocked supply/return vents, collapsed flex duct, or a very dirty evaporator coil itself.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — low pressure drops coil temperature below freezing. Common on older systems and a classic "freezes again after thawing" symptom.
4. **Failing or dirty blower motor / wheel** — reduced CFM across the coil. May be a weak ECM motor, bad capacitor, or caked blower wheel.
5. **Running the AC in cold weather** — using cooling when outdoor temps are below roughly 60°F can freeze the coil on many systems.
6. **Faulty metering device (TXV/piston)** — a stuck or failing expansion valve overfeeds or starves the coil.
7. **Thermostat set too low or fan set to AUTO with heavy demand** — extreme setpoints extend runtime and encourage icing on marginal systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these in order. Stop as soon as cooling returns normally.

1. **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** At the thermostat, set the mode to OFF and the fan to ON. This melts the ice safely using indoor air. Wait 1–3 hours. Do **not** chip or scrape ice off the coil.
2. **Check and replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the top cause. Slide it out — if you can't see light through it, replace it with the correct size.
3. **Open and unblock vents.** Walk the house and make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and not covered by rugs, furniture, or drapes. Keep interior doors open.
4. **Check the thermostat setting.** Don't run cooling if it's cold outside (under ~60°F). Avoid extreme low setpoints like 65°F on a hot day, which run the coil for hours.
5. **Check the breaker.** If the system tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
6. **Check the condensate drain and pan.** A thawed coil dumps a lot of water. Make sure the drain line is clear and the pan isn't overflowing — a clogged drain can shut the system off or cause water damage. A wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain outlet often clears a clog.

Once fully thawed, run cooling and watch. If the coil freezes again within a day, you have a refrigerant or airflow problem that needs a technician — don't keep re-freezing it.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will confirm the coil is fully thawed first, then:

- **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil and blower to rule out duct restriction or a weak blower motor.
- **Inspect and clean the evaporator coil and blower wheel** if they're dirty.
- **Connect gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to determine if the refrigerant charge is correct — this is the key test that separates an airflow problem from a leak.
- **Leak-search** with electronic detector or dye if the charge is low. They should **find and repair the leak**, not just "top off" — adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary and increasingly costly patch, especially on R-454B systems.
- **Test the blower capacitor and motor amp draw**, and check the metering device (TXV/piston) operation.

A quote that says "just add refrigerant" without a leak search is a red flag. Ask them to show you the superheat/subcooling numbers.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, weak/warm air, dirty filter | Clogged filter starving airflow | Thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, none needed if resolved |
| Freezes again days after new filter | Low refrigerant from leak | Thaw, then call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water on floor near furnace/air handler | Melted ice + clogged drain | Clear drain, replace filter | Clear/flush drain, check pan safety switch |
| Weak airflow from all vents, coil freezes | Failing blower motor or dirty wheel | Check/replace filter | Test capacitor/motor, clean blower wheel |
| Coil freezes only on cool days | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Switch to fan/off in cold weather | Confirm operating conditions |
| Freezes even with clean filter + open vents | Bad TXV or restricted metering | Thaw, call pro | Test/replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Diagnostic/service call fees ($90–$180) often apply on top and may be credited toward the repair.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 professional
- **Thaw + inspection service call:** $90–$200
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (ECM) replacement:** $500–$1,000
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ (varies heavily by leak location and refrigerant type — R-454B on 2025+ Carrier units costs more per pound than R-410A)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+

If your system is older (10–15+ years), uses R-22, and needs a coil or major leak repair, ask your tech whether repair or replacement makes more financial sense.

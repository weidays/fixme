## What this code means

Carrier air conditioners do not have a dedicated "frozen coil" fault code the way a furnace has numbered flash codes. Instead, a frozen evaporator coil is a **symptom** you diagnose by sight and behavior: the indoor coil (inside your air handler or above the furnace) drops below freezing, and the moisture it normally removes turns to ice instead of draining away.

On Infinity-series systems with a communicating control, you may see the wall control report reduced capacity, a "service" reminder, or airflow-related messages — but there is no standard code that literally says "frozen coil." The ice itself is the diagnosis.

A frozen coil always comes down to one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant/pressure in the coil**. Both make the coil colder than it should be until condensation freezes.

This is rated **pro** because the two most serious causes — low refrigerant and compressor damage — require EPA-certified handling and gauges. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out airflow yourself first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Starved airflow lets the coil get too cold and freeze.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers, blocked returns, or a collapsed duct starves the coil the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and cut airflow, even with a clean filter.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. This is the second most common cause and is **not DIY**.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor** — a weak ECM or PSC blower moves too little air. Common on older Comfort-series units.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil on many systems.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — a backed-up drain can trip a float switch and, combined with other issues, contribute to freezing.
8. **Metering device (TXV) problem** — a stuck or failed thermostatic expansion valve overfeeds or underfeeds the coil. Pro diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — several fix the problem for free.

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This stops making ice and thaws the coil faster by pushing warm indoor air across it. Never run cooling on a frozen coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray or you can't see light through it, swap it. This is the #1 fix.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or curtains.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and that the schedule isn't running the AC on a cold night. Replace thermostat batteries if it uses them.
- **Check the breaker.** If the outdoor unit's breaker is tripped but the indoor blower runs, that combination can freeze the coil — reset it once.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** Look for standing water or overflow near the indoor unit. Clear a visible clog and add drain tablets.
- **Look for water damage while thawing.** Melting ice can overflow the drain pan — put towels down and keep an eye on it.

If the coil freezes again within a day or two of a clean filter and good airflow, the cause is almost certainly refrigerant, blower, or the coil itself — stop and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified Carrier tech will typically:

1. **Confirm the coil has fully thawed** before testing — a frozen coil hides real readings.
2. **Measure airflow** across the coil and inspect the blower motor, wheel, and ducts.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt, bent fins, and biofilm.
4. **Connect refrigerant gauges** to read suction and head pressure, then calculate **superheat and subcooling** to judge the charge.
5. **Leak-search** with electronic detectors or dye if the charge is low — adding refrigerant without fixing the leak is a temporary and improper fix.
6. **Test the metering device (TXV)** and, on Infinity systems, read the control board's data for blower and capacity faults.
7. **Check the compressor** for damage if the unit was run frozen for a long time.

A trustworthy quote names the actual root cause (leak location, dirty coil, weak blower) — not just "add refrigerant." Recharging a leaking system without a repair means you'll be frozen again in weeks.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines or coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Turn off cooling, fan ON, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, check duct static |
| Coil refreezes days after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running it; call a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Little to no air from vents | Failing blower motor | Confirm filter is clean | Test/replace ECM or PSC blower |
| Water pooling near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear visible clog, add tablets | Clear line, test float switch |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Running AC in cold weather | Avoid cooling below ~60°F outdoors | Confirm no other fault |
| Warm air, high energy bills, ice | Dirty evaporator coil | Replace filter, schedule cleaning | Professional coil cleaning |
| Coil freezes with good airflow | Stuck TXV / metering device | None — pro required | Test and replace TXV |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your Carrier model, region, and warranty status all move these numbers.

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY) — often the entire fix.
- **Clearing a condensate drain:** $0–$25 DIY; $100–$250 for a pro service call.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (PSC) or $600–$1,200 (ECM/variable-speed).
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$350 for the leak search.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $300–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R‑410A costs more than older systems).
- **TXV (metering valve) replacement:** $400–$800.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500+ if the coil itself leaks; higher on newer high-efficiency units.
- **Compressor replacement** (if run frozen too long): $1,300–$2,800 — a strong reason to shut the unit off at the first sign of ice.

If your system is under Carrier's parts warranty, major components like the coil or compressor may be covered — you typically still pay labor and refrigerant.

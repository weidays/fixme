## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is a **symptom, not a stored fault code** on most Carrier air conditioners. The indoor evaporator coil — inside your air handler or above your furnace — has dropped below freezing, and moisture from the air is turning to ice on it. Once ice forms, airflow collapses, cooling stops, and the problem accelerates.

Carrier base and mid-tier units (Comfort and Performance series) do **not** display a dedicated code for this. You'll notice it as weak airflow, warm air, water on the floor, or visible ice on the copper lines. Higher-end **Infinity** systems with the SYSTXCCITC/ITW wall control may flag a reduced-capacity or airflow-related message, but the exact wording varies by control firmware — treat any such message as a starting point, not a diagnosis.

A frozen coil almost always traces back to one of two root problems: **not enough air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant in the system**. The severity is rated **pro** because the second cause — and confirming it — requires refrigerant gauges and EPA certification.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing. Cheap to rule out first.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — furniture over returns, closed registers in unused rooms, or a collapsed return duct starve the coil of air.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature, causing ice. This is a repair, not a "top-off," and needs leak detection.
4. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — a weak blower moves too little air. Common on ECM and PSC motors as they age.
5. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust build a layer that insulates and restricts airflow across the fins.
6. **Stuck or failing metering device (TXV/piston)** — over-feeds or restricts refrigerant, freezing the coil even with a correct charge.
7. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** (below ~60°F) — low outdoor temps drop suction pressure and freeze the coil.
8. **Clogged condensate drain backing up against the coil** — less common, but standing water can refreeze.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the thermostat fan to ON.** This is the most important step — it thaws the coil safely. Leave it running until all ice is gone (1–24 hours).
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open all supply registers and clear returns.** Move furniture, rugs, or drapes blocking vents. Don't run the system with closed registers.
- **Check the breaker and thermostat.** Confirm the system has power and the thermostat is set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint (not extreme).
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** If you see standing water, clear the drain line with a wet/dry vacuum at the outdoor termination and add a drain tablet.
- **Feel the two copper lines at the outdoor unit** (once thawed). The large insulated line should feel cold but not iced.

Do **not** chip ice off the coil, add refrigerant, or open the sealed system. If the coil re-freezes after a filter change and a full thaw, stop and call a technician — you have a deeper problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Carrier tech will:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — readings on an iced coil are meaningless.
2. **Measure static pressure and airflow** to catch duct or blower restrictions.
3. **Test the blower motor and capacitor** (ECM or PSC) for correct speed and amp draw.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges and check superheat/subcooling** against Carrier's spec for your model. Low subcooling with high superheat points to undercharge or a leak.
5. **Run electronic leak detection or a dye test** if the charge is low — a proper tech finds and fixes the leak, not just refills.
6. **Inspect the evaporator coil and metering device (TXV/piston)** for dirt or restriction.

If a contractor quotes a "refrigerant recharge" without mentioning a **leak search**, get a second opinion — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so a low charge means it leaked out somewhere.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter | Replace filter, thaw with fan ON | Verify airflow, check static pressure |
| Ice on big copper line at outdoor unit | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw only — don't add refrigerant | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Freezes again days after filter change | Blower or refrigerant issue | Note pattern, call pro | Test blower/capacitor, gauge the system |
| Water pooling around air handler | Thawing ice or clogged drain | Clear drain line, thaw coil | Inspect pan, drain, and coil |
| Coil freezes only on cool nights | Low outdoor temp operation | Avoid AC below ~60°F | Confirm no other fault present |
| Reduced airflow, dusty coil visible | Dirty evaporator coil | Change filter; don't clean coil yourself | Professional coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, 2026):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 service call
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (ECM):** $500–$900+
- **Evaporator coil professional cleaning:** $250–$500
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ (depends on leak location; a leaking evaporator coil replacement can run $1,200–$2,500+)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900

A same-day emergency or after-hours call typically adds $100–$300. Because R-410A / newer refrigerants and coil access drive most of the cost, a **frozen coil that turns out to be a leak is the expensive scenario** — get an itemized quote showing the leak location.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code on the Carrier control board—it's a physical condition you'll see or infer. The indoor coil (inside your furnace or air handler cabinet) drops below freezing and collects ice, sometimes a solid block. When that happens, airflow across the coil chokes off, cooling stops, and you may see water pooling near the indoor unit as the ice melts.

A coil freezes for one core reason: **the coil gets colder than it should because heat isn't reaching it fast enough.** That happens either because too little warm air blows across the coil (an airflow problem) or because there's too little refrigerant to carry heat away properly (a refrigerant problem). Both end the same way—ice.

Because refrigerant work and blower diagnosis require gauges and EPA certification, this is marked **pro** severity. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out the simple airflow causes yourself, but if it refreezes, a technician is the right call.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of the warm return air it needs, dropping coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — Too many closed registers, blocked returns, or shut doors reduce total airflow and freeze the coil.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and biofilm insulate the coil so it can't absorb heat, mimicking an airflow shortage.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature, causing freezing. This is the most common *non-airflow* cause and always needs a pro (refrigerant is not a top-off DIY job).
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — A weak blower moves too little air. Common on older Carrier PSC blowers; ECM/variable-speed motors on Infinity units fail differently.
6. **Clogged condensate drain (secondary)** — A full drain pan can trip a safety float switch or let humidity worsen icing in some setups.
7. **Running the AC in cold weather** — Operating cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F can freeze the coil regardless of everything else.
8. **Metering device (TXV/orifice) failure** — Less common; a stuck valve overfeeds or underfeeds the coil. Pro diagnosis only.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these in order. **First, turn the thermostat to OFF for cooling and set the fan to ON.** Running the fan (not the compressor) thaws the coil fastest and safely.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL at a reasonable temperature and, if a Carrier Infinity/thermostat, isn't stuck in a fault or lockout screen. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Vents and returns:** Open all supply registers and make sure return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or filters stacked on them.
- **Condensate:** Check the drain pan and line for standing water. If your unit has a float switch and it's tripped, clear the drain (tablets or a wet/dry vac on the line).
- **Breaker:** If the indoor blower isn't running at all, check the breaker for the air handler/furnace. Reset once—if it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Wait for full thaw:** Don't restart cooling until every bit of ice is gone (1–3 hours, sometimes overnight). Put towels down for meltwater.

After a full thaw with a clean filter and open vents, run cooling and watch. **If the coil freezes again, stop and call a technician**—the cause is refrigerant or airflow hardware, not something to keep resetting.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech won't just add refrigerant. Expect them to:

- **Confirm airflow first** — inspect the filter, coil cleanliness, and blower operation; measure static pressure and, on ECM/Infinity blowers, check the motor and control programming.
- **Read superheat and subcooling** — connect gauges to see whether the system is undercharged (points to a leak) or has a metering/airflow problem.
- **Leak search if charge is low** — use electronic detectors, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure testing to *find* the leak rather than just recharging. Recharging without fixing a leak is a temporary bandage.
- **Check the metering device** — verify the TXV or orifice is feeding the coil correctly.
- **Verify the condensate system** and float switch operation.

Sanity check on quotes: if a tech proposes "just add a couple pounds of refrigerant" without a leak search, ask where the refrigerant is going. A sealed system doesn't lose charge unless it's leaking.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines / indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Turn cooling OFF, fan ON, replace filter, open vents, let thaw | Confirm airflow, measure static pressure |
| Coil refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running cooling; call pro | Leak search, repair, evacuate & recharge |
| Warm air, water around furnace, ice on coil | Frozen coil melting | Thaw fully, mop water, protect floor | Diagnose root cause |
| Blower barely moves air | Weak blower motor / bad capacitor | Verify fan is set to ON | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Musty smell + water backing up | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain line, add tablets | Clear/reroute drain, check float switch |
| Coil frozen on a cool day | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run cooling in cold weather | N/A |
| Dirty, matted coil surface | Coil fouling | Gentle no-rinse cleaner if accessible | Deep coil clean or pull-and-clean |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your Carrier model, coil access, and refrigerant type (older R-410A vs. newer systems) affect price.

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 DIY.
- **Thaw + restart:** $0 DIY (just time).
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$450 depending on access.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Blower motor (PSC):** $400–$700. **ECM/variable-speed (Infinity):** $600–$1,200+.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant amount.
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $1,200–$2,800+, higher if still under a sealed-system parts warranty (labor still applies).
- **TXV/metering device replacement:** $400–$900.

If your Carrier system is under 10 years old, check for a **parts warranty**—the coil or compressor may be covered, though labor usually isn't.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a specific Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition where the indoor coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) drops below 32°F and moisture from the air freezes into a block of ice. On Carrier Infinity systems you may see a "low airflow," "low suction pressure," or cooling-fault message on the wall control, but many Carrier ACs give no code at all — you just notice weak cooling, ice on the copper lines, or water on the floor.

The coil freezes for one root reason: **not enough heat is reaching it.** Either warm indoor air isn't moving across the coil (an airflow problem) or the refrigerant is too cold and low (a charge problem). Both end the same way — ice, then no cooling, then a risk to your compressor if you keep running it.

This is rated **pro** because the two most damaging causes — low refrigerant and blower failure — require gauges, a leak search, or electrical work that homeowners should not attempt.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust insulate the coil and choke airflow across its fins.
3. **Low refrigerant / refrigerant leak** — low charge drops coil temperature below freezing even with good airflow.
4. **Blower motor or fan problem** — a failing ECM/PSC motor, bad capacitor, or wrong speed tap moves too little air.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — starves the system of return air.
6. **Collapsed or crushed ductwork / undersized return** — chronic low airflow.
7. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~62°F outside) — outdoor temps too low for the system to run correctly.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV/piston)** — over-feeds or under-feeds the coil. Less common, needs a pro.
9. **Dirty blower wheel** — caked buildup on the fan blades cuts airflow.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system **OFF** first:

- **Turn the AC off and let it thaw.** Set the thermostat to OFF, then Fan to ON to blow room air over the coil and melt the ice faster. Wait until it's fully thawed (1–24 hrs) before anything else.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, furry, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's on COOL, the setpoint is reasonable (68–74°F), and the fan is set to AUTO for normal operation. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Open your vents.** Walk the house and open every supply register and confirm return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or filters stacked on top.
- **Check the breaker.** After a thaw, confirm the AC and air-handler breakers are on.
- **Clear the condensate drain.** As ice melts, water dumps into the pan. Make sure the drain line isn't clogged — a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain end can pull out a clog. Check for standing water in the pan.

If, after a fresh filter, open vents, and a full thaw, the coil freezes again within a day — **stop running it and call a pro.** Repeated freezing points to refrigerant or blower problems.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will:

1. **Confirm and clear the ice** — inspect the coil, drain pan, and lines after thawing.
2. **Check airflow first** — verify filter, blower speed, and measure static pressure across the system. Carrier ECM motors can be read via the Infinity control or motor diagnostics.
3. **Inspect the coil and blower wheel** for dirt buildup.
4. **Connect refrigerant gauges** to read suction pressure and superheat/subcooling — this tells them if the charge is low and whether the metering device is working.
5. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen) if charge is low — because "just adding refrigerant" without fixing a leak is a waste of your money.
6. **Test the blower motor and capacitor** if airflow is weak with a clean filter.

Sanity check: if a tech wants to add refrigerant, they should also tell you *where the leak is*. Repeatedly topping off a leaking system is not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil/lines, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Thaw fully, replace filter | Verify airflow, check static pressure |
| Weak cooling, coil freezes days after new filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw, stop running it | Gauge readings, leak search, repair & recharge |
| Water on floor near furnace/air handler | Melting ice + clogged drain | Vac out drain line, check pan | Clear drain, add float switch |
| Coil freezes, blower barely blowing | Bad blower motor/capacitor | Confirm filter is clean | Test/replace ECM motor or capacitor |
| Freezes only on cool days | Running AC below ~62°F outside | Don't run AC in cold weather | Confirm operating conditions |
| Recurs even with clean filter & good charge | Dirty coil or metering device | Note when it freezes | Clean coil, inspect/replace TXV |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and Carrier model):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor (ECM/variable-speed) replacement:** $450–$1,200 — Infinity variable-speed motors run higher
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ (coil, labor, refrigerant)

A frozen coil caused by a filter costs you almost nothing. One caused by a leaking coil is one of the pricier AC repairs — get a written quote and a second opinion before approving a coil or motor replacement.

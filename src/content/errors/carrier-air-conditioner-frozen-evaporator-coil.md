## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" isn't a fault code with a number — it's a physical condition Carrier air conditioners and heat pumps develop when the indoor coil gets cold enough for condensation to freeze into ice instead of draining away.

The evaporator coil sits inside your indoor air handler or furnace cabinet. When warm indoor air passes over it, the refrigerant inside absorbs heat and moisture condenses on the coil. That condensation is normal. But if airflow drops too low, or refrigerant pressure drops too far, the coil temperature falls below freezing and the moisture turns to ice. Once a thin layer forms, it insulates the coil and makes the problem snowball into a solid block of ice.

Symptoms Carrier owners usually notice: weak or no cool air, higher energy bills, hissing or bubbling, water pooling near the indoor unit, or visible ice on the refrigerant lines outside. Some Infinity/Performance systems with communicating controls may flag a low-suction or airflow fault, but the exact wording varies by board and thermostat.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow and drops its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins act as insulation and cut heat transfer.
4. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — low charge drops suction pressure and coil temperature. This is a pro repair and often the reason a coil re-freezes after thawing.
5. **Failing blower motor or wheel** — a weak, dirty, or failing indoor blower moves too little air across the coil.
6. **Running AC in cold weather** — operating cooling below ~60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — standing water can back up onto the coil and refreeze.
8. **Stuck or failed expansion valve/metering device** — restricts refrigerant flow and over-cools the coil (pro repair).

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do them before paying for a service call.

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This stops making ice and lets the blower thaw the coil faster. Never run cooling on a frozen coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, dusty, or older than 1–3 months, swap it for the correct size. This resolves a large share of freeze cases.
- **Open all supply registers and clear returns.** Make sure no furniture, rugs, or closed vents are choking airflow.
- **Check the thermostat.** Set it to a reasonable temperature and set the fan to ON (not AUTO) to help thawing.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor unit tripped, the blower can't run to thaw the coil.
- **Look at the condensate drain.** Clear standing water from the pan and vacuum the drain line if it's clogged. Drain tablets help prevent recurrence.
- **Wait for a full thaw** — 1 to 3 hours, up to a day for heavy ice — then restart cooling and watch whether it re-freezes.

Do **not** open refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, or bend coil fins yourself. If the coil freezes again after a clean filter and full thaw, stop and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech should work through this, roughly in order:

1. **Confirm the coil is thawed** and inspect the filter, blower wheel, and coil for restrictions.
2. **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil to rule out a duct or blower problem.
3. **Connect gauges and read refrigerant pressures**, then calculate superheat and subcooling to determine if the charge is correct or low.
4. **Inspect for leaks** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low — adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.
5. **Check the metering device / TXV** operation and coil temperature.
6. **Test blower motor amperage and speed tap** (or ECM programming on variable-speed Carrier units).
7. **Verify condensate drainage** and pan condition.

If a tech immediately wants to "top off the refrigerant" without measuring airflow or looking for a leak, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open registers, thaw | Verify airflow, check blower |
| Coil re-freezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw, then stop and call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water around indoor unit | Melting ice / clogged drain | Clear drain, vacuum line | Flush drain, check pan/float switch |
| Ice on outdoor lines, hissing | Low charge or metering fault | None safe | Gauge readings, TXV/leak diagnosis |
| Coil freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Stop cooling until warmer | Advise on low-ambient operation |
| Weak airflow, clean filter | Dirty coil or blower wheel | None (access limited) | Clean coil/blower, test motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your Carrier model, region, and matched indoor unit affect the price.

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Coil cleaning (in place):** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $300–$900 (standard); $600–$1,200+ for variable-speed ECM
- **Refrigerant leak search + minor repair + recharge:** $400–$1,200 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ (higher if under warranty labor only)
- **Compressor damage from prolonged freezing:** $1,500–$2,800+

Note: R-410A and older R-22 systems have very different refrigerant costs. Repeated freezing that damaged the compressor is the worst-case outcome — which is exactly why you should never keep running a frozen unit.

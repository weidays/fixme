## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a numeric Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition. The indoor evaporator coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) drops below freezing, and moisture from the air freezes onto it as ice or frost. Most standard Carrier split systems don't display a code for this at all; you'll notice weak or no cooling, ice on the copper lines, or water around the indoor unit as it thaws.

On higher-end Carrier **Infinity** and some **SmartComfort** systems, the control may log an airflow-related or low-temperature fault, but there is no universal "frozen coil" code across the Carrier lineup. If your thermostat or control shows a specific code, look it up separately.

A coil freezes for one basic reason: **not enough heat is reaching the coil to boil off the refrigerant.** That comes from either restricted airflow or low refrigerant charge. Both are covered below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of warm return air, so it drops below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and grime insulate the coil and reduce heat transfer, leading to freeze-up over time.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — undercharge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This is a pro repair; refrigerant does not "run out" normally, so low charge means a leak.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / capacitor** — if the indoor fan can't move enough air, the coil freezes even with a clean filter.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — using cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F can freeze the coil on many systems.
7. **Clogged condensate drain (secondary effect)** — a full drain can trigger a safety float switch or contribute to icing and water backup.
8. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/orifice)** — less common; causes improper refrigerant flow and localized freezing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no refrigerant, no opening sealed panels beyond the filter.

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This is step one. It stops making ice and thaws the coil safely. Wait until all ice is gone (1–24 hours) before running the AC again.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it with the correct size.
- **Check your thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool and the setpoint isn't extremely low. Replace thermostat batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Check the breaker.** If the air handler or condenser tripped, that can stop the fan and contribute to freeze-ups.
- **Open blocked vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unobstructed by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
- **Check the condensate drain.** After thawing, look for standing water at the indoor unit. Clearing a visible drain line (wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain end) is homeowner-safe.

If the coil freezes again within a day or two of a clean filter and clear vents, stop and call a technician — you likely have a refrigerant or blower problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will confirm the cause rather than just recharging refrigerant. Expect them to:

- Verify the coil is fully thawed before testing (running against ice gives false readings).
- Measure **airflow** — check the filter, blower wheel condition, blower motor amperage and capacitor, and duct static pressure.
- Inspect the **evaporator coil** for dirt and restricted fins.
- Attach **gauges** and measure superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct, low, or if the metering device is faulty.
- **Leak-search** if the charge is low — using electronic detectors, UV dye, or a nitrogen pressure test. Simply "topping off" without finding the leak is a red flag.
- Check the condensate drain and float switch operation.

A good quote names the specific cause. Be cautious of any tech who adds refrigerant without explaining why it was low.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, weak cooling | Dirty filter / low airflow | Turn off cooling, fan ON, replace filter | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Coil re-freezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Shut down, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water pooling at indoor unit | Thawed ice + clogged drain | Clear visible drain line | Clear/flush drain, test float switch |
| Reduced airflow, clean filter | Failing blower or capacitor | Check breaker only | Test motor amps, capacitor, wheel |
| Freezes in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run cooling in cold temps | Confirm no other fault |
| Whole coil iced, dirty fins | Dirty evaporator coil | Replace filter, thaw | Professional coil cleaning |
| Uneven icing on coil | Faulty TXV/metering device | Call pro | Test superheat, replace TXV |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges. Actual cost varies by region, system size, and refrigerant type (older R-410A vs. newer systems).

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Coil thaw:** $0 (just time)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150–$300 professional
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $450–$900+ (ECM motors cost more)
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$500
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often the case if the coil itself leaks)

If refrigerant is repeatedly low, replacing an old leaking coil or aging system may be more economical than repeated recharges.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code on Carrier equipment — there's no blinking-light number for it. It's a physical condition: ice has built up on the indoor evaporator coil (the A-coil above your furnace or inside the air handler). On Infinity systems you may instead see a low-airflow or low-suction-related fault, but most homeowners discover it by symptom: warm air from the vents, a hissing or gurgling unit, or visible ice on the copper lines near the indoor unit.

The evaporator coil freezes when the coil surface drops below 32°F. That happens when there's too little heat moving across the coil — either because airflow is restricted or because there isn't enough refrigerant to keep pressures normal. Ice acts as an insulator, so once it starts, cooling collapses and the problem snowballs.

Because the two big root causes (airflow and refrigerant) require different fixes — and refrigerant work is EPA-licensed — this is rated a **pro** repair once the basic airflow checks are ruled out.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil of warm air and lets it ice over.
2. **Blocked return/supply vents or closed dampers** — furniture over returns, too many closed registers, or a collapsed flex duct starves airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and choke airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. This is the top *non-airflow* cause and needs a licensed tech.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / wrong fan speed** — a weak ECM motor, bad capacitor (on PSC blowers), or misconfigured airflow setting on an Infinity board reduces CFM across the coil.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Metering device (TXV/piston) failure** — a stuck expansion valve floods or starves the coil. Less common but not rare on older units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system in a safe state. **First, turn the thermostat to OFF for cooling and set the fan to ON** — running the blower helps melt the ice and prevents further compressor risk.

- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Confirm the thermostat is set correctly** — fan ON, cooling OFF while thawing. On Infinity thermostats, make sure you aren't in a mode that keeps the compressor running.
- **Walk the house and open closed supply registers.** Aim for keeping the vast majority open; over-closing registers restricts airflow.
- **Clear the return grille** of furniture, rugs, or drapes blocking it.
- **Look at the outdoor breaker and the disconnect near the condenser.** If the unit tripped, don't repeatedly reset it — one reset is fine, a repeated trip is a diagnostic clue for the tech.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** A thawing coil dumps a lot of water. Make sure the drain line is clear and the pan isn't overflowing; drain tablets help keep the line open.

Let the coil thaw completely (1–3 hours, sometimes longer) before restarting cooling. If it refreezes after a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Carrier tech should:

- **Confirm the coil is actually frozen** and let it fully thaw before taking readings — measurements on an iced coil are meaningless.
- **Measure static pressure and airflow (CFM)** across the coil, and check the blower motor and (on PSC units) the run capacitor. On Infinity/variable-speed systems they'll verify the programmed airflow setting matches the equipment.
- **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt or biofilm buildup.
- **Attach gauges and read superheat and subcooling.** Low charge shows as high superheat and low subcooling — this points to a refrigerant leak, not an airflow issue.
- **Perform a leak search** (electronic sniffer, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low, because simply "adding refrigerant" without fixing the leak is a temporary and wasteful fix.
- **Check the metering device (TXV/piston)** if pressures are odd but the charge and airflow are correct.

Sanity check: if a tech proposes "just adding a pound of Freon" without doing a leak search or checking airflow, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, visible ice on coil/lines | Frozen coil (any cause) | Set cooling OFF, fan ON; let it thaw | Diagnose root cause after thaw |
| Coil freezes again after clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | None — stop running it | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Dirty gray filter, weak airflow | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Confirm airflow restored |
| Registers closed / return blocked | Airflow restriction | Open vents, clear returns | Measure static pressure |
| Water overflowing near furnace | Thawing + clogged drain | Clear drain, use tablets | Clear line, check pan/float switch |
| Coil dirty even with new filter | Fouled evaporator coil | Light fin-comb cleaning if accessible | Professional coil cleaning |
| Blower runs weak or won't start | ECM motor / capacitor | Note the symptom | Test/replace motor or capacitor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY with tablets/wet-vac; $75–$250 pro.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $200–$500.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450 depending on method.
- **Refrigerant recharge (R-410A):** $150–$600+ depending on amount; R-410A pricing has risen sharply.
- **Leak repair (accessible joint vs. coil replacement):** $200–$800 for a repairable joint; a full evaporator coil replacement runs **$1,000–$2,500** including refrigerant.
- **Blower motor (ECM):** $400–$900; **run capacitor:** $150–$400.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900.

If the coil itself is leaking and the system uses phased-out R-22, replacing the whole system is often more economical than a coil-and-recharge repair — ask for both quotes.

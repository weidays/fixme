## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" is a condition, not a specific Carrier fault code — most Carrier air conditioners won't flash a dedicated number for it. You'll notice it as reduced or no cooling, ice or frost visible on the indoor coil or on the copper refrigerant line (the larger insulated one) at the air handler, and often water pooling as the ice melts.

The coil freezes when its surface temperature drops below 32°F. That happens for two broad reasons: **not enough warm indoor air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant / wrong pressure** in the system (a refrigerant problem). Both cause the coil to get too cold, condensation freezes, and the ice then blocks airflow further — a snowball effect that ends with a solid block of ice and zero cooling.

Because the two root causes (airflow vs. refrigerant) require very different repairs, and because refrigerant work is legally and technically pro-only, this is marked **pro severity**. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out the simple airflow causes yourself, but a persistent or recurring freeze needs a licensed HVAC tech.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or furniture over returns reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and restrict airflow between the fins.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge drops coil pressure and temperature. This is a leak that must be found and repaired, not just "topped off."
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong blower speed** — an ECM or PSC blower that isn't moving rated CFM lets the coil overcool. Carrier variable-speed (Infinity) systems can also under-run if a control setting or airflow tab is wrong.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating cooling when it's below ~60°F outdoors can freeze the coil on some systems.
7. **Clogged condensate drain causing a shutdown loop** — less a freeze cause, but a full pan/float switch can leave the system in odd states; worth checking alongside.
8. **Failed metering device (TXV/orifice)** — a stuck expansion valve overfeeds or underfeeds the coil, causing frost. This is a pro diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with cooling turned **off** first, so nothing is running on a frozen coil:

- **Turn the thermostat to OFF for cooling, then set the fan to ON (fan-only).** Running the blower without the compressor melts the ice faster and safely. Do not run cooling again until fully thawed.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open all supply registers and clear returns.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed doors aren't choking airflow. Aim for most vents open.
- **Check the breaker.** If the air handler or condenser tripped, a coil can freeze from a partially running system. Reset once; if it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the thermostat batteries** (if battery-powered) so it isn't behaving erratically.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** Melting ice produces a lot of water — make sure the pan isn't overflowing and the drain line isn't clogged. Drain tablets and a shop vac on the outdoor drain end can clear a simple clog.
- **Let it fully thaw** (1–24 hours) before restarting cooling. Once thawed with a fresh filter and open vents, run cooling and watch: if it refreezes, you have an airflow or refrigerant problem that needs a technician.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any float/safety switch. Refrigerant handling is pro-only and legally regulated.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will confirm and quantify the problem rather than guess:

- **Verify airflow first** — inspect filter, coil cleanliness, and measure static pressure or blower CFM. On Carrier Infinity/variable-speed systems they'll check the airflow configuration and blower operation via the control.
- **Connect gauges** to read suction and head pressure, and measure superheat and subcooling to determine whether the charge is correct, low (leak), or the metering device is misbehaving.
- **Leak search** if the charge is low — using electronic detectors, UV dye, or soap on fittings and coil. A proper repair finds and fixes the leak, then weighs in a new charge.
- **Inspect the metering device (TXV/orifice)** and coil temperature to confirm even feeding.
- **Check the blower motor and speed tap/settings** to confirm rated airflow.

You should expect a quote that names the actual cause — "low charge from a leak at the evaporator" or "dirty coil, insufficient airflow" — not just "your AC froze." If a tech offers only to add refrigerant without mentioning a leak search, ask why.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak cooling | Dirty/clogged filter | Turn cooling off, fan on, replace filter, thaw | Verify airflow, inspect coil |
| Ice on large copper line at air handler | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn cooling off, thaw, then call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Coil refreezes after clean filter | Dirty coil or weak blower | Fan-only thaw; open vents | Clean coil, test blower CFM |
| Water pooling / overflow after thaw | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, vacuum line, add tablets | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC when it's cold out | Avoid cooling below ~60°F outdoors | Confirm low-ambient limits |
| Frost pattern uneven on coil | Failing TXV/metering device | None — pro job | Diagnose and replace metering device |
| Infinity/variable-speed under-cooling & icing | Wrong airflow config or blower fault | None — pro job | Check control airflow settings, blower |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Simple airflow fixes are cheap; refrigerant and coil work are not.

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0–$50 DIY; $75–$250 for a service call.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450 depending on access and how dirty.
- **Blower motor repair/replacement:** $300–$900 (PSC) up to $600–$1,300 for ECM/variable-speed modules.
- **Refrigerant leak search + minor repair + recharge:** $500–$1,200+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type/amount.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ (coil, labor, refrigerant), higher for R-410A/R-454B systems and difficult access.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** typically $80–$200, often credited toward the repair.

Refrigerant costs are rising as older refrigerants are phased out, so a leak on an aging Carrier system may make replacement worth pricing.

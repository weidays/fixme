## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is not a numeric fault code on Carrier equipment — it is a physical condition inside your indoor air handler or furnace cabinet. Ice forms on the evaporator coil (the A-shaped coil the blower pushes air across) when the coil gets colder than the air passing over it can keep it.

Two things cause that: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant absorbing heat correctly**. Either way, coil surface temperature drops below freezing, moisture condenses and freezes, and the ice snowballs — blocking airflow and making the problem worse.

Symptoms you'll notice: weak or warm air from vents, higher humidity indoors, water pooling near the indoor unit as ice melts, hissing or bubbling near the coil, or visible frost on the copper refrigerant line (the larger insulated one) at the outdoor condenser.

**Why this is rated "pro":** the most common lasting fix — a refrigerant leak — legally requires an EPA-certified technician. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out airflow, but do not run a frozen unit for long: liquid refrigerant returning to the compressor can destroy it.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil of warm air. Cheap to fix.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many rooms closed off, furniture over returns, or a collapsed flex duct starves airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust cakes the coil fins, insulating them and reducing airflow. Requires professional cleaning.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the second most common real fault. Low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. Needs leak repair + recharge.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the blower isn't moving enough air. Common on ECM and PSC motors as they age.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — running cooling below ~60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil.
7. **Thermostat set too low or fan set to "On" incorrectly** — extended low-temp runtime, or a stuck contactor keeping the compressor running.
8. **Metering device (TXV or piston) failure** — restricts or over-feeds refrigerant, dropping coil temperature. Professional diagnosis and part swap.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **First, turn the system to Off (or Fan-only) and let the coil thaw completely — running it frozen risks compressor damage.**

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see light through it, replace it. Check monthly during cooling season.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to Cool and the setpoint is reasonable (not, say, 60°F). Set the fan to **On** temporarily to help the ice thaw faster with the compressor off.
- **Vents and returns:** Open all supply registers. Clear furniture, rugs or boxes off return grilles. Don't run with more than a couple of rooms closed off.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure the condenser isn't buried in leaves, grass or a mulch pile blocking airflow.
- **Condensate:** As the coil thaws it can dump a lot of water. Check the drain pan and drain line — a clogged line will overflow. You can clear a clogged line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain exit.
- **Breaker:** If the indoor blower isn't running at all, check the breaker for the air handler/furnace.

If a clean filter, open vents, and a full thaw fix it — great. If it re-freezes within a day or two, the cause is deeper: call a pro. **Do not repeatedly reset or restart a unit that keeps freezing.**

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier technician will:

1. **Confirm the thaw** — no accurate readings are possible on an iced coil. They may thaw it first or use fan-only.
2. **Measure airflow** — check filter, blower speed/amp draw, and static pressure across the coil to rule out a duct or blower problem.
3. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — read suction/liquid pressures and calculate **superheat and subcooling**. Low pressures point to undercharge or a metering issue.
4. **Weigh or estimate charge** and inspect for **leaks** with an electronic detector, UV dye, or bubble solution at joints, the coil, and line set.
5. **Inspect the coil** for dirt and the metering device (TXV/piston) for proper operation.
6. **Check blower components** — capacitor, motor amps, and (on ECM units) module fault history.

Sanity check: any quote to "just add refrigerant" without a **leak search** is a red flag. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up" — if it's low, it leaked out somewhere.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Replace filter, thaw fully | Verify airflow, check static pressure |
| Warm air, ice returns after thaw | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw, then stop running unit | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Frost on outdoor line set | Low charge or metering issue | Turn off, let thaw | Gauge readings, superheat/subcool |
| Water pooling by indoor unit | Melting ice / clogged drain | Clear drain with wet/dry vac | Clear/replace drain, coil clean |
| Blower weak or noisy | Failing motor / bad capacitor | Confirm blower runs at all | Test capacitor, replace motor |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Don't run cooling in cold | Confirm no other fault |
| Several closed rooms + ice | Insufficient return airflow | Open vents and returns | Evaluate duct sizing |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges. Actual cost varies by region, model, and refrigerant type (R-410A and newer R-32 systems differ).

- **New air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thaw + restart, no parts:** $0 DIY, or $90–$150 diagnostic if you call a pro
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (PSC) / $600–$1,200 (ECM)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100–$350
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and charge amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ (higher if the coil is under warranty but labor isn't, or refrigerant type is scarce)

A leaking coil that's out of parts warranty often pushes homeowners toward weighing repair vs. replacement, especially on older R-410A systems.

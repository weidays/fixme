## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a stored fault number on most Carrier equipment — it's a physical condition you'll see or feel. The indoor evaporator coil (inside your furnace or air handler) drops below freezing, and moisture in the air turns to frost or a solid block of ice on the coil and refrigerant lines.

Carrier's basic single-stage systems won't flash a code for this. On **Infinity** systems, the wall control may log related faults — low airflow, low suction, or a charge-related communication fault — but the ice itself is the underlying problem you're chasing.

A frozen coil almost always means one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant** (a charge or leak problem). Both drop coil temperature below the dew point and then below 32°F.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of warm return air, so it freezes.
2. **Blocked airflow from closed/covered vents or dirty return grilles** — reduced airflow has the same effect as a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and cut airflow through the fins.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — low pressure drops the coil's boiling temperature below freezing. Common on older systems.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / capacitor** — the fan can't push enough air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~60°F outdoor) — low outdoor temps can trigger freezing.
7. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** — restricts refrigerant flow, dropping coil temperature.
8. **Blend/duct problems or a collapsed flex duct** — chronic low airflow on one section.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system **off** so the coil can thaw. Never scrape ice off the coil.

- **Turn off cooling at the thermostat, set the fan to ON.** This blows room-temperature air over the coil and speeds thawing safely. Give it 1–3 hours.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. Note the size printed on the frame.
- **Open all supply registers and clear return grilles.** Move furniture, rugs, and drapes away from vents. Don't close off rooms to "save energy."
- **Check the thermostat setting.** Very low setpoints (60°F) in mild weather can freeze the coil — set it to 72–74°F.
- **Check the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and outdoor unit.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** A frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it thaws — make sure the pan isn't overflowing and the drain line isn't clogged. Cleaning tablets or a wet/dry vac on the drain line outlet can help.
- **After it's fully thawed and dry,** turn cooling back on and watch. If it re-freezes within a day, stop and call a pro.

Do **not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or bypass any safety switch — those are pro jobs and DIY refrigerant is illegal without EPA certification.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will confirm the coil is thawed first, then:

1. **Measure airflow / temperature split** across the coil (should be roughly 15–22°F difference between return and supply air).
2. **Inspect the blower wheel, motor, and capacitor** — a slow or dirty blower reduces airflow.
3. **Connect gauges and take pressure/temperature readings**, calculating superheat and subcooling to determine if the system is undercharged.
4. **Leak-search** with electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen if the charge is low — topping off a leaking system is a temporary fix, not a repair.
5. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt and the metering device (TXV/orifice) for correct operation.
6. On **Infinity** systems, read stored faults and live sensor data at the wall control.

If a quote skips the airflow and pressure diagnosis and jumps straight to "you need refrigerant," get a second opinion — refrigerant doesn't disappear unless there's a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil or copper line, weak airflow | Dirty filter | Replace filter, run fan to thaw | Confirm airflow, check blower |
| Coil freezes again after clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Shut off, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Some rooms warm, others cold; low airflow | Closed vents / duct restriction | Open all registers, clear returns | Inspect ducts, measure static pressure |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Thawed coil overflowing drain | Clear drain line, empty pan | Clear/replace drain, add safety switch |
| Fan runs but little air moves | Failing blower or capacitor | Note the symptom, call pro | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Coil dirty and matted with dust | Dirty evaporator coil | Improve filtration | Professional coil cleaning |
| Freezing only in mild/cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Turn off cooling in cool weather | Verify charge and controls |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual cost varies by region, system size, and access.

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Drain line clearing:** $0 DIY – $150–$250 professional.
- **Blower capacitor:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor (ECM/PSC):** $400–$1,000+ depending on type; ECM modules on variable-speed systems cost more.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $200–$700+ depending on system and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing varies).
- **Leak repair (line/coil):** $500–$1,500+.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ installed; more if the coil is under a sealed cabinet or the refrigerant type is being changed.
- **TXV/metering device replacement:** $400–$900.

A frozen coil caused by a filter is essentially free to fix. If it's a refrigerant leak or coil replacement, you're into major-repair territory — weigh it against the age of the system.

## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numeric fault code — Carrier ACs don't flash a specific code for it. It's a **physical symptom**: a layer of ice builds up on the indoor evaporator coil (inside your furnace cabinet or air handler) and sometimes on the copper refrigerant line running outside.

The evaporator coil is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. It normally runs cold but stays above freezing. When something drops the coil temperature below 32°F — either **too little warm air passing over it** or **too little refrigerant inside it** — condensation on the coil freezes instead of draining away. The ice then blocks even more airflow, so it snowballs: less cooling, more ice, and eventually near-zero output while the system runs nonstop.

Left running frozen, the biggest risk is **liquid refrigerant flooding back to the compressor**, which can destroy it. That's why this is rated a pro-level issue even though the first checks are DIY.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil of warm air.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — furniture over registers, closed doors, or a return that's undersized.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust build a film that insulates the coil and blocks airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — lower pressure means a colder coil. A leak never fixes itself; it needs a pro.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong fan speed** — including a failed capacitor or an ECM motor running slow.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** (below ~60°F) — the system isn't designed for it and the coil frosts.
7. **Clogged condensate drain triggering a float switch**, or dirty ductwork restricting flow.
8. **Metering device (TXV) or refrigerant charge problems** — an overcharge or a stuck valve, less common but real.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with cooling **turned off** at the thermostat first — running a frozen system risks the compressor.

- **Switch the thermostat to "Fan On" (cool OFF).** This blows room-temp air over the coil to thaw it. Full thaw can take 1–24 hours. Never chip at ice.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Open and unblock all supply registers and return grilles.** Move furniture, rugs, or drapes off them. Keep interior doors open for return airflow.
- **Look at the breaker.** If the outdoor unit tripped, note it — but don't repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** If there's standing water or the pan is full, the drain may be clogged; a float safety switch can shut the system down. You can vacuum the drain line's outdoor end with a wet/dry vac.
- **Confirm outdoor temperature.** If it's below ~60°F, that alone can frost the coil — wait for warmer weather.

If the coil refreezes after a clean filter and open vents, the cause is likely refrigerant or blower related — **call a pro.** Do not add refrigerant yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech won't just top off refrigerant and leave. Expect them to:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — readings are meaningless on an iced coil.
2. **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil and check blower speed, capacitor, and motor operation.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil and filter** for dirt buildup insulating the fins.
4. **Attach gauges and read superheat/subcooling** to judge refrigerant charge and metering-device behavior.
5. **Leak-search** if the charge is low — with electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen — rather than just adding refrigerant.
6. **Check the condensate drain and float switch.**

Sanity check: if a tech quotes a refrigerant recharge without mentioning a **leak search**, push back. Refilling a leaking system is a repeat bill and is discouraged by EPA rules for recurring leaks.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / closed vents | Replace filter, open all vents, run fan-only | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Coil refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn cooling off, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice on outdoor copper line | Low charge or airflow | Fan-only to thaw, then call | Gauge readings, seal leak |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Melting ice or clogged drain | Clear drain line, empty pan | Clear drain, test float switch |
| System runs constantly, little cooling | Blower or refrigerant issue | Fan-only, note behavior | Test blower/ECM, capacitor, charge |
| Freezing only in mild weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Turn off until warmer | Confirm no other fault |
| Dirty coil visible through access | Coil buildup | Leave to pro | Professional coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your area, model, and how much refrigerant your system holds all shift these numbers.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40 — often the whole fix.
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $85–$180, sometimes waived if you approve the repair.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Blower motor (ECM) replacement:** $400–$900+.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (older R-410A vs. newer refrigerants vary in price).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000+ installed, more if the coil is under warranty but labor isn't covered.
- **Compressor damage from running frozen (worst case):** $1,500–$2,800+ — the reason you shouldn't keep running an iced-up system.

If your Carrier system is under 10 years old, ask about the **parts warranty** — coils and compressors are often covered, though you'll still pay labor.

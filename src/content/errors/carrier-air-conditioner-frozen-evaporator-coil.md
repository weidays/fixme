## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a numbered Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition where ice forms on the indoor coil (usually inside the air handler or above the furnace). Your Carrier system may show no code at all, or you'll simply notice weak airflow, no cooling, and water on the floor when the ice melts.

The coil freezes when its surface temperature drops below freezing. That happens for one of two root reasons: **not enough warm air is moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **the refrigerant pressure is too low** (a refrigerant or metering problem). Either way, moisture in the air condenses and freezes into a block of ice that blocks airflow entirely — making the cooling problem worse the longer it runs.

This is rated **pro** because most root causes beyond a dirty filter require refrigerant gauges, leak detection, or component testing that homeowners can't safely do.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature until it freezes.
2. **Blocked return or supply vents** — closed registers, furniture over vents, or a collapsed return duct starve the coil of airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat exchange.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — lower pressure means a colder coil. Common in older R-410A and R-22 systems.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / wheel** — a weak or dust-clogged blower can't push enough air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — running cooling below about 60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or failing metering device (TXV or piston)** — over-feeds or under-feeds refrigerant to the coil.
8. **Undersized or crushed ductwork** — chronic airflow restriction that mimics a dirty filter.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner:

- **Turn cooling OFF, blower to FAN ON.** This is the most important step — it thaws the coil without running the compressor into ice. Leave it for several hours.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Open every supply register** and clear furniture, rugs, or boxes off vents and return grilles.
- **Confirm thermostat settings.** Make sure it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and the fan setting isn't stuck on AUTO with a fault.
- **Check the breaker** for the air handler and condenser; a tripped breaker can cause odd behavior.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** A thawing coil dumps a lot of water — make sure the drain line isn't clogged and the pan isn't overflowing. Drain tablets help prevent future clogs.
- **Look for ice** on the copper lines or coil. If you see ice, do NOT run cooling until it's fully melted.

Do **not** open the refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, or chip at ice with any tool. If a new filter and thawing don't hold, it's a pro job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — you can't read pressures on an iced system.
2. **Measure airflow and check the blower** — inspect the wheel for dust, verify motor amperage and speed tap.
3. **Inspect the coil and filter** for restriction.
4. **Connect refrigerant gauges** and measure superheat and subcooling to determine if the charge is correct.
5. **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or UV dye if the charge is low — adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.
6. **Test the metering device (TXV/piston)** for proper operation.
7. **Check duct static pressure** if airflow seems chronically poor.

If a tech proposes "just adding a pound of refrigerant" without a leak search, ask where the refrigerant went — a sealed system doesn't consume it.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Set FAN mode to thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, check blower |
| Coil refreezes after new filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop cooling, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water on floor near air handler | Melting ice / clogged drain | Thaw system, clear drain line | Clear drain, inspect pan |
| Whole coil iced, blower runs weak | Failing/dirty blower motor | Note if airflow is weak on FAN | Clean wheel, test/replace motor |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC too cold outside | Stop cooling below ~60°F outdoor | Advise on low-ambient limits |
| Frost near one spot on coil | Stuck metering device | Call pro | Test/replace TXV or piston |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and system age):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Clearing a condensate drain:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (more for ECM/variable-speed motors)
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair:** $200–$1,500+ depending on leak location
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $150–$600 (R-410A); older R-22 systems cost much more
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $300–$700
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+

If the system is old and the leak is in the coil itself, a tech may recommend weighing coil replacement against a full system replacement.

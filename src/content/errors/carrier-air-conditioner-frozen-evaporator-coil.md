## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition. The indoor evaporator coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) has dropped below freezing, so moisture in the air condenses and turns to ice on the coil instead of draining away.

Once ice builds up, it blocks airflow, which makes the coil even colder, which makes more ice. The result: little or no cold air, water pooling around the indoor unit as it thaws, and — if left running — a real risk of compressor damage from liquid refrigerant returning to it.

On **Carrier Infinity** systems, a related airflow or low-charge condition may appear as a fault on the wall control. On **base Comfort and Performance** models there is no code — you diagnose it by the ice and symptoms.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant (leak)** — low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This never fixes itself and requires a licensed tech.
3. **Blocked or dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate them and restrict airflow.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / capacitor** — not enough air moving across the coil.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — starves the system of return air.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~62°F outdoor) — low outdoor temps drop coil pressure and cause freezing.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — a backed-up drain can trip a safety switch or contribute to icing issues on some setups.
8. **Metering device (TXV/orifice) failure** — feeds too much or too little refrigerant. A pro diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the system OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to OFF (or Emergency Heat off) and the fan to ON. This thaws the coil without running the compressor. Allow 1–24 hours.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A gray, matted filter is the #1 culprit. Swap in a clean one of the correct size and MERV rating.
- **Open and unblock all supply vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't starving airflow. Keep return grilles clear.
- **Check the breaker and thermostat batteries.** Confirm the system has power and the thermostat is calling correctly.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** If you see standing water, clear the drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination and check the float switch.
- **Look for visible ice** on the indoor coil, the copper suction line (the larger insulated line at the outdoor unit), or the outdoor service valve.

Do **not** run the system while the coil is iced, chip at the ice, add refrigerant yourself, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just recharge and leave. Expect them to:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — you can't measure a frozen system.
2. **Measure airflow** across the coil (static pressure) and inspect the blower wheel and motor.
3. **Inspect the filter and coil** for restriction and cleanliness.
4. **Connect gauges and read superheat/subcooling** to determine if the charge is low, high, or correct.
5. **Leak-search** with electronic detector or dye if the charge is low — a recharge without a leak fix is a temporary band-aid.
6. **Check the metering device (TXV/orifice)** and blower capacitor.
7. On **Infinity** systems, read any logged faults on the wall control.

If a tech offers to "just add a pound of refrigerant" without searching for a leak, that's a red flag — refrigerant doesn't get used up; it leaks out.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / restricted airflow | Replace filter, thaw, open vents | Measure static pressure, clean coil |
| Ice on outdoor suction line, low cooling | Low refrigerant / leak | Shut off, thaw, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Coil re-freezes after thaw | Unresolved airflow or low charge | Recheck filter/vents | Full airflow + charge diagnosis |
| Water around indoor unit | Thawing coil / clogged drain | Clear drain, thaw, mop up | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Weak airflow, no obvious ice yet | Failing blower / capacitor | Verify filter clean | Test blower motor & capacitor |
| Freezing only in cool weather | Running AC below ~62°F outdoor | Don't run AC in cold temps | Verify low-ambient limits |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $100–$400
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (ECM/PSC):** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far costlier)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$800
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+
- **Compressor replacement** (if flood-back damaged it): $1,500–$3,000+

The cheap fix (filter + thaw) resolves a large share of frozen-coil calls. The expensive outcomes come from running the system frozen for too long and killing the compressor — which is why shutting it off early matters.

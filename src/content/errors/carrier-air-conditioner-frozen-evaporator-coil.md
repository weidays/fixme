## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a numbered fault code—it's a physical condition your Carrier air conditioner develops when the indoor coil gets cold enough for condensation to freeze into ice. Most Carrier thermostats and boards won't flash a specific code for it; you'll notice weak or no cooling, ice on the copper lines or coil, and water pooling as the ice melts.

The root problem is almost always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant** in the system. Both drop the coil temperature below freezing, and the humidity in your home's air freezes onto it. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil and blocks airflow further, so the problem snowballs—which is why a frozen coil often means the AC has been struggling for hours or days.

Because a frozen coil can be caused by a refrigerant leak (which requires EPA-certified handling) or blower failure, we rate this **pro** severity. You can safely thaw it and rule out simple causes, but the actual repair usually needs a technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow and drops its temperature below freezing.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too-few supply/return vents** — Furniture over registers, closed room dampers, or undersized returns reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and block airflow, even with a clean filter.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature, freezing it. This is a professional repair; refrigerant is never a DIY top-off.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor** — A weak PSC motor, failing ECM module, or bad capacitor moves too little air. Board settings can also mis-configure blower speed.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — Operating cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F can freeze the coil; use only in appropriate conditions.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — Less common as a freeze cause, but standing water and restricted airflow can contribute.
8. **Metering device (TXV / piston) stuck or failing** — Restricts refrigerant flow into the coil, dropping temperature. Pro diagnosis required.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat mode to OFF and the fan to ON. This stops the freezing and thaws the coil safely—do this first.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A gray, matted, or months-old filter is the likely culprit. Slide in a clean one of the correct size (see the frame for dimensions).
- **Walk the house and open every supply register.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed doors aren't blocking returns. Aim for at least 80% of vents open.
- **Confirm the thermostat setpoint is reasonable.** Setting it very low (e.g., 62°F) makes the system run continuously and freeze more easily.
- **Check the condensate line and pan.** If you see standing water or a full pan, the drain may be clogged. You can pour drain tablets or use a wet/dry vac on the drain line outside.
- **Verify the breaker is on.** After thawing, make sure the indoor and outdoor units both have power.

Do **not** chip or scrape ice off the coil, and do **not** keep running the AC while it's iced. If it re-freezes within a day of a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will confirm you're not overpaying by working through this sequence:

- **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before any measurements—readings taken on an iced system are meaningless.
- **Measure airflow / temperature split.** They'll check the temperature drop across the coil (typically 15–22°F) and inspect blower speed, filter, and coil cleanliness.
- **Inspect the blower.** Motor amp draw, capacitor test (PSC units), or ECM module diagnostics; on Infinity/Performance systems, they'll verify the board's programmed airflow settings.
- **Attach gauges and read pressures.** Low suction pressure and superheat/subcooling numbers point to low charge or a metering-device fault.
- **Leak search if charge is low.** Electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test to find the leak—refilling without finding the leak is a red flag.
- **Inspect the metering device (TXV/piston)** if pressures suggest a restriction.

Ask the tech to show you the pressure and superheat readings. "Just needs a recharge" without a leak search means the problem will return.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn cooling off, fan on; replace filter | Measure charge & airflow; find root cause |
| Weak cooling, filter is filthy | Clogged filter | Replace filter, let coil thaw | Verify no further airflow restriction |
| Refreezes days after clean filter | Refrigerant leak or dirty coil | Stop running AC; call pro | Leak search, coil clean, recharge |
| Water pooling near indoor unit | Melting ice or clogged drain | Clear condensate line, thaw coil | Confirm drain and coil condition |
| Blower runs weak or intermittently | Failing motor/capacitor/ECM | None—call pro | Test/replace blower components |
| Freezing in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run cooling in cold weather | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US prices including labor; your area and system size affect the total.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250 (or ~$10 DIY with tablets/vac)
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement (PSC):** $400–$700
- **ECM blower motor/module (variable-speed):** $600–$1,200
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+

If your system uses older R-22 refrigerant, recharge costs are significantly higher and replacement may be more economical—ask for a written comparison.

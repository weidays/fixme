## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numbered Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition your air conditioner develops. The evaporator coil is the indoor coil (inside your air handler or above your furnace) where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. When something goes wrong, that coil drops below freezing and moisture in the air turns to ice on it.

Once ice forms, it blocks airflow, which makes the coil even colder, which makes more ice — a runaway cycle. The end result is an AC that blows warm or no air, ice or water around the indoor unit, and in the worst case, a damaged compressor.

Because Carrier equipment rarely displays a specific code for this, you'll usually notice it as **weak/warm airflow, ice on the copper lines, or water leaking from the indoor unit** rather than a lit code. This page is marked **pro** because the two most common root causes — low refrigerant and airflow faults — often need professional tools to confirm and fix.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of airflow, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Restricted airflow elsewhere** — closed or blocked supply/return vents, collapsed flex duct, or a dirty blower wheel.
3. **Low refrigerant charge (often a leak)** — low pressure lowers the coil's temperature and freezes it. This requires an EPA-certified tech.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust film insulates the coil and disrupts airflow and heat transfer.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / wrong fan speed** — reduced CFM across the coil. Common with aging PSC or misconfigured ECM motors.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating cooling below roughly 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck metering device (TXV) or plugged filter drier** — less common, but starves the coil of proper refrigerant flow.
8. **Clogged condensate drain** — indirectly; a backed-up drain plus a wet coil can accelerate icing on some systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are homeowner-safe. Do **not** open the refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any switch.

- **Turn the system to FAN ONLY.** Set the thermostat to Off for cooling and the fan to On. This melts the ice — the first and most important step.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the #1 cause. Swap in a clean one of the correct size.
- **Open and unblock all vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs.
- **Check the thermostat setting.** Don't run cooling when it's cold outside (below ~60°F).
- **Look at the breaker.** If the unit tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** If you see standing water, a wet/dry vac on the drain line outlet can clear a clog.
- **Let it thaw completely before restarting.** Restarting on ice just refreezes and can flood the drain pan.

If it refreezes after a clean filter and full thaw, the cause is deeper — call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech works from airflow to refrigerant, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirms the thaw** and inspects the coil, filter, and blower compartment.
2. **Measures airflow / static pressure** to rule out duct restrictions, a dirty blower wheel, or wrong ECM/PSC fan speed.
3. **Inspects the evaporator coil** for dirt film that blocks heat transfer.
4. **Connects gauges and takes superheat/subcooling readings** to determine if the charge is correct — this is how they confirm low refrigerant.
5. **If low, performs a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just "topping off." Beware any quote that adds refrigerant without finding the leak.
6. **Checks the metering device (TXV/piston)** and filter drier for restrictions.

A legitimate diagnosis should identify the *root cause*, not just melt the ice and recharge. If a quote is just "add refrigerant," ask where the leak is.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, ice on copper lines | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Fan-only thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, static pressure test |
| Coil refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw fully, then stop and call a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water leaking from indoor unit | Thawing ice / clogged drain | Vac the drain line, place towels | Clear drain, correct root cause |
| Ice + weak airflow, filter is clean | Dirty coil or slow blower | Thaw, inspect visible coil | Clean coil, test/replace blower motor |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run cooling when cold out | Confirm no low-ambient control needed |
| Hissing + oily residue on lines | Refrigerant leak | Do not touch — call a pro | Locate/repair leak, evacuate, recharge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement (PSC):** $300–$650
- **Blower motor / ECM module replacement:** $600–$1,200
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant recharge (with leak repair):** $300–$800 depending on refrigerant type (R‑410A vs. older) and amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+
- **Diagnostic service call:** $80–$200 (often credited toward the repair)

A frozen coil caused only by a dirty filter costs you nothing but a filter. Refrigerant and coil issues are where costs climb — and repeated freezing that damages the compressor is far more expensive, which is why you should stop running a frozen unit.

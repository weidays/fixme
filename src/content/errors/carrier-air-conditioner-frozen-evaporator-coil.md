## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition your air conditioner develops when the indoor coil drops below freezing and moisture in the air turns to ice on it. Carrier control boards don't usually flash a specific number for this; you notice it by ice on the copper lines, weak or no cold air, or water pooling near the indoor unit as the ice melts.

The evaporator coil is designed to run cold, but not below freezing. When something disrupts the balance of airflow and refrigerant across the coil, its surface temperature falls, ice forms, and the ice then insulates the coil so it cools even worse. Left running, this can slug liquid refrigerant back to the compressor — a leading cause of compressor failure, which is why this problem is rated pro.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Blocked or dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil itself reduce heat transfer.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing.
4. **Failing or slow blower motor** — a weak or dying blower, or a wrong-speed setting, starves the coil of airflow.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — reduces total system airflow.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below about 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil on many systems.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** (indirect) — water backup can accompany freezing and worsen the mess.
8. **Dirty or failing metering device / TXV** — a stuck expansion valve mis-meters refrigerant. Board and valve behavior vary by model.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan to ON.** At your thermostat, switch the system from Cool to Off but set the fan to ON. This thaws the coil safely — running the blower without cooling melts the ice fastest.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A gray, matted, or clogged filter is the top cause. Replace it if you can't see light through it.
- **Look at your supply and return vents.** Open any closed registers and remove furniture, rugs, or curtains blocking them. Don't close off more than a couple of vents.
- **Check the thermostat setting.** Make sure it isn't set unrealistically low (like 60°F), which can promote freezing.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** If you see standing water, clear visible debris and pour drain tablets in the line. A wet floor near the indoor unit is a sign to act.
- **Check the breaker.** If the system tripped, you can reset it once — but do not repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.
- **Let it fully thaw before retesting.** Wait until all ice is gone (1–3 hours, sometimes longer) before switching back to Cool.

Do **not** open the refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or handle the sealed lines — that requires EPA certification and specialized gauges.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the root cause instead of just thawing the coil:

- Verifies the coil is fully thawed, then measures **airflow** across the coil and checks blower speed and amp draw.
- Inspects the **evaporator coil and filter** for dirt, biofilm, or crushed fins.
- Connects **gauges** to read suction and head pressures and calculates **superheat and subcooling** to judge the refrigerant charge.
- If charge is low, performs a **leak search** (electronic sniffer, UV dye, or bubble test) — because simply "topping off" a leak is a temporary and improper fix.
- Tests the **metering device / TXV** operation and checks the **condensate drain** for backup.
- Reviews thermostat settings and outdoor temperature to rule out cool-weather operation.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or an airflow check, ask why — a frozen coil often isn't a charge problem at all.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper line at indoor unit | Low airflow (dirty filter) | Turn cool off/fan on, replace filter | Confirm airflow, clean coil |
| Weak cold air, coil frosts over | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw, replace filter | Professionally clean coil |
| Coil refreezes soon after thawing | Low refrigerant leak | Stop running unit, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Air barely moves from vents | Failing/slow blower motor | Check filter and vents | Test blower motor, capacitor, speed |
| Water pooling near furnace/indoor unit | Melting ice + clogged drain | Clear drain, add tablets | Flush drain, verify pan safety switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Outdoor temp too low | Avoid AC below ~60°F | Verify no low-ambient issue |
| No cold air at all, no ice yet | Multiple / charge issue | Basic checks only | Full pressure and charge diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual pricing varies by region and model.

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Coil thaw:** $0 if you do it yourself.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450 depending on access.
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900+ (variable-speed ECM motors on Infinity/Performance systems cost more).
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100–$400.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+, more if the coil itself leaks and must be replaced.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800+ including refrigerant.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$1,000.

The cheap fixes (filter, vents, thaw) cost almost nothing. Refrigerant and coil work is where bills climb — get the leak found and quoted before authorizing a recharge.

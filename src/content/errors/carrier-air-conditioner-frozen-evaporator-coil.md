## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a numbered fault code on most Carrier units — it's a physical condition. The evaporator coil (indoors, in the air handler or above the furnace) gets so cold that condensation on it freezes into ice instead of draining away.

The root cause is almost always the same physics: **either not enough warm air is moving across the coil, or there isn't enough refrigerant to properly absorb heat.** Both drop the coil below freezing.

On **Carrier Infinity systems**, the wall control may flag a related fault (low suction pressure, or an airflow/CFM warning) — but it won't say "frozen coil" in those words. On **Comfort and Performance base models**, there's no diagnostic display at all; you'll notice weak airflow, no cooling, ice on the copper lines, or water leaking near the indoor unit.

Left running, a frozen coil can push liquid refrigerant back into the compressor and destroy it — which is why this is rated a pro-level problem.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant charge (usually a leak)** — less refrigerant means lower pressure and a colder coil. Requires a licensed tech.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust film insulate the coil and choke airflow.
4. **Blocked or closed returns/supply vents** — too many closed registers, blocked returns, or crushed ductwork starve the coil of air.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / wrong fan speed** — a weak ECM motor or misconfigured airflow setting moves too little air.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating cooling below ~60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil on many systems.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — a full drain pan can contribute in some setups, though it's more often a symptom.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV/piston)** — feeds the wrong amount of refrigerant; a diagnostic call.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with cooling **turned off** so the coil can thaw:

- **Turn the thermostat to OFF (cooling), but set the FAN to ON.** This runs the blower to thaw the ice faster and safely. Give it 1–3 hours (up to 24 for heavy ice).
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Walk your vents.** Open any closed supply registers and make sure furniture, rugs, or curtains aren't blocking return grilles.
- **Check the outdoor unit.** Clear leaves, grass, or debris off the condenser fins so it can shed heat.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** If you see standing water or a full pan, the drain line may be clogged — clear the visible outlet if you can reach it.
- **Confirm the breaker and thermostat batteries.** A tripped breaker or dead thermostat batteries can cause odd cooling behavior.

Once fully thawed, switch cooling back on and watch it. If it re-freezes within a day, you have a deeper problem — **stop running it and call a technician.** Do not keep resetting or restarting a unit that keeps freezing.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will:

- **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — measurements are meaningless on iced equipment.
- **Measure airflow** and check blower motor speed and amp draw, and verify the fan is set to the correct CFM for your tonnage.
- **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup insulating the fins.
- **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to determine if the charge is low — the definitive test for a refrigerant problem.
- **Leak-search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if refrigerant is low, because simply "topping off" a leaking system is a code violation and a temporary bandage.
- **Check the metering device (TXV or fixed piston)** if pressures are abnormal but the charge is correct.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or airflow check is a red flag — insist they rule out the free/cheap causes first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, ice on indoor coil | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents, thaw | Verify airflow, check duct restriction |
| Ice on the copper line at outdoor unit | Low refrigerant / leak | Shut off cooling, thaw, then call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water pooling near air handler | Melted ice or clogged drain | Clear visible drain outlet | Clear drain, flush, treat pan |
| Re-freezes within a day of thawing | Refrigerant, blower, or coil issue | Stop running unit; call pro | Full airflow + charge diagnosis |
| Coil freezes only on cool nights | Marginal charge or airflow | Raise thermostat setpoint | Measure charge, verify airflow |
| Dirty film all over coil fins | Dirty evaporator coil | Light no-rinse cleaner if accessible | Deep coil clean, possible pull-and-clean |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, vary by region and system size):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Service call / diagnostic fee:** $80–$180
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement (ECM):** $500–$1,000+
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing varies)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+, higher if the coil is under a matched Carrier warranty vs. out of warranty
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900

If your system uses older refrigerant or the coil is corroded from a leak, replacement often makes more sense than repeated repairs — ask your tech to price both.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition you'll usually spot as ice on the indoor coil, the copper refrigerant lines, or a puddle of water once it melts. Most Carrier thermostats won't display an error for it, though variable-speed **Infinity** systems may occasionally log an airflow-related fault.

The evaporator coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. When something disrupts that heat exchange — too little airflow or too little refrigerant — the coil surface drops below freezing and condensation turns to ice. That ice blocks airflow further, which makes the problem snowball fast.

The result: warm air from your vents, weak or no airflow, water around the indoor unit, and — if ignored — a damaged compressor. **This is a "pro" severity issue** because the two most common root causes (refrigerant leaks and blower/motor failures) require a licensed technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of the warm return air it needs, and it ices over.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharged systems run the coil too cold. If the coil re-freezes after every thaw, suspect this. Requires EPA-certified repair.
3. **Blocked or restricted airflow** — closed/blocked supply vents, crushed flex duct, or a dirty evaporator coil itself.
4. **Failing or dirty blower motor / wheel** — a weak blower can't move enough air across the coil. Variable-speed Infinity units may flag this.
5. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating cooling below ~60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
6. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/orifice)** — an expansion valve that won't feed refrigerant correctly. A technician diagnosis.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — less a cause than a companion symptom; a backed-up drain plus ice means a soaked, restricted coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do — no refrigerant, no opened panels beyond the filter access.

- **Turn cooling OFF, set fan to ON.** This stops the freeze and speeds thawing by pushing room air over the ice. Leave it until all ice is gone (up to 24 hours).
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open all supply and return vents.** Confirm nothing (furniture, rugs, closed dampers) is blocking airflow in any room.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's set to a reasonable temperature and not calling for cooling on an unusually cold day. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Look at the breaker.** If the outdoor unit tripped, the indoor blower may still run — leave it and let the coil thaw before resetting anything.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** Clear standing water; a shop vac on the drain line outlet can pull a clog. Add drain tablets to prevent regrowth.
- **Do NOT** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or run cooling repeatedly hoping it clears. Repeated runs while frozen risk compressor damage.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will confirm your quote reflects real diagnosis, not guessing:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — you can't measure a frozen system accurately.
2. **Measure airflow and static pressure** across the coil to rule out duct/blower restriction.
3. **Inspect the blower wheel and motor** for dirt, wear, or (on Infinity/variable-speed) commanded-vs-actual RPM faults.
4. **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to determine if the charge is correct.
5. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low — electronic sniffer, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test. A recharge without finding the leak is a red flag.
6. **Check the metering device (TXV/orifice)** for proper operation.
7. **Verify the condensate drain** clears freely.

Expect them to explain *why* the coil froze — "I topped off the refrigerant" without a leak search means it'll freeze again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil/lines, warm vents | Dirty filter or airflow block | Fan ON to thaw, replace filter, open vents | Airflow/static test if it recurs |
| Coil re-freezes days after thaw | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw and stop cooling until repaired | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow at all vents | Failing/dirty blower motor | Check/replace filter | Clean or replace blower motor/wheel |
| Water pooling around air handler | Melted ice + clogged drain | Clear drain with shop vac, tablets | Clear line, confirm coil cause |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Switch off cooling when it's cold out | Advise on low-ambient operation |
| Freezes with clean filter & open vents | TXV/metering device fault | None — call a pro | Diagnose and replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your actual price varies by region, model, and refrigerant type (R-410A older units vs. newer R-454B on some 2025+ Carrier systems).

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain clearing (DIY):** $0–$25 in supplies; $75–$200 for a pro
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM/variable-speed higher)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500 if the coil itself is damaged or leaking
- **Compressor replacement (if freeze damaged it):** $1,500–$2,800 — the reason not to keep running a frozen unit

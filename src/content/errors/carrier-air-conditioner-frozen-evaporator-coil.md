## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is a symptom, not a numbered Carrier fault code. Most Carrier air conditioners — including Comfort and Performance split systems — have no display that reports icing directly. You'll notice it as reduced or no cooling, ice on the copper lines near the indoor unit, or water pooling near the furnace/air handler after the ice melts.

The evaporator coil is the indoor coil that absorbs heat from your home's air. It normally runs cold but above freezing. When something disrupts the balance of airflow or refrigerant, the coil surface drops below 32°F, condensation freezes, and the ice sheet blocks airflow — which makes the problem snowball until the whole coil is a block of ice.

On **Carrier Infinity** systems with a communicating control, you may see a general cooling or airflow fault logged, but the board does not have a dedicated "frozen coil" code. Treat any ice you can see as the primary evidence.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked return vents, closed supply registers, or a dirty blower wheel** — same effect as a dirty filter: not enough air moving across the coil.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — reduces coil pressure and temperature, causing icing even with good airflow. Requires a technician.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the fins and reduce heat transfer.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the fan can't move enough air. More common on older ECM or PSC blower units.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~60°F outdoor) — outdoor temps too low for the system to operate correctly.
7. **Clogged condensate drain causing a float switch trip or water backup** — can accompany icing as melted ice overwhelms the drain.
8. **Metering device (TXV/piston) failure** — less common; feeds the wrong amount of refrigerant to the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This blows room-temperature air over the coil to thaw it safely. Do not chip or scrape the ice.
- **Check and replace the air filter** if it's gray or clogged. This is the fix for a large share of frozen-coil calls.
- **Confirm the thermostat** is set to COOL with a reasonable temperature — not set unusually low, which invites over-cooling and icing.
- **Walk the house and open all supply registers** and clear anything blocking return grilles (furniture, rugs, drapes).
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan** near the indoor unit. If you see standing water, clear the visible drain line opening; a clogged drain can trip the safety float switch and shut cooling off.
- **Check the breaker** for the indoor blower/furnace and the outdoor unit if the system is completely dead after a shutdown.

Do **not** open the refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or run the AC repeatedly while it's iced — you can destroy the compressor. If a clean filter and full thaw don't fix it, that points to refrigerant or blower problems that need a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier technician will:

1. Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing (running a frozen system gives false readings).
2. Measure airflow — inspecting the blower wheel, motor amp draw, and static pressure to rule out an airflow restriction.
3. Attach gauges and take **superheat and subcooling** readings to judge refrigerant charge without just "topping off."
4. If charge is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than simply adding refrigerant.
5. Inspect the evaporator coil and condensate drain for dirt and blockage.
6. On Infinity/communicating systems, pull fault history from the control to check for related blower or low-pressure events.

Be cautious of any quote that adds refrigerant without finding a leak — refrigerant does not get "used up," so a low charge means there's a leak to fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil/lines, weak cooling | Dirty filter, low airflow | Turn cooling off, fan on to thaw; replace filter | Verify airflow, static pressure, blower wheel |
| Coil re-freezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running unit; call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Warm air, outdoor unit running | Frozen coil blocking airflow | Thaw fully before restart | Diagnose root cause after thaw |
| Water pooling near air handler | Melted ice + clogged drain | Clear visible drain opening | Clear drain, check float switch, coil |
| Weak airflow at every vent | Blower motor/capacitor failing | Check filter and vents first | Test motor amps, capacitor, ECM module |
| Icing only in mild/cool weather | Running AC too cold outside | Don't run AC below ~60°F outdoor | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and system age):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Coil thaw + inspection service call:** $75–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $75–$250
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (PSC or ECM):** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis + repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 (accessible fitting leaks are cheaper; coil or buried-line leaks cost more)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ depending on model and refrigerant type
- **Compressor damage from repeated running while frozen:** $1,500–$3,500+ (why you should never run an iced unit)

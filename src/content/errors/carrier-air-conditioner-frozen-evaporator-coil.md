## What this code means

Most Carrier air conditioners do not display a dedicated "frozen coil" code — the symptom shows up as an iced-over indoor evaporator coil, weak or no cold air, and often a puddle when the ice melts. On **Infinity variable-speed systems**, the SAM/Infinity control *may* log an airflow or coil-temperature-related fault, but the exact code and wording varies by control board and firmware, so treat any logged fault as a starting point rather than a definitive diagnosis.

The physics is the same regardless of model: the evaporator coil is cold by design, and when either **airflow across it drops** or **refrigerant charge drops**, the coil surface falls below 32°F. Humidity in the air then freezes onto the coil instead of draining away, and the ice keeps growing until the whole coil is blocked — which stops cooling entirely and can slug liquid refrigerant back to the compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted return air starves the coil of warm air and lets it ice over.
2. **Blocked airflow elsewhere** — closed/blocked supply registers, a collapsed or crushed return duct, or a dirty blower wheel.
3. **Failing blower motor or capacitor** — a slow or non-running indoor blower cuts airflow across the coil.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — a system that is undercharged runs the coil colder than designed and freezes it. This is a sealed-system repair.
5. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust film on the coil insulates it and reduces heat pickup.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating cooling when it is below roughly 60°F outside can freeze the coil on many systems.
7. **Metering device (TXV/piston) problem** — a stuck or failing expansion valve feeds refrigerant incorrectly and can freeze the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This is the single most important safe step — it thaws the coil (allow several hours) and prevents further damage. Do not keep running cooling on a frozen coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A visibly gray or clogged filter is the likely culprit. Use the size printed on the old one.
- **Open and unblock all supply registers** and make sure return grilles aren't covered by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
- **Check the thermostat** — confirm it's set to Cool and to a reasonable temperature, and replace batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Check the breaker** for the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor unit; reset once if tripped, but don't repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** A frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it thaws — make sure the drain line isn't clogged and the pan overflow switch (if present) hasn't shut the system down. You can clear a drain line with a wet/dry vacuum and drain tablets.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bend/scrape the coil fins yourself — those are pro tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Carrier tech should:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — you can't measure a frozen system accurately.
2. **Measure airflow and static pressure** and inspect the blower wheel, motor, and capacitor for correct operation.
3. **Inspect the filter, coil cleanliness, and ductwork** for restrictions.
4. **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to determine whether the charge is correct, low, or the metering device is the problem.
5. **Leak-search** if the charge is low — using electronic detectors, dye, or a nitrogen pressure test — because simply "topping off" refrigerant without finding the leak is a temporary and improper fix.
6. On **Infinity systems**, pull any stored faults from the control to cross-check against physical findings.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or airflow check, ask why — that's a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil/line, weak airflow | Dirty filter or blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents, run fan to thaw | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Freezes again after clean filter | Failing blower motor/capacitor | Thaw, then stop cooling | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Ice + hissing/bubbling, poor cooling | Low refrigerant from a leak | Turn off, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Coil frozen, lots of water on thaw | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain line with wet/dry vac | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Stop cooling until warmer | Advise on low-ambient limits/kit |
| Cooling poor, coil dirty & iced | Dirty evaporator coil | Replace filter, thaw | Professional coil cleaning |
| Ice despite good airflow/charge | Stuck TXV/metering device | Turn off, call pro | Diagnose/replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **Air filter replacement:** $0–$30 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150–$250 pro
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $90–$200
- **Run-capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (variable-speed ECM motors on Infinity systems run higher)
- **Evaporator coil professional cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type; a coil replacement can exceed $1,500
- **TXV/metering device replacement:** $400–$900

If your system uses R-22 (older units), refrigerant costs are much higher, and a major sealed-system repair may push you toward replacement.

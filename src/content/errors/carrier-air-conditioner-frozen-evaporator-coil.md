## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numbered fault code — it's a physical condition where ice builds up on the indoor evaporator coil (the A-coil above your furnace or inside the air handler) of your Carrier air conditioner. On most Carrier equipment there is no dedicated error code for it; you notice it by weak cooling, ice on the copper lines, or water around the indoor unit when it thaws.

Infinity systems with a communicating control may log a *low suction pressure* or *low temperature* system malfunction that points toward the same problem, but standard Comfort and Performance units simply run poorly and freeze up silently.

The root cause is always the same physics: the coil gets colder than it should, moisture in the air freezes on it, and the ice snowballs until airflow chokes off completely. Two things drive it — **not enough air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant charge**.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil drop below freezing.
2. **Blocked return/supply vents or closed dampers** — same effect as a dirty filter: too little air across the coil.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat transfer.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge drops coil temperature and pressure, guaranteeing ice. This is a repair, not a "top-off."
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / wrong fan speed** — reduced CFM starves the coil of warm air.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below ~62°F outdoors can freeze the coil on units without low-ambient controls.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up onto the coil** — less common but possible.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV/piston)** — over-restricts refrigerant flow to the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with cooling turned **Off** at the thermostat:

- **Turn off cooling and run the fan.** Set the thermostat to Off, fan to On, to thaw the ice. Never run the compressor while the coil is iced.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Open all supply registers and return grilles.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed vents aren't strangling airflow. Keep at least 80% of vents open.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint (68–72°F), and replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breaker.** After a long freeze-up, verify the AC and air-handler breakers haven't tripped.
- **Clear the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan; a shop-vac on the condensate line outlet can clear a light clog.
- **Give it time to fully thaw.** A badly iced coil can take 1–3 hours. Wipe up meltwater so it doesn't overflow the pan.

If it cools normally after a new filter and a full thaw, you fixed it. **If it refreezes within a day, stop and call a pro** — that points to low refrigerant or a coil/blower problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Carrier tech should:

- Confirm the ice is fully thawed before testing (a frozen coil masks readings).
- Measure **airflow / static pressure** and blower speed to rule out restriction.
- Inspect the evaporator coil and blower wheel for dirt.
- Attach gauges and check **superheat and subcooling** against Carrier's charging chart for your model — this reveals low charge or a metering-device fault.
- **Leak-test** with electronic detector or dye if the charge is low, rather than just adding refrigerant.
- On Infinity systems, pull logged system faults from the control for supporting evidence.

Be wary of any quote that just tops off refrigerant without a leak search — low charge means a leak, and a refill will fail again in weeks or months.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents, run fan to thaw | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Refreezes within a day of thawing | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off cooling, call pro | Leak test, repair leak, recharge |
| Coil ices but filter is clean | Dirty evaporator coil | Inspect/clean accessible coil face | Deep coil cleaning, blower service |
| Water pooling indoors | Condensate drain clog + thaw | Clear drain line, shop-vac outlet | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Weak air, blower sounds slow | Failing blower / wrong speed | None safe | Test motor, correct fan speed |
| Freezes only in cool weather | No low-ambient control | Don't run AC below ~62°F | Advise on low-ambient kit if needed |
| Warm air, gauges show restriction | Faulty TXV/metering device | None | Replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $8–$30 — often the entire fix.
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor / capacitor replacement:** $150–$650 depending on motor type (ECM costs more).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $100–$350.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A).
- **Metering device (TXV) replacement:** $400–$900.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+, higher on Infinity/matched systems.

If your system is old and leaking through a corroded coil, ask your tech to compare a coil replacement against a full system replacement before spending on refrigerant repairs.

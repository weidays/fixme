## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a numbered fault code — it's a physical condition your Carrier air conditioner develops when the indoor coil drops below 32°F and condensation on it turns to ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, cooling collapses, and you'll often notice warm air from the vents, water on the floor near the indoor unit, or ice visible on the copper lines.

Carrier doesn't usually flash a dedicated code for this on standard boards. On some Infinity/communicating systems you may see cooling lockouts or airflow-related fault messages, but the freeze itself is diagnosed by observation, not a code number.

The two root drivers are almost always **restricted airflow** or **low refrigerant charge**. Both make the coil too cold. Because confirming refrigerant charge requires gauges and EPA certification, this is rated a **pro-level** problem once the basic airflow checks are ruled out.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Reduced airflow lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Blocked return vents or closed supply registers** — starves the coil of warm return air.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust buildup on the fins insulates and restricts airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant / refrigerant leak** — lower pressure drops coil temperature below freezing.
5. **Failing blower motor or wheel** — weak or uneven airflow across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below ~60°F outdoor temps can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — some systems throttle or shut the blower, worsening freeze cycles.
8. **Stuck or failing TXV / metering device** — over-feeds or under-feeds the coil (models with a TXV).

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner and often solve the problem:

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to Off (or Fan Only) to stop making ice and start thawing. Never chip at ice with tools.
- **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it's gray, dusty, or you can't see light through it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open all supply registers and clear return grilles.** Move furniture, rugs, or drapes blocking airflow. Keep interior doors open.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it isn't set to a very low temperature that runs the system nonstop.
- **Look at the breaker.** If tripped, that's a separate electrical issue — don't repeatedly reset it.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** Standing water or overflow points to a clogged drain line; clearing it with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain outlet is safe DIY.
- **After a full thaw, restart and watch.** If it re-freezes within a day, stop and call a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bend past the panel to touch electrical components.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing (a frozen coil gives false readings).
- **Measure airflow** — check filter, blower speed, static pressure, and coil cleanliness.
- **Connect gauges** to read suction/head pressures and calculate superheat and subcooling.
- **Verify refrigerant charge** against Carrier's spec for that model and metering device.
- **Leak-search** with electronic detector or dye if the charge is low.
- Inspect the **blower motor amp draw** and wheel condition.
- Check the **TXV or metering device** operation on applicable models.

If a tech proposes "just adding refrigerant" without a leak search, push back — refrigerant doesn't get consumed, so low charge means a leak that will recur.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, warm air from vents | Dirty filter / restricted airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Airflow + static pressure test |
| Coil re-freezes after filter change | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw and monitor | Deep-clean coil |
| Water on floor near indoor unit | Ice melting / clogged drain | Vac the drain line, dry area | Clear drain, check pan/switch |
| Freeze plus hissing or bubbling | Refrigerant leak | Turn off, call pro | Leak search + repair + recharge |
| Weak airflow at all vents | Failing blower motor/wheel | Check filter first | Test/replace blower motor |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Low outdoor temp operation | Avoid running AC below ~60°F | Verify charge and controls |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thaw + system reset:** $0 DIY, or $90–$180 diagnostic visit
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,600+ (higher for R-410A or newer refrigerant coils)

If the system is old (12–15+ years) with a major leak, ask about replacement economics before sinking large repair dollars into it.

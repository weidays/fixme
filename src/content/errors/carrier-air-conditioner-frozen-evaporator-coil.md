## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is a physical condition, not a numeric fault code — Carrier air conditioners do not display a dedicated "frozen coil" code on most systems. Instead you'll notice ice on the indoor coil or the refrigerant line, weak or no cooling, and possibly water pooling near the indoor unit as ice melts. Infinity systems with a communicating control may log airflow or low-suction related faults, but the ice itself is what you'll physically observe.

The evaporator coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) gets cold to absorb heat from your home's air. When something drops the coil temperature below freezing — usually low airflow or low refrigerant — condensation on the coil freezes into a solid block of ice. That ice then blocks airflow further, making the problem worse until the system cools almost nothing at all.

This is rated **pro** because the two most common root causes (a refrigerant leak or a failing blower) require licensed diagnosis and EPA-certified refrigerant handling. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out the easy airflow causes yourself first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil insulate it and reduce heat absorption, promoting freezing.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This requires professional leak repair and recharge; adding refrigerant without fixing the leak is only a temporary patch.
5. **Failing or weak blower motor** — a dying ECM/PSC blower or a slipping belt (on older units) can't push enough air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating cooling below about 60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain causing a safety shutoff** — some systems shut the compressor off when the drain float trips, which can be mistaken for or accompany freezing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF and set the fan to ON.** This is the single most important step — it stops making ice and lets the coil thaw. Never chip or scrape ice off the coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
- **Verify the thermostat setting.** Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint, and if it's a Carrier Infinity/Edge control, check for any displayed alerts.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm both the indoor and outdoor unit breakers are on and haven't tripped.
- **Inspect the condensate drain.** Look for standing water or a full drain pan; a wet/dry vac on the drain line outlet can clear a clog. Drain tablets help prevent recurrence.
- **Let it thaw completely** (up to 24 hours) before restarting cooling. Place towels to catch melting ice.

If a clean filter and full thaw restore cooling and it stays fixed, you're done. If it freezes again, stop and call a pro — that points to a refrigerant or airflow problem you shouldn't chase yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work systematically so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before taking measurements — readings on a frozen system are meaningless.
2. **Inspect airflow components** — filter, blower wheel cleanliness, blower motor operation and amp draw, and duct restrictions.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup that insulates it.
4. **Attach gauges and measure superheat/subcooling** to determine whether the refrigerant charge is correct. Low readings suggest a leak.
5. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low — a legitimate shop repairs the leak, they don't just top off refrigerant.
6. **Check blower speed settings and static pressure** to confirm the system is moving rated airflow.

Be cautious of any tech who simply adds refrigerant without leak testing, or who quotes a full coil/compressor replacement before confirming airflow is adequate.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, weak cooling, dirty filter | Restricted airflow from filter | Replace filter, thaw fully, restart | Verify airflow, check blower if it recurs |
| Coil freezes again after clean filter | Dirty coil or low refrigerant | Thaw, confirm vents open | Clean coil, measure charge, leak search |
| Ice on outdoor line, hissing, weak cooling | Refrigerant leak / low charge | Turn off, thaw, call pro | Leak detection, repair, EPA recharge |
| Weak airflow from all vents, coil freezes | Failing blower motor | Thaw and observe fan operation | Test blower amp draw, replace motor |
| Water around indoor unit, possible shutoff | Melting ice + clogged drain | Clear drain with wet/dry vac | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Freezes in cool/spring weather | Running AC in low outdoor temps | Don't run cooling below ~60°F outside | Confirm no other cause |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and system variation applies):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain clearing (DIY or service):** $0 DIY – $150 service call
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak detection:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A/R-454B systems vary)
- **Blower motor (ECM) replacement:** $400–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500+ if the coil itself is leaking or corroded

The cheap fixes (filter, thaw, drain) are genuinely DIY. Anything involving refrigerant is legally restricted to EPA-certified technicians — that cost isn't optional.

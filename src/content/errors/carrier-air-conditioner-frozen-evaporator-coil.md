## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numeric fault code—it's a physical condition where ice forms on the indoor evaporator coil (the A-coil above your furnace or inside your air handler). Most Carrier air conditioners have no dedicated sensor for it, so your base or mid-tier system won't flash a code. Instead you'll notice weak or warm airflow, water around the indoor unit, or visible ice on the copper lines.

On **Carrier Infinity** systems, the communicating thermostat may log related faults (low airflow, low suction pressure) that point to the same root causes, but "frozen coil" itself is diagnosed by observation, not a stored code.

A coil freezes when it gets colder than 32°F—which happens when either **not enough warm air** moves across it, or the **refrigerant charge/pressure is wrong**. Ice then blocks airflow entirely, making cooling worse in a downward spiral.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked return/supply vents or closed dampers** — reduces airflow across the coil the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust buildup on the coil fins insulates it and chokes airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low pressure lowers the coil's boiling temperature, so it freezes. Requires a technician.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — moves too little air. More common on ECM (variable-speed) blowers going bad.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~60°F outdoors) or setting the thermostat too low overnight.
7. **Metering device (TXV/orifice) failure** — over-feeds or restricts refrigerant, causing localized freezing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

**First: turn cooling OFF at the thermostat, but set the fan to ON.** This runs room-temperature air over the coil to melt the ice safely. Do not chip at ice—you can puncture the coil.

- **Air filter** — pull it and inspect. If it's gray or clogged, replace it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Vents and registers** — walk the house and open any closed supply vents; clear furniture or rugs off return grilles.
- **Thermostat** — make sure it's not set unreasonably low (below 68°F on a cool day can freeze the coil).
- **Breaker** — after thawing, confirm the AC breaker and furnace/air-handler switch are on.
- **Condensate drain** — a frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it melts. Check the drain pan and clear the line; use a wet/dry vac on the drain outlet if the pan is overflowing.
- **After a full thaw (4–24 hrs)**, restart cooling and watch. If it re-freezes within a day, stop and call a pro.

Do **not** open refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, or bypass any controls—those are licensed-technician tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the coil is fully thawed, then:

- Measures **static pressure and airflow (CFM)** to confirm the blower moves enough air.
- Checks **superheat and subcooling** with gauges to determine whether the charge is correct—the key test that separates an airflow problem from a refrigerant problem.
- Inspects the **evaporator and condenser coils** for dirt and the blower wheel for buildup.
- Tests the **blower motor and capacitor**, and on ECM motors, checks the module.
- If charge is low, performs a **leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just "topping off."

Be wary of any quote that adds refrigerant without a superheat/subcooling reading or a leak search—that's a temporary patch on a leaking system.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, weak airflow | Dirty filter / low airflow | Turn cooling off, fan ON, replace filter, thaw | Measure airflow, clean coil/blower |
| Water pooling at indoor unit | Melting ice + clogged drain | Clear drain line, vac the pan | Clear/repair condensate system |
| Re-freezes after filter change | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running unit, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Warm air even after thaw | Failing blower / capacitor | None—call pro | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Coil freezes only on cool nights | Thermostat set too low | Raise setpoint, avoid <68°F | Verify metering device operation |
| Only part of coil ices up | TXV/metering restriction | None—call pro | Replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US pricing including labor; your area and system size will vary.

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (PSC):** $400–$700
- **ECM/variable-speed blower motor:** $600–$1,200
- **Refrigerant leak search + minor repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800+

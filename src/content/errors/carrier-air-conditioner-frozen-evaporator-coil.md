## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numeric fault code on Carrier equipment — it's a physical condition you'll see or feel: ice building up on the indoor coil (inside the air handler or on top of the furnace) and often on the copper refrigerant line running outside. Some Carrier Infinity systems may log a related airflow or low-suction condition, but most Comfort and Performance units give you no code at all — just weak cooling and visible ice.

The coil freezes when its surface temperature drops below 32°F. That happens for two broad reasons: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **too little refrigerant / low pressure** (a sealed-system problem). Both cause the same symptom, which is why diagnosis matters before you spend money.

This is rated **pro** because confirming and fixing the underlying cause frequently involves refrigerant, which requires EPA certification and gauges. You can safely do the airflow checks and the thaw yourself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. A choked filter starves the coil of airflow, dropping its temperature until it ices over.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — closed registers, furniture over returns, or too many closed rooms restrict airflow the same way.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat transfer.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature, causing ice. This is a sealed-system repair, not a "top-off."
5. **Failing blower motor or wheel** — a weak, dirty, or failing blower moves too little air.
6. **Clogged condensate drain triggering issues** — a backed-up drain won't itself freeze the coil, but it's often found alongside airflow problems and can trip a float switch.
7. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** — operating cooling below about 60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil.
8. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/orifice)** — less common; disrupts refrigerant flow into the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

**First: turn the thermostat to OFF for cooling, and set the fan to ON.** Running the blower with cooling off is the safe, fast way to melt the ice. Never chip or scrape ice off the coil.

- **Air filter** — pull it and look. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to Cool and a reasonable setpoint (not, say, 62°F). Set fan to ON to speed thawing.
- **Supply and return vents** — open every register and clear furniture, rugs, or boxes off return grilles. Airflow needs a clear path.
- **Breaker** — if the system tripped, note it; don't repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.
- **Condensate drain** — check for water around the indoor unit. A frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it thaws; make sure the pan and drain aren't overflowing. You can vacuum out the drain line with a wet/dry vac.
- **Outdoor conditions** — if it's below ~60°F outside, don't run cooling.

Let the system thaw fully (1–3 hours), replace the filter, then run cooling again. If it re-freezes within a day, you have a deeper problem — call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech won't just add refrigerant. Expect them to:

- **Confirm the coil has fully thawed** before testing — you can't gauge a frozen system accurately.
- **Measure airflow** — check filter, blower amperage, and static pressure to rule out an airflow restriction first.
- **Inspect the evaporator coil and blower wheel** for dirt buildup.
- **Attach refrigerant gauges** and read superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct or low.
- **Leak-search** if the charge is low — with electronic detectors, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test. Simply adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary, non-compliant fix.
- **Check the metering device (TXV/orifice)** if pressures point that way.

If a tech reaches for gauges before checking your filter and airflow, that's a red flag on the quote.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow at vents | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open all vents, thaw with fan ON | Verify static pressure, clean coil |
| Coil refreezes after filter change | Dirty coil or blower | Confirm airflow path is clear | Clean evaporator coil and blower wheel |
| Ice on outdoor copper line, poor cooling | Low refrigerant (leak) | Shut cooling off, thaw, call pro | Leak search, repair, evacuate & recharge |
| Water pooling around indoor unit | Thawed coil overwhelming drain | Vacuum drain line, add drain tablets | Clear blockage, check float switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather/at night | Running AC too cold outside | Don't run cooling below ~60°F | Confirm charge; check low-ambient setup |
| Blower runs weak or noisy | Failing blower motor/capacitor | None — call pro | Test/replace motor or capacitor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thaw + diagnostic service call:** $90–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (more for ECM/variable-speed on Infinity systems)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant recharge (leak repaired):** $250–$750 depending on refrigerant type and amount
- **Leak repair + recharge combined:** $600–$1,500+
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800 if the coil itself is leaking

If your AC uses older R-22 refrigerant, recharge costs run much higher and a coil leak often makes a system replacement worth pricing.

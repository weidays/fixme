## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code on a display — it's a physical condition. The indoor coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) gets so cold that moisture in the air freezes into a block of ice on it. Instead of a code, you'll notice weak or no cold air, ice on the copper refrigerant lines, water pooling near the indoor unit, or the system running constantly without cooling.

The root problem is almost always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant in the system**. Both drop the coil temperature below freezing. Because diagnosing which one requires gauges and airflow measurement, we rate this **pro** — but there are safe steps you can do first that fix it for free in many cases.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of airflow, letting it drop below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This *always* means a leak; refrigerant is not "used up."
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and block airflow, same effect as a dirty filter.
4. **Blower problems** — a failing blower motor, wrong fan speed, or a slipping belt (older units) reduces airflow.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — restricting airflow starves the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating the system below about 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **Thermostat set to fan "on" with a leaking system, or a stuck TXV/metering device** — advanced causes a tech confirms.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these in order. Stop and call a pro if the coil re-freezes after a clean filter and full thaw.

- **Turn the system to fan-only (or OFF) to thaw.** Set the thermostat to *Fan: On*, *Cool: Off*. This melts the ice safely — never chip at it. Allow several hours, up to a full day if it's fully iced.
- **Place towels or a shallow pan** under the indoor unit; melting ice can overflow the drain pan and pump.
- **Replace the air filter** with the correct size. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, that was likely your problem.
- **Check the thermostat:** make sure it's not set unreasonably low (below ~68°F encourages freezing) and confirm fan settings.
- **Check the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and the outdoor unit. A tripped breaker can leave the blower off while the compressor runs.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles aren't closed, covered by rugs/furniture, or blocked.
- **Check the condensate drain.** If water is backing up, gently clear the drain line. A clogged line combined with a freeze can flood the area.

Once fully thawed **and** the filter is fresh, run the AC and watch it. Cools normally and stays ice-free? You're done. Re-freezes? Call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms it's thawed** — no accurate readings can be taken on iced equipment.
2. **Measures airflow / static pressure** and inspects the blower, filter, coil, and duct restrictions.
3. **Connects refrigerant gauges** and checks superheat/subcooling against Carrier's spec for your model to determine if the charge is correct.
4. **Leak-searches** if the charge is low — electronic sniffer, UV dye, or bubble test. A good tech finds and repairs the leak rather than just "topping off."
5. **Inspects the metering device (TXV/piston)** and coil condition.
6. **Verifies operation** after repair with a full cooling cycle.

A reputable tech will not simply add refrigerant to a system that's leaking without disclosing the leak — that's a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil/lines, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Thaw, replace filter, retest | None if it resolves |
| Weak airflow, clean filter | Dirty coil or blower issue | Thaw, inspect vents | Clean coil, service blower |
| Re-freezes after filter change | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw and stop running it | Leak search + repair, recharge |
| Water flooding near unit | Overflow from melting ice / clogged drain | Clear drain line, place pan | Clear/replace drain, pump |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run cooling when cold | Confirm no other cause |
| Long run times, no cold air | Multiple: charge, airflow, metering | Thaw, check filter/vents | Full diagnostic w/ gauges |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and model):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + retest (DIY):** $0
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$200
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$500
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors run higher)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+; on older R-22 systems, replacement may push toward full system replacement

If your system uses R-22, refrigerant costs are high and rising — ask whether repair or replacement makes more financial sense.

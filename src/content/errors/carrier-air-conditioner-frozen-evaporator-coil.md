## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a physical condition your Carrier air conditioner develops when the indoor coil (inside the air handler or furnace cabinet) drops below freezing and moisture in the air turns to ice.

The evaporator coil is supposed to run cold, but airflow across it and the right refrigerant charge keep it above 32°F. When either of those is off, the coil ices over. Ice then blocks airflow even more, which makes the freezing worse — a runaway cycle that can end in warm air, water leaks, and in the worst case a burned-out compressor.

Carrier Infinity and some Performance systems may log related communication or airflow faults on the control board or thermostat, but most homeowners discover this problem the same way: the AC runs constantly, blows weak or warm air, and there's ice on the copper lines or water pooling near the indoor unit.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — the single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the coil of airflow, letting it drop below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns reduce airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and restrict airflow, even with a clean filter.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — low pressure lowers coil temperature. This is a top cause when freezing repeats after airflow is ruled out. **Requires a technician.**
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / capacitor** — if the fan can't move enough air, the coil freezes.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating cooling below ~60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or dirty blower wheel** — debris on the blower wheel reduces airflow output.
8. **Kinked or restricted ductwork** — collapsed flex duct or a closed damper limits total airflow.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do them **after** shutting cooling off and letting any ice melt.

- **Thaw first:** Set the thermostat to OFF (cooling) and fan to ON. Wait until all ice is gone — usually 1–3 hours. Never chip ice off with a screwdriver or knife.
- **Check the air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see light through it, replace it. Do this even if it looks "okay."
- **Check the thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint makes sense. Very low setpoints on mild days encourage freezing.
- **Check vents and returns:** Open all supply registers and make sure furniture, rugs, or filters aren't blocking return grilles.
- **Check the breaker:** Confirm both the indoor air handler/furnace and outdoor condenser breakers are on.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan:** Frozen coils dump a lot of water when they thaw. Make sure the pan isn't overflowing and the drain line isn't clogged — a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain end clears many clogs.
- **Verify the fan actually runs:** With fan set to ON, you should feel steady air at the registers. Weak or no airflow points to a blower problem for a technician.

If the coil freezes again within a day or two of clean filter and clear airflow, stop running the unit and call a pro — that pattern usually means refrigerant or blower issues.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified HVAC tech should work through airflow and refrigerant systematically. Here's what a good visit looks like so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the coil is thawed** and inspect for water damage or a clogged drain.
2. **Measure airflow / temperature split** — checking the temperature difference between return and supply air (typically ~16–22°F on a healthy system).
3. **Inspect the filter, coil, and blower wheel** for restriction.
4. **Test the blower motor and capacitor** — measuring amp draw and confirming proper speed (ECM motors on many Carrier units are checked differently than PSC motors).
5. **Check refrigerant charge** using gauges and superheat/subcooling — this is where low charge from a leak shows up. This step requires EPA certification.
6. **Leak search** if charge is low — dye, electronic sniffer, or soap bubbles at joints and the coil itself.
7. **Inspect ductwork** for collapse, closed dampers, or undersized returns.

Be cautious of any tech who tops off refrigerant without searching for a leak — adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary, non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked airflow | Thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, clean coil |
| Water pooling near indoor unit | Coil thawed, drain clogged | Vac out drain line, add tablets | Clear drain, check pan/float switch |
| Freezes again after clean filter | Low refrigerant from leak | Stop running unit, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Warm air, fan runs weak or not at all | Failing blower or capacitor | Confirm fan set to ON | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Coil freezes only on cool days | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Avoid cooling in mild weather | Confirm no control fault |
| Whole coil iced, filter clean | Dirty evaporator coil | None (coil is sealed) | Professional coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your Carrier model and region affect the final number.

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY with a shop vac, or $75–$200 for a service call.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors on Infinity/Performance units cost more).
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $100–$350.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing varies).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500+ if the coil itself is leaking or corroded.

A simple filter-and-thaw fix costs almost nothing. Repeat freezing that turns out to be a refrigerant leak is where costs climb — and why a proper diagnosis matters before you approve any work.

## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numeric fault code on most Carrier equipment — it's a physical condition your indoor coil is in. On Carrier Infinity systems the control may instead flag a related symptom (low airflow, low suction, or a comfort fault) on the touchscreen, but standard Comfort and Performance systems give you no code at all. You simply notice ice on the copper lines, weak or no cold air, and water pooling near the indoor unit.

The evaporator coil (inside your air handler or above the furnace) gets cold as refrigerant absorbs heat from your air. When something drops the coil temperature below freezing **and** there isn't enough warm airflow across it, condensation on the coil freezes into ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, which makes it colder still — so the problem snowballs until the whole coil is a block of ice.

This is rated **pro** because the two most damaging causes (low refrigerant and a failing blower or metering device) require gauges, recovery equipment, and EPA certification to fix legally and safely.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — the single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the coil of warm return air, letting it freeze.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns choke airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust cakes the fins and blocks air even with a clean filter.
4. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — low pressure drops coil temperature and causes freezing; a very common cause when the filter is clean.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / wheel** — insufficient CFM across the coil. May be a bad capacitor, motor, or a dirty blower wheel.
6. **Thermostat set too low / running non-stop** — running for long stretches in cool weather (below ~62°F outdoors) can freeze the coil.
7. **Failing metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** — over-feeds or under-feeds refrigerant, dropping coil temperature.
8. **Clogged condensate drain** — not a freezing cause directly, but water backup often shows up alongside a frozen coil and confuses the diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these only after turning cooling **Off** and letting the coil fully thaw (see the FAQ).

- **Turn cooling off, fan on.** Set the thermostat to Off (or Heat briefly is not needed) and set the fan to **On**. This melts the ice faster and prevents further icing. Put towels down to catch water.
- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it with the correct size. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Open your vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or curtains.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor blower isn't running at all, confirm the air-handler/furnace breaker is on.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint (72–75°F), not something extreme.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan. A wet-vac on the drain line outlet clears simple clogs; drain tablets help prevent recurrence.

If the coil refreezes within a day of a clean filter and full thaw, stop and call a technician — you likely have a refrigerant or airflow problem beyond DIY.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Carrier tech should:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — you can't measure anything on a block of ice.
2. **Measure airflow / static pressure** across the coil and check the blower amperage and capacitor to rule out weak airflow.
3. **Inspect the coil and blower wheel** for dirt buildup.
4. **Connect gauges and take temperatures** — reading superheat and subcooling to judge the refrigerant charge and metering device.
5. **Leak-search if charge is low** — using an electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test. A pro should **find and fix the leak**, not just "top off" the refrigerant.
6. **On Infinity systems, read stored faults** on the touchscreen controller for airflow or comfort codes.

Be wary of any quote that adds refrigerant without a leak search — that's a temporary patch you'll pay for again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, filter is filthy | Dirty air filter | Replace filter, thaw fully, restart | Verify airflow, none needed if it holds |
| Ice returns after clean filter, weak airflow | Dirty coil or blower wheel | Thaw, check vents | Clean coil/blower, test motor & capacitor |
| Warm air, hissing, coil refreezes fast | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, thaw, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Blower runs slow or not at all | Failing motor or capacitor | Check breaker only | Replace capacitor or blower motor |
| Coil freezes only in cool/night weather | Low charge or low airflow | Raise setpoint | Measure charge & airflow |
| Water everywhere, drain clogged | Condensate drain clog | Wet-vac drain, add tablets | Clear line, check pan & float switch |
| Frost near metering line, uneven cooling | Faulty TXV/orifice | Turn off, thaw, call pro | Test & replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and system size):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 pro service call.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $450–$1,000 (ECM/variable-speed motors on Infinity systems cost more).
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing varies).
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900.
- **Full evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800 if the coil itself leaks.

A clogged filter fix is genuinely $0–$40. If a tech is quoting refrigerant work, expect the higher ranges and insist on a leak search.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is not a Carrier fault code — it is a physical condition. The evaporator coil sits inside your indoor furnace or air handler and absorbs heat from your home's air. When something disrupts that heat exchange, the coil surface drops below freezing, condensation turns to ice, and the ice snowballs until the coil is a solid block.

On Carrier systems this shows up as: little or no cold air, ice visible on the copper lines or coil cabinet, water pooling near the indoor unit as it thaws, or the outdoor unit's larger copper line (the suction line) frosted over. Communicating Infinity systems may report weak cooling or airflow faults, but there is no dedicated "frozen coil" code — the ice is the symptom of an underlying airflow or refrigerant problem.

Because the two most serious root causes — a refrigerant leak or a failing blower — require gauges, EPA certification, or electrical work, this is rated a **pro** repair once basic thawing and filter checks are done.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter.** The single most common cause. Restricted airflow over the coil lets it get too cold and freeze. Easy to check and fix yourself.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature, causing icing. Requires a technician — you cannot legally or safely add refrigerant yourself.
3. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents.** Too many closed registers or blocked returns starve the coil of airflow.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil.** Dust and biofilm insulate the coil, reducing heat transfer. Needs professional cleaning.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / wrong fan speed.** Weak airflow across the coil promotes freezing. Common on aging ECM or PSC blowers.
6. **Clogged condensate drain (secondary).** A full drain pan or float switch can shut the system oddly and mask airflow issues; not usually the direct freeze cause but often found alongside it.
7. **Stuck metering device (TXV or piston).** A faulty expansion valve overfeeds or underfeeds refrigerant, causing icing. Technician diagnosis.
8. **Running AC in cold weather.** Cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil; not a fault, just misuse.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — they resolve a large share of frozen-coil calls at no cost:

- **Turn the system off, set the thermostat fan to ON (FAN ONLY).** This thaws the coil without running the compressor. Let it run 1–3 hours until all ice is gone and the drain has cleared.
- **Replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the top cause. Slide the old one out, note the size printed on the frame, and install a fresh one of the same size and a moderate MERV rating.
- **Open and unblock all vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and don't run cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F.
- **Check the breaker.** If a float switch tripped the unit, resetting the breaker after thawing is fine — but do not repeatedly cycle a unit that won't stay running.
- **Look at the condensate drain.** After thawing, a lot of water should drain. If water backs up, the drain line may be clogged — a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain outlet often clears it.

Once the coil is fully thawed, run the AC and watch it. If it freezes again within a day with a clean filter and open vents, stop running it and call a pro — continuing risks compressor damage.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will work root cause, not just re-thaw the coil:

- **Confirm airflow.** Measure static pressure and verify blower speed, check the filter, coil cleanliness, and duct restrictions.
- **Attach refrigerant gauges.** Read suction/liquid pressures and superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct. Low readings point to a leak.
- **Leak search if charge is low.** Electronic leak detector, UV dye, or a nitrogen pressure test to locate the leak — Carrier warranty coil claims usually require documented proof.
- **Inspect the metering device (TXV/piston)** for correct feed and bulb contact.
- **Check the blower motor** amp draw and operation, and inspect the drain/float switch.

A quote that says "just add refrigerant" without a leak search is a red flag — refrigerant doesn't get used up, so if it's low, there's a leak that will freeze the coil again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/no cold air, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Ice on suction line, refrigerant low | Refrigerant leak | Thaw and shut off | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice returns after clean filter | Dirty coil or weak blower | Thaw, confirm vents open | Coil clean or blower service |
| Water pooling at indoor unit | Thawing ice / clogged drain | Vac the drain line | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Coil freezes only on cool nights | Running AC too cold outside | Stop cooling below ~60°F | Confirm no other fault |
| Warm air, coil frosts fast | Stuck TXV / metering | Thaw and shut off | Test/replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and model):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Coil thaw + diagnostic service call:** $80–$200.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$500.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors run higher).
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type; older R-22 systems cost more.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500+ (parts often covered if under Carrier warranty; labor typically is not).
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $300–$700.

If a leaking coil is out of warranty on an older R-22 system, a tech may recommend weighing repair against replacement.

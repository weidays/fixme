## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numbered Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition where ice builds up on the indoor coil (the A-coil above your furnace or in the air handler). Some Carrier Infinity and Performance systems with communicating controls may log a related **low airflow** or **low suction pressure** fault, but many units display nothing at all. You'll usually notice it as weak or no cooling, ice on the copper lines, or water pooling near the indoor unit as the ice melts.

The coil freezes when it gets too cold for the amount of warm air passing over it. That happens for one of two big reasons: **not enough air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant / wrong pressure** inside it. Both cause the coil surface to drop below freezing, so humidity in the air turns to ice instead of draining away as water.

This is rated **pro** because the two most serious causes — a refrigerant leak or a failing compressor/metering device — require EPA-certified refrigerant handling. The airflow causes, however, are often DIY.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil and it freezes over.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or a blocked return does the same thing as a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow through the fins.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — a leak lowers pressure, which drops coil temperature below freezing. Very common and requires a pro.
5. **Failing blower motor or wrong blower speed** — a weak or miswired blower moves too little air.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below ~60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **Faulty metering device (TXV/piston)** — a stuck expansion valve floods or starves the coil.
8. **Restricted or plugged condensate drain** — can trip a safety float, and standing water contributes to icing in some setups.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the system to Off, fan to On.** Setting the thermostat fan to On (not Auto) blows room-temperature air over the coil and thaws the ice much faster. Never run the cooling/compressor while the coil is frozen.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray or clogged, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers aren't closed and returns aren't blocked by furniture, rugs or curtains.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool at a sensible temperature and not stuck calling in cool weather.
- **Check the breaker.** If the outdoor unit's breaker tripped while the indoor fan kept running, that can promote icing — reset it once after the coil is fully thawed.
- **Look at the condensate line and pan.** Clear any obvious standing water or clogged drain, and empty a full condensate pump reservoir.
- **Inspect for visible ice on the copper lines** near the indoor unit to confirm the diagnosis.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or repeatedly restart a unit that keeps freezing. If it refreezes after a clean filter and full thaw, you have a deeper problem — call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing, then measure airflow across the coil (static pressure) and check blower speed/settings.
- Inspect the filter, coil cleanliness, and duct restrictions.
- Connect gauges and take **superheat and subcooling** readings to determine whether the charge is correct, low (leak), or the metering device is faulty.
- Perform a leak search (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low.
- Check the metering device (TXV or fixed piston) operation.
- Verify condensate drainage and the float switch.

Ask to see the superheat/subcool numbers. If a tech wants to "just add refrigerant" without finding a leak, that's a red flag — topping off a leaking system is a temporary, non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, weak airflow at vents | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents, thaw coil | Measure static pressure, advise on duct issues |
| Refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw fully, then stop and call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice + hissing or bubbling at indoor unit | Faulty TXV or metering device | None — call pro | Test and replace metering device |
| Ice, blower runs weak or not at all | Failing blower motor/capacitor | Thaw, check breaker | Replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Water pooling near air handler | Condensate drain clog / melting ice | Clear visible clog, empty pump | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Stop cooling until warmer | Verify low-ambient limits/charge |
| Dirty, matted coil fins | Coil fouling | Gentle no-rinse coil cleaner (if accessible) | Professional coil clean/replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor):

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Coil thaw:** $0 (DIY, just time)
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Condensate drain clearing / new pump:** $75–$300
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $100–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type; R-410A/R-454B pricing varies)
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors cost more)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800
- **Full evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type, and whether your Carrier unit is still under parts warranty (register your equipment to confirm). Older R-22 systems are increasingly expensive to service — sometimes replacement makes more sense.

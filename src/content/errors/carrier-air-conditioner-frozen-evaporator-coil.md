## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is not a numbered fault code — it is a physical condition inside the indoor unit of your Carrier air conditioner. The evaporator coil (usually mounted above or beside your furnace or air handler) is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. When something goes wrong, the coil temperature drops below freezing, moisture in the air condenses on it and turns to ice.

Once the coil ices over, airflow is blocked, cooling capacity collapses, and you get warm air from your vents even though the outdoor unit is running. In severe cases you'll see ice on the copper refrigerant lines outside or water pooling around the furnace as the ice melts.

Carrier units don't typically flash a dedicated "frozen coil" code, but the condition often shows up alongside low-pressure lockouts or a system that short-cycles and won't cool. If your thermostat or Infinity control reports a communication or pressure issue, a frozen coil may be the underlying cause.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature, causing ice. This is the most common *repeat* freezing cause.
3. **Blocked return vents or closed supply registers** — starving the coil of warm return air.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust insulate the coil and disrupt heat transfer.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad ECM module** — not enough air moving across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV / expansion valve)** — feeds too much refrigerant into the coil.
8. **Blocked condensate drain triggering a float switch** — indirectly related; standing water and poor drainage often accompany freezing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. Do them before paying for a service call.

- **Turn the AC off, set the fan to ON.** Switch cooling off but run the fan to thaw the ice safely. This can take 1–3 hours. Never chip or scrape the ice.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the #1 cause. If it looks gray or clogged, swap it. Filters should generally be changed every 1–3 months.
- **Open all supply registers and clear return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs or closed doors aren't blocking airflow.
- **Inspect the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan or a clogged drain line near the indoor unit. Vacuum the line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain exit.
- **Verify the thermostat setting.** Make sure it isn't set to an extremely low temperature and that "auto" fan mode isn't causing short cycles.
- **Check the breaker.** If the unit tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Once fully thawed**, restart cooling and watch. If it freezes again within a day, you have a refrigerant or airflow problem that needs a technician.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any float or safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier technician should work through airflow and refrigerant in order:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before taking any measurements — readings on an iced coil are meaningless.
2. **Measure airflow and static pressure** across the coil and filter to rule out blower, duct or filter restriction.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup and the blower wheel for debris.
4. **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to determine if the refrigerant charge is correct. On newer R-32 units they'll follow model-specific charging procedures.
5. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector or nitrogen/dye) if the charge is low — because "just add refrigerant" without finding the leak is a temporary fix.
6. **Test the blower motor and ECM module** amperage and RPM if airflow is weak.
7. **Check the TXV / metering device** operation if charge and airflow are both fine.

Sanity check: if a tech says "you're low on refrigerant" and offers to top it off without mentioning a leak search, ask where the leak is. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm air at vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Fan-only to thaw, replace filter | Airflow & static pressure test |
| Coil re-freezes within a day of thawing | Low refrigerant from a leak | Thaw, then stop running AC | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice on outdoor copper lines | Low charge or bad metering device | Turn off, thaw | Gauge readings, TXV check |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Melting ice + clogged drain | Vacuum condensate line | Clear drain, confirm float switch |
| Weak airflow even with clean filter | Failing blower motor / dirty coil | Check/open vents | Test ECM motor, clean coil |
| Freezing only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Don't run cooling in cold | Advise on low-ambient use |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual price varies by region and model.

- **Air filter replacement:** $0 DIY – $30. The cheapest and most common fix.
- **Thaw + system inspection service call:** $90 – $200.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150 – $400.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100 – $250.
- **Blower motor / ECM module replacement:** $400 – $900 depending on Carrier series.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150 – $500.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500 – $1,200+ (higher if the leak is in the coil itself).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $800 – $2,000+, often more on Infinity systems.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400 – $700.

If your unit is out of warranty and needs a new coil plus a refrigerant recharge, weigh the repair against system age — replacement may be more sensible on a 12+ year old unit.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is a physical condition, not a Carrier fault code — most Carrier units don't display a dedicated "frozen coil" alarm. Instead you notice ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, little or no cold air, and possibly water pooling near the indoor unit as ice melts. On Infinity systems, the wall control may instead show a low-charge or airflow-related message that points you toward the same root causes.

The coil freezes when it gets colder than 32°F. That happens when either **not enough warm air moves across the coil** (airflow problem) or **the refrigerant pressure is too low** (charge or metering problem). Both let the coil surface drop below freezing so humidity condenses and turns to ice.

Because the two main causes (airflow and refrigerant) require different fixes — and one involves sealed refrigerant that only a licensed tech may handle — this is rated **pro**.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — furniture, rugs, or too many closed registers reduce airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust caked on the coil fins insulates it and cuts airflow.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — undercharge drops coil temperature below freezing. Requires a licensed tech.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong fan speed** — the blower can't push enough air. Infinity systems may flag this.
6. **Closed or collapsed ductwork** — crushed flex duct or a stuck damper.
7. **Faulty metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** — the coil floods or starves. Tech diagnosis needed.
8. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating below about 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner:

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to *Cool: Off* and *Fan: On* to start thawing the coil. Never chip or scrape the ice.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or older than 60–90 days, swap it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply and return registers are open and not covered by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
- **Check the thermostat** — confirm it isn't set to an unusually low temperature that keeps the system running nonstop.
- **Check the breaker** — a tripped breaker to the indoor blower or outdoor unit can cause odd behavior; reset once only.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** Melting ice produces extra water; make sure the drain isn't clogged and the pan isn't overflowing. Drain tablets help keep it clear.
- **Let it fully thaw** (1–24 hours) before restarting cooling.

If it refreezes after a filter change and full thaw, stop and call a pro — that points to refrigerant or blower issues you shouldn't tackle yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — a frozen coil gives false readings.
2. **Measure airflow** — check filter, blower speed, and static pressure across the coil to rule out duct restrictions.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup and clean it if needed.
4. **Attach gauges and read superheat/subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct.
5. **Leak-search** with electronic detector or dye if the charge is low — adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a temporary fix.
6. **Test the metering device (TXV/orifice)** and blower motor operation.
7. On **Infinity systems**, pull any stored fault history from the wall control to confirm airflow or charge codes.

You should expect airflow to be checked *first* — a quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without inspecting the filter, coil, and blower deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents, thaw with fan on | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Refreezes after filter change | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Coil iced, dust caked on fins | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw, then call pro | Chemical coil cleaning |
| Water around indoor unit | Melting ice / clogged drain | Clear condensate drain | Flush drain, confirm slope |
| Little air from any vent | Weak blower / wrong speed | Check breaker once | Test blower motor, set fan speed |
| Freezes in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Don't run cooling when cold | Advise on operating limits |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw and restart:** $0 (just time)
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A/R-454B)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ if the coil itself is leaking
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$700

Prices vary by region and system size. Refrigerant work must be done by an EPA-certified technician.

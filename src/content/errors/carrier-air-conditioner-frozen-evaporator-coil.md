## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code Carrier displays — it's a physical condition you'll see or infer: ice building up on the indoor coil (inside the air handler or furnace cabinet), and often on the copper refrigerant lines leading to the outdoor unit. When ice forms, airflow drops to near zero and the system stops cooling your home.

The evaporator coil is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your indoor air. It normally runs cold — just above freezing — but stays ice-free because warm air keeps flowing across it. When something starves the coil of warm airflow **or** drops the refrigerant pressure too low, coil temperature falls below 32°F and moisture in the air freezes onto it. Once ice starts, it snowballs: the ice further blocks airflow, which makes the coil colder, which grows more ice.

This is rated **pro** because the two most common root causes — low refrigerant and airflow problems inside sealed equipment — usually require an EPA-certified technician. You can safely thaw the unit and rule out simple airflow issues, but you should not open the refrigerant system.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of warm return air.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces coil pressure and temperature below freezing. Requires a tech to locate, repair, and recharge.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust build an insulating layer that mimics an airflow restriction.
4. **Weak or failed blower motor / wrong fan speed** — variable-speed Infinity/Performance blowers can drop to low speed on a fault; PSC blowers can weaken with age.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — over-restricting airflow to save energy backfires.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below ~60°F outdoors lowers pressures and can ice the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — some systems shut down or short-cycle, and standing water refreezing worsens ice.
8. **Failed thermostat or stuck contactor** — the system runs continuously without cycling, over-cooling the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — they're free and safe:

- **Turn the system to OFF (cool), but set the fan to ON.** This runs the blower to thaw the coil faster without running the compressor against ice. Never keep cooling a frozen unit.
- **Wait for a full thaw** — 1 to 3 hours. Place towels around the air handler; melting ice produces a surprising amount of water.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, clogged, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Check your thermostat.** Confirm it's set to cool and a reasonable temperature (not 60°F). Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Check the breaker.** If the air handler and outdoor unit are on separate breakers, confirm both are on.
- **Open and unblock all supply vents and return grilles.** Move furniture, rugs, and drapes away. Don't run with vents intentionally closed.
- **Check the condensate drain.** If the drain pan is full or overflowing, clear the line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination.

If the coil re-freezes after a clean filter and clear vents, stop and call a technician — the cause is almost certainly refrigerant or a mechanical fault.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Carrier tech will:

1. **Confirm the coil is thawed** before testing — you can't read pressures accurately on an iced system.
2. **Measure airflow** across the coil and inspect the blower motor, blower wheel, and filter rack for restriction.
3. **Attach gauges and check refrigerant pressures and superheat/subcooling.** Low readings point to a leak or undercharge; this is the key test.
4. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or bubble test) if the charge is low — recharging without fixing the leak is a temporary and non-compliant fix.
5. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup that mimics low airflow.
6. **Check blower speed settings and control board** — on communicating Infinity/Performance systems, they'll pull fault history; on basic Comfort systems, they'll verify tap/PWM speeds manually.
7. **Verify the thermostat cycles correctly** and the contactor isn't stuck closed.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search is a red flag. Ask where the leak is and what the subcooling reading was.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak/no cool air | Clogged filter | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, confirm no other cause |
| Coil re-freezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running unit | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice on outdoor line, low airflow | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw and inspect | Chemical coil cleaning |
| Weak airflow from all vents | Failing blower / wrong speed | Check filter and vents | Test/replace blower, set speed |
| Water overflowing near air handler | Clogged condensate drain | Clear line with wet/dry vac | Clear drain, treat pan, check switch |
| System never shuts off, over-cools | Thermostat or stuck contactor | Replace thermostat batteries | Replace thermostat/contactor |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F | Don't run AC on cold days | Advise, verify no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + service call / diagnosis:** $90–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Blower motor (PSC):** $400–$700
- **Blower motor (variable-speed/ECM on Infinity/Performance):** $700–$1,200
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs older R-22 is far pricier)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800 if the coil itself is the leak source and out of warranty

Carrier coils are often covered under a 10-year parts warranty if registered — ask the tech to check your serial number before quoting a full coil replacement.

## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" is not a Carrier fault code you'll see flashed on a board — it's a physical condition. The indoor evaporator coil (in your air handler or furnace cabinet) drops below freezing, and moisture in the air turns to ice on it. Once ice forms, it blocks airflow further, which makes the coil even colder, so the problem snowballs fast.

You'll notice it as **weak or no airflow from the vents, warm air despite the system running, ice or frost on the copper lines near the indoor unit, or water pooling around the furnace/air handler** once things start to thaw.

The root cause is almost always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant** (a charge or leak problem). Everything below narrows down which.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — furniture over returns, closed registers, or an undersized duct setup starves the coil of air.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat exchange, causing it to run colder.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. Common on older systems.
5. **Failing or stuck blower motor / wheel** — a weak ECM or slipping blower can't move enough air; dirty blower wheels do the same.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temp can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or failing metering device (TXV/piston)** — meters too little refrigerant to the coil, causing localized freezing.
8. **Clogged condensate drain (on some setups)** — a float switch may not stop it, but standing water and poor drainage can accompany icing symptoms.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are homeowner-safe. Do them in order.

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan to ON.** Set the thermostat to OFF for cooling but the fan to ON (or AUTO). This starts thawing the coil and prevents compressor damage. Wait until all ice is gone — up to 24 hours in bad cases.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the #1 cause. Swap in a clean one of the correct size and a reasonable MERV (8–11 for most homes).
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure all supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
- **Check the breaker and thermostat batteries.** Confirm the system has power and, if your thermostat uses batteries, that they're fresh.
- **Look at the condensate drain.** If there's a standing pool or a full drain pan, clear the visible drain line with a wet/dry vac and consider drain tablets. Don't disassemble internal plumbing.
- **Let it thaw fully, then test.** Once ice is gone, restart cooling and watch. If it re-freezes within a day, stop and call a pro — that signals refrigerant or blower issues you can't fix safely.

Do **not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or scrape ice off the coil. Refrigerant handling is legally restricted and dangerous.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the coil is thawed** before testing — running gauges on an iced system gives false readings.
2. **Inspects filter, coil, and blower** for airflow restrictions and measures blower performance (static pressure or CFM).
3. **Connects refrigerant gauges** to read suction and head pressures, then calculates **superheat and subcooling** to judge whether the charge is correct.
4. **Checks the metering device (TXV or piston)** operation if pressures look off.
5. **Performs leak detection** (electronic sniffer, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if refrigerant is low — because "just adding refrigerant" without finding the leak is a temporary, wasteful fix.
6. **Verifies temperature split** (return vs. supply air, typically 16–22°F) after any correction.

Be wary of any tech who tops off refrigerant without discussing a leak search. Low charge means refrigerant escaped somewhere.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil/lines, weak airflow | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Replace filter, open all vents, thaw system | Verify airflow, measure static pressure |
| Re-freezes soon after clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Shut cooling off, call pro | Leak detection, repair, recharge |
| Warm air, coil frozen, dirty coil visible | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw, then schedule service | Professional coil cleaning |
| Weak airflow, filter is clean | Failing blower motor or dirty wheel | Thaw and observe | Test/replace ECM motor, clean blower wheel |
| Ice only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoor | Switch to heat/fan mode | Confirm low-ambient limits |
| Water around furnace after thaw | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain with wet/dry vac | Flush drain, check pan/float switch |
| Frost on part of coil only | Stuck TXV/metering device | Call pro | Replace metering device, verify charge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges. Regional labor and system size shift these.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Clearing a condensate drain (DIY):** $0–$25 with a shop vac and tablets.
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$450 depending on access.
- **Blower motor (ECM) replacement:** $400–$900 including labor.
- **Refrigerant leak detection:** $100–$350 as a standalone diagnostic.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (older R-22 systems cost far more).
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+, sometimes more on communicating Infinity systems or if the coil is discontinued.

If your system is 12+ years old, uses R-22, and needs a coil or major leak repair, ask your tech whether replacement is the smarter long-term spend.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil is not a Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition you'll find inside the indoor air handler or furnace cabinet. The evaporator coil is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. When something drops the coil's temperature below freezing, humidity condensing on it turns to ice instead of draining away.

Once ice forms, it insulates the coil and blocks airflow, which makes the problem worse fast. Common signs are warm air from the vents, ice on the copper lines near the indoor unit, water pooling around the air handler as ice melts, or the outdoor unit running constantly without cooling the house.

The root cause is almost always **restricted airflow** or **low refrigerant**. Both are worth understanding, because low refrigerant means a leak that needs a licensed technician — refrigerant is not a DIY repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of warm return air, letting it drop below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — Too many closed registers or blocked returns reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust caked on the coil fins insulates it and restricts airflow, even with a clean filter.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — Low pressure lowers the coil's boiling temperature below freezing. Requires an EPA-certified tech.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor** — A slowing or failing indoor blower can't move enough air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — Cooling with outdoor temps below ~62°F can freeze the coil; most homes only hit this in shoulder season.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — A backed-up drain can trip a safety float or cause water/ice buildup around the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop cooling first: set the thermostat to **OFF** for cooling and **FAN to ON** so the coil can thaw.

- **Air filter** — Pull it and inspect. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of freeze-ups.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint. Avoid setting it far below room temperature during hot spells, which can overwork the coil.
- **Supply and return vents** — Walk the house and open any closed registers. Make sure furniture, rugs, or curtains aren't blocking returns.
- **Breaker** — After the coil has fully thawed (1–3 hours), you can restart. Don't repeatedly cycle a frozen system.
- **Condensate drain** — Check for standing water in the drain pan or a clogged drain line. Clearing an accessible line and adding drain tablets is safe.
- **Outdoor unit** — Make sure the condenser coil outside isn't buried in leaves, grass clippings, or debris that blocks airflow.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any float/safety switch. If the coil re-freezes after a clean filter and full thaw, call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech confirms the cause before quoting parts. Expect them to:

- **Verify airflow** — Measure static pressure and confirm the blower is moving rated CFM; inspect filter and coil for restriction.
- **Inspect the evaporator coil** — Look for dirt buildup insulating the fins after thawing.
- **Check refrigerant charge** — Connect gauges, read suction/superheat and subcooling, and compare to Carrier's spec for your model. Low readings point to a leak.
- **Leak search** — If charge is low, use an electronic detector or dye to locate the leak rather than just topping off.
- **Test the blower motor and capacitor** — Confirm the motor spins up to speed and draws correct amperage.
- **Check the condensate system** — Verify the drain and float switch operate correctly.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, ask where the leak is. Adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary and non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm vent air | Dirty air filter | Replace filter, thaw, restart | Confirm airflow, static pressure |
| Coil re-freezes after new filter | Dirty evaporator coil | None — call tech | Clean coil, verify airflow |
| Ice + hissing or bubbling lines | Low refrigerant / leak | None — call tech | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow from all vents | Failing blower motor | Check filter first | Test motor, capacitor, replace |
| Water around air handler | Melting ice / drain clog | Clear accessible drain | Clear line, check float switch |
| Freeze-ups only in cool weather | Running AC below ~62°F | Avoid cooling in cold temps | Verify low-ambient operation |
| Outdoor unit choked with debris | Blocked condenser | Clear debris, rinse fins | Full coil cleaning, charge check |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your quote varies by region and model.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Coil thaw (DIY, time only):** $0
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors on higher-end Carrier units run more)
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $100–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and R-410A quantity
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ if the coil itself is leaking

A frozen coil caught early — usually just a filter — costs almost nothing. Ignored freeze-ups that damage the compressor are the expensive scenario, which is why shutting the system off matters.

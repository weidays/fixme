## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition your indoor coil enters when its surface temperature drops below freezing and condensation turns to ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil and blocks airflow, so cooling collapses and the problem gets worldwide worse the longer the system runs.

On Carrier systems with communicating controls (Infinity), you may see the system reporting weak cooling, low suction, or airflow-related messages, but many single-stage Carrier units give no code at all — you just notice warm air, a sheet of ice on the copper lines, or water pooling around the indoor unit as the ice melts.

The two root causes are almost always the same: **not enough air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant in the system**. Both drop the coil temperature below freezing.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Blocked return/supply vents or closed dampers** — reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust bypassing the filter insulate and restrict the coil.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — lowers coil pressure and temperature; usually causes repeat freezing.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / blower wheel caked with dirt** — can't push enough air.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~60°F outdoors) — outdoor conditions drop coil temp too low.
7. **Undersized ductwork or a collapsed/disconnected duct** — chronic low airflow.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** — overfeeds or underfeeds the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to *Off* for cooling but the fan to *On*. This thaws the coil safely and prevents compressor damage. Do this first.
- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, dusty, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Walk your vents.** Make sure supply registers aren't closed and returns aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
- **Look at the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to *Cool* at a reasonable temperature, and that "fan" settings are correct.
- **Check the breaker.** After thawing, confirm the indoor air handler/furnace and outdoor unit breakers are on.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A frozen coil often overflows the pan. Look for standing water; clear a clogged drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination if you can reach it safely.
- **Don't chip the ice.** Let it melt. Don't restart cooling until the coil is bone dry.

If the coil re-freezes after a filter change and a full thaw, stop and call a technician — that points to refrigerant or a mechanical airflow problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified Carrier tech will:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed, then measure **static pressure** across the air handler to quantify airflow restriction.
- Inspect the **filter, blower wheel, and evaporator coil** for dirt buildup.
- Attach gauges to read **suction/head pressure and superheat/subcooling** — the key numbers that reveal low refrigerant or a metering-device fault.
- **Leak-search** with electronic detector or dye if refrigerant is low (adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag in a quote).
- Verify **blower motor amperage and speed tap / ECM programming** against the Carrier spec for your model.
- Check **duct integrity** for disconnected or crushed runs.

A good quote should name a specific cause (e.g., "dirty coil," "leak at the coil," "blower running on wrong tap") — not just "add refrigerant."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil/lines, warm air | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Turn cooling off, thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, measure static pressure |
| Coil re-freezes after filter change | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running unit; call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Water around indoor unit | Overflow from melting ice + clogged drain | Clear drain line, thaw coil | Clear drain, treat pan, check float switch |
| Weak airflow at all vents | Dirty coil, blower, or duct issue | Check/replace filter | Clean coil/blower, inspect ducts, check ECM |
| Freezes in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Turn AC off in cold weather | Confirm no low-ambient control needed |
| Freezes with clean filter & good airflow | Faulty TXV/metering device | Call pro | Diagnose and replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Coil thaw + service call/diagnosis:** $90–$200.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor cleaning or ECM/PSC motor replacement:** $300–$900.
- **Condensate drain clearing / new float switch:** $75–$250.
- **Refrigerant leak repair (accessible joint) + recharge:** $400–$1,200.
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leak in the coil):** $900–$2,500+, higher for R-410A/newer refrigerant systems.
- **Metering device (TXV) replacement:** $400–$700.

If your system is old and the coil is leaking, ask whether a coil replacement is worth it versus a system replacement — refrigerant and coil availability vary by model age.

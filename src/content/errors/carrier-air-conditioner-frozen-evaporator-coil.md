## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" is not a numeric fault code on most Carrier air conditioners — it's a physical condition you'll see or diagnose. The evaporator coil (inside your indoor air handler or furnace cabinet) drops below freezing and ice forms on it, choking off airflow and stopping cooling.

On base and mid-tier Carrier units (Comfort and Performance series), there is **no error code** — you simply notice weak or no cold air, visible ice, or water around the furnace. On **Infinity** systems with a communicating SYSTXCC control, the user interface may log low-airflow or system faults, but it does not report "frozen coil" in those exact words.

The core physics: an air conditioner is designed to boil refrigerant inside the coil at a temperature above freezing. Anything that reduces airflow across the coil, or lowers the refrigerant pressure too far, drives the coil surface below 32°F. Condensation then freezes instead of draining away, and the ice snowballs.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold and freeze.
2. **Blocked return or supply airflow** — closed vents, crushed flex duct, closed dampers, or too many rooms shut off.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and grime insulate the coil and restrict air over the fins.
4. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — low pressure lowers the coil's boiling point below freezing. Very common cause and always requires a pro.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — not enough air is being moved even with a clean filter.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~60°F outdoor) — outdoor temps too low for the system's design.
7. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV / fixed orifice)** — overfeeds or underfeeds the coil.
8. **Undersized or collapsed ductwork** — chronic low airflow that keeps causing repeat freeze-ups.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to OFF for cooling and FAN to ON. This stops making ice and thaws the coil faster. Do not run cooling on a frozen coil.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the most common cause. Swap in a clean one of the correct size and MERV rating.
- **Open all supply vents and return grilles.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't starving the system for airflow.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** A frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it thaws. Clear a clogged drain line with a wet/dry vac and add drain tablets. Watch for overflow.
- **Check the thermostat setting.** Don't run cooling when outdoor temps are below about 60°F.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor blower isn't running at all, verify the furnace/air-handler breaker and switch are on — that alone can cause freeze-ups.

If the coil thaws, you replaced the filter, and it cools normally without refreezing — you're done. If it refreezes, stop and call a pro; the remaining causes involve refrigerant or sealed components.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified HVAC tech will:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing (running gauges on an iced coil gives false readings).
- Measure **airflow** — blower speed, static pressure, filter and coil condition.
- Attach gauges and check **refrigerant pressures and superheat/subcooling** to determine if the charge is low or the metering device is faulty.
- **Leak-search** if the charge is low — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means a leak that must be found and repaired, not just topped off.
- Inspect the **blower motor, capacitor, and wheel** for weak airflow.
- On Infinity systems, read any logged faults at the SYSTXCC interface to cross-check airflow data.

A trustworthy quote names the specific root cause (e.g., "leak at the coil," "failed blower capacitor") rather than just "add refrigerant." Repeatedly adding refrigerant without finding a leak is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines / coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Turn off cooling, thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Warm air + ice, blower barely running | Weak blower motor/capacitor | Confirm breaker on | Test/replace capacitor or motor |
| Water pooling near furnace | Thawing coil overwhelming drain | Wet/dry vac drain, add tablets | Clear/repair condensate system |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoor | Don't cool in cold weather | Confirm no other fault |
| Chronic repeat freeze-ups | Dirty coil or duct restriction | Replace filter, open airflow | Clean coil, correct ductwork |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; regional and system-size variation is large):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors on Infinity systems cost more)
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis + repair:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $150–$600+ depending on refrigerant type (R-410A vs. newer blends) and amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000+
- **Compressor replacement (if slugged/damaged):** $1,200–$2,800+

Continuing to run a frozen unit is what turns a $30 filter problem into a compressor replacement — thaw first, fix the cause second.

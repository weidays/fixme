## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is a symptom, not a Carrier fault code — most Carrier air conditioners won't flash a specific number for it. On Infinity systems, the control board may log airflow or low-pressure related faults, but the freeze itself is something you'll usually notice as poor cooling, ice on the copper lines, or water pooling near the indoor unit.

The evaporator coil sits inside your indoor air handler or furnace cabinet. It gets cold as refrigerant expands through it. Under normal conditions, warm indoor air blowing across the coil keeps it above freezing. When something reduces airflow across the coil **or** drops the refrigerant charge too low, the coil surface falls below 32°F, condensation freezes, and ice builds up. That ice then blocks airflow further, making the problem snowball.

The result: little or no cold air, a block of ice on the coil or suction line, and eventually water damage as the ice melts. This is rated **pro** because the two most common non-airflow causes — low refrigerant and blower/metering problems — require gauges, EPA-certified refrigerant handling, and diagnostic tools.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow and it freezes.
2. **Blocked airflow elsewhere** — closed/blocked supply registers, closed dampers, or a collapsed/disconnected return duct.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and cut heat transfer, even with a clean filter.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — low pressure drops the coil temperature below freezing. Requires a technician; refrigerant is never a DIY top-off.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong fan speed** — an ECM or PSC blower running slow, or an incorrectly set fan tap, reduces airflow.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — cooling with outdoor temps below roughly 60°F can freeze the coil on many systems.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — less a freeze cause, more a symptom that shows up alongside it; standing water and a dirty coil often go together.
8. **Faulty thermostat or stuck contactor** — running the compressor nonstop can overcool and freeze the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. **Stop here and call a pro if the coil keeps freezing after you've done all of these.**

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to *Cool → Off* and the fan to *On* (not Auto). This melts the ice safely. Never chip or scrape it.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. Check the size printed on the frame.
- **Open all supply registers** and make sure furniture, rugs, or closed doors aren't blocking return-air grilles.
- **Check the thermostat batteries** (if battery-powered) and confirm the setpoint isn't set unrealistically low, like 60°F.
- **Look at the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and outdoor unit. A tripped breaker on the blower side kills airflow.
- **Check the condensate drain** — if there's a float switch or overflow pan full of water, and it's tripped, clear standing water with a wet/dry vacuum at the outdoor drain outlet.
- **Let everything dry** for a couple of hours after the ice melts before restarting cooling.

If cooling returns and the coil stays clear after a fresh filter and open vents, you fixed it. If it freezes again within a day or two, the cause is deeper — call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will work in this order so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — readings on an iced coil are meaningless.
2. **Measure static pressure and airflow** across the coil and blower to rule out duct and filter restriction.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt, biofilm, or bent fins.
4. **Verify blower operation** — motor speed, capacitor (PSC) or ECM programming, and the fan-speed tap for cooling.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and take suction/liquid pressures, then calculate superheat and subcooling to judge the charge.
6. **Leak-search** if the charge is low — dye, electronic sniffer, or nitrogen pressure test. **A refrigerant top-off without finding the leak is a red flag**; it'll just freeze again.
7. **Check the metering device** (TXV or piston) and airflow-related sensors on Infinity systems.

Ask any tech who says "just needs a recharge" *where the leak is*. Low charge means refrigerant escaped somewhere.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open registers, thaw with fan ON | Measure static pressure, inspect ductwork |
| Freezes again after clean filter | Dirty coil or low refrigerant | Thaw and inspect for visible dirt | Clean coil or leak-search and repair charge |
| Ice on copper suction line outside | Low refrigerant charge | None — do not add refrigerant | Leak detection, repair, evacuate and recharge |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Melting ice + clogged drain | Vacuum drain line, add drain tablets | Clear drain, check pan and float switch |
| Little airflow, blower sounds slow | Failing blower / wrong fan speed | Confirm breaker is on | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor, set fan speed |
| Coil freezes on cool days | Running AC in low outdoor temps | Avoid cooling below ~60°F outdoors | Advise on low-ambient operation limits |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Regional labor and system size shift these.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40 for a multipack.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY with a wet/dry vac, or $75–$250 for a pro service call.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450 depending on access and whether the coil is pulled.
- **Blower motor capacitor (PSC):** $150–$400 installed.
- **Blower motor (ECM/variable-speed):** $500–$1,200+ — Infinity variable-speed modules run higher.
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$450.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type. Older R-22 systems cost more per pound.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+, more if the coil is under warranty (you still pay labor).

If your system is out of warranty and needs a coil *and* has a compressor leak, get a replacement-vs-repair quote — sometimes a new system is the better value.

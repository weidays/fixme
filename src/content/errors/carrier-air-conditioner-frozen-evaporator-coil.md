## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a numbered Carrier fault code — it is a physical condition. The evaporator coil (the indoor coil inside your air handler or above your furnace) has dropped below freezing and ice has formed on it. Most Carrier base and mid-tier units do **not** flash a specific code for this; you notice it by warm air, reduced airflow, or visible ice on the copper lines.

The root cause is almost always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant** in the system. Both drop the coil temperature below 32°F, and the humidity condensing on it freezes instead of draining away.

Higher-end **Carrier Infinity** systems with the SAM/Infinity control may log an airflow-related fault or a low-suction condition that points you here, but they still don't have a dedicated "frozen coil" code. Treat this as a diagnosis, not a code lookup.

> Running the AC while the coil is frozen can send liquid refrigerant to the compressor and destroy it. If you see ice, shut cooling OFF and thaw before doing anything else.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil of warm air and it ices over.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — furniture over returns, too many closed rooms, or undersized ductwork reduce airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust builds up on the fins over years and insulates the coil, dropping its temperature.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This is the top cause when freezes repeat after a clean filter.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the fan can't push enough air. Common on ECM and PSC blowers alike.
6. **Clogged condensate drain** — water backs up, and on some systems a float switch cycling can contribute to freezing patterns.
7. **Running the AC in cool weather (below ~62°F outside)** — the system can't maintain proper pressures and the coil freezes.
8. **Stuck or failed TXV / metering device** — restricts refrigerant flow and drops coil temperature. A technician-only diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do them in order.

1. **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** At the thermostat, set the system to OFF (or Emergency Heat off) and set the fan to ON. Let the coil thaw fully — 1 to 3 hours. Do not chip ice with any tool.
2. **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of freezes.
3. **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't covered by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
4. **Check the breaker.** If the air handler or furnace breaker tripped, the blower can't run. Reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
5. **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan or a tripped float switch. You can vacuum the drain line outlet with a wet/dry vac. Don't force anything.
6. **Look at the outdoor temperature.** If it's below the low 60s°F, don't run cooling — the coil will freeze regardless.

If the coil thaws, you replace the filter, and it stays frozen-free — you're done. If it freezes again within a day or two, you have a refrigerant or airflow problem that needs a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified Carrier tech will:

- **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before measuring anything — readings on a frozen system are meaningless.
- **Measure airflow** — static pressure across the coil, blower amperage, and check the blower capacitor or ECM module.
- **Inspect the coil and filter** for dirt and duct restrictions.
- **Attach refrigerant gauges** and take superheat/subcooling readings to confirm whether the charge is correct. Low subcooling with high superheat points to undercharge or a leak.
- **Leak-search** if the charge is low — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means a leak. Expect a dye test or electronic sniffer.
- **Check the TXV/metering device** if airflow and charge are both fine but the coil still freezes.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search is a red flag — you'll just freeze again next season.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, warm air from vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Thaw with fan ON, replace filter | Measure static pressure, clean coil |
| Coil re-freezes after new filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Thaw, then stop running cooling | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow, blower hums or won't spin | Blower motor or capacitor | Check breaker only | Test/replace capacitor or blower motor |
| Water on floor, tripped float switch | Clogged condensate drain | Vacuum drain line | Clear line, flush pan, test switch |
| Freezes only in mild weather | Running AC below ~62°F outside | Don't run cooling in cool weather | Confirm no other fault |
| Coil freezes, charge & airflow normal | Stuck TXV / metering device | None — call a pro | Replace TXV / metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Carrier parts run at or slightly above generic equivalents.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $8–$40
- **Thaw + filter (DIY):** $0 beyond the filter
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **ECM blower motor or module:** $500–$1,200
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs older R-22 is far pricier)
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement (if corroded/leaking):** $1,200–$2,500+

If your system uses R-22 (older units, roughly pre-2010), refrigerant costs are high and replacement often makes more financial sense than repeated repairs.

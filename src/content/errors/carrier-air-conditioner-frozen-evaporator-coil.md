## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a numeric fault code — it's a physical condition your Carrier air conditioner develops when the indoor coil drops below freezing and moisture in the air turns to ice on it. Carrier's basic Comfort and Performance systems usually don't flash a specific code for this; you notice it as **weak or no cold air, ice on the copper lines, or water pooling near the indoor unit**. Infinity systems with a communicating control may log low-pressure or airflow-related faults, but the diagnosis is the same.

The root problem is almost always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or **not enough refrigerant** (a charge or leak problem). Both lower the coil temperature until condensation freezes instead of draining away.

The single most important thing to do right now: **turn cooling OFF at the thermostat, set the fan to ON, and let the coil thaw fully before running the system again.**

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air, and it freezes.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — a leak lowers coil pressure and temperature. This is a pro repair and will refreeze until fixed.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust buildup insulates the coil and blocks airflow, dropping its surface temperature.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / wheel** — a dying blower, slipping wheel, or wrong fan speed setting cuts airflow.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating cooling below roughly 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
7. **Undersized or collapsed ductwork** — chronic low airflow that repeatedly freezes the coil.
8. **TXV / metering device failure** — an expansion valve stuck closed starves the coil (pro diagnosis).

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do them **after** the ice has fully melted.

- **Turn cooling off, fan ON.** This is step one and helps the coil thaw. Never chip or scrape ice off.
- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open all supply registers and clear return grilles.** Furniture, rugs, or closed vents choke airflow.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's set to COOL at a reasonable temperature and not stuck calling for extreme cooling in mild weather.
- **Check the breaker.** Confirm both indoor and outdoor units have power (a tripped breaker can leave the blower off).
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** As the ice melts you'll get a lot of water. Make sure the drain line isn't clogged and the pan isn't overflowing; a wet/dry vac on the drain line can clear a partial clog.
- **Look at the outdoor unit.** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from the condenser coil so the system can move heat.

If the coil refreezes after a clean filter and open vents, stop running it and call a pro — you likely have a refrigerant or mechanical problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified HVAC tech should:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing anything meaningful.
2. **Measure airflow** across the coil and check blower speed, wheel condition, and duct static pressure.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt, and the filter and ducts for restriction.
4. **Attach gauges and check refrigerant charge** using superheat/subcooling — the key test for a leak or undercharge.
5. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector or dye) if the charge is low. Simply "adding refrigerant" without finding the leak is a red flag.
6. **Test the metering device (TXV/piston)** if airflow and charge look correct.
7. On **Infinity systems**, read logged faults at the control to confirm the story.

If a quote says "just needs a recharge" without any leak search, ask why the refrigerant left — sealed systems don't consume refrigerant.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil / lines, weak cooling | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents, thaw with fan ON | Verify airflow, check duct static |
| Refreezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water leaking near indoor unit | Melting ice + clogged drain | Clear drain line, vac out clog | Clear/repair drain, check pan |
| Warm air, blower sounds weak | Failing blower motor/wheel | None — call pro | Test/replace blower motor or wheel |
| Freezes only in mild weather | Running cooling too cold | Raise setpoint, avoid cooling below 60°F | Advise on low-ambient operation |
| Coil clean, charge good, still freezes | Stuck metering device (TXV) | None — call pro | Test and replace TXV/piston |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (labor + parts vary by region and system):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Clearing a condensate drain (DIY):** $0–$20
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors on Infinity systems can run higher)
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ (higher if the coil is a matched Infinity unit)

Note: older R-22 systems cost significantly more to recharge, which often pushes homeowners toward replacement.

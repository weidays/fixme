## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a Carrier control-board fault code — it's a physical condition you'll see or feel: ice or frost building up on the indoor coil, the copper refrigerant lines, or inside the air handler. Some Carrier Infinity and Performance systems may flag a related symptom (weak cooling, high indoor humidity, or the system short-cycling), but there's no dedicated numeric "frozen coil" code.

The evaporator coil freezes when it gets colder than 32°F. That happens either because **not enough warm air is moving across the coil** (an airflow problem) or because **the refrigerant pressure is too low** (a charge or metering problem). Both drive the coil surface below freezing, and condensation turns to ice.

Ice acts as an insulator, so a frozen coil actually cools *worse* — you'll notice warm air from vents even though the system runs constantly. Continuing to run it risks liquid refrigerant reaching the compressor, which is an expensive failure. That's why this is rated **pro**: the safe DIY steps are limited, and repeat freezing points to something a technician must fix.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of airflow and drops its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked airflow elsewhere** — closed or blocked supply/return registers, a collapsed flex duct, or a dirty blower wheel all reduce airflow like a dirty filter does.
3. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — low pressure lowers the coil's boiling temperature, causing freeze-up. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means a leak.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and block airflow.
5. **Failing blower motor or capacitor** — a blower spinning too slowly moves too little air.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — using cooling when it's below ~62°F outside can freeze the coil.
7. **Faulty metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** — a stuck or failing metering device feeds refrigerant incorrectly. This is less common but harder to diagnose.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** At your thermostat, switch the mode from Cool to Off, and set the fan from Auto to On. This thaws the coil safely. Wait until all ice is gone (1–3 hours, sometimes longer).
- **Check and replace the air filter.** Pull your filter — if you can't see light through it, replace it with the correct size. This is the fix in a large share of cases.
- **Open and unblock all vents.** Make sure supply registers aren't closed and returns aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes. AC needs open airflow, not "closed rooms to save energy."
- **Look at the condensate drain.** A frozen coil dumps a lot of water when it thaws. Confirm the drain pan isn't overflowing and the drain line isn't clogged — a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain outlet can clear a simple clog.
- **Check the breaker.** If the system tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **After thawing, run it and watch.** If it cools normally with a fresh filter, the filter was your problem. If it freezes again, you need a technician.

Do **not** scrape or chip ice off the coil, add refrigerant yourself, or repeatedly reset a system that keeps freezing.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech won't just "add a pound of Freon." Expect them to:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing (a frozen system gives false readings).
- Measure **airflow** — static pressure, blower speed, and filter/coil condition.
- Attach gauges to read **suction and head pressures** and calculate **superheat and subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct.
- **Leak-search** with electronic detectors or dye if the charge is low — a legitimate tech finds and repairs the leak rather than just topping off.
- Inspect the **blower motor, capacitor, and metering device (TXV)**.
- Check the coil for dirt and verify the condensate system drains properly.

If a quote proposes adding refrigerant without any leak search, ask why — recharging a leaking system is a temporary and repeat-failure fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil/lines, warm air from vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Turn to fan-only, thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Freezes again days after filter change | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water overflowing from unit | Thawed ice + clogged drain | Vac out drain line | Clear drain, check safety switch |
| Weak airflow at all vents | Dirty coil or slow blower | Check filter and vents | Clean coil, test blower/capacitor |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~62°F | Stop cooling in cool weather | Advise on low-ambient limits |
| Cooling poor + high humidity | Failing TXV / metering | None — call pro | Diagnose and replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (labor + parts; varies by region and system size):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40 — often the entire fix.
- **Thaw + diagnostic service call:** $80–$200.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$500.
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors on Infinity systems cost more).
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $300–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far pricier).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ if the coil itself leaks.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$900.

If your system uses R-22 (older units), repairs get expensive fast, and replacement may be more sensible.

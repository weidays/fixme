## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code on most Carrier air conditioners — it's a physical condition your system has slipped into. The indoor coil (the "A-coil" inside your air handler or above your furnace) drops below freezing, and moisture from the air turns to ice on it. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, cooling gets worse, and the freeze accelerates.

On **Carrier Infinity and some Performance systems**, the wall control may display a related fault — such as low airflow, low suction pressure, or a "system communication/cooling" warning — but it typically won't say "frozen coil" in plain language. **Base Comfort-series units usually show nothing at all**; you just notice warm air, ice on the copper lines, or water on the floor.

The root problem is almost always one of two things: **not enough refrigerant** or **not enough airflow** across the coil. Everything below sorts out which.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold and freeze.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — a leak lowers pressure, which lowers coil temperature and causes freezing. This is the most common *recurring* cause.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — a coated coil can't transfer heat, so it over-cools and ices up.
4. **Blower problems** — a failing blower motor, wrong fan speed, or a slipping belt (on older units) starves the coil of air.
5. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — reduces total airflow through the system.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating cooling when it's below roughly 60°F outside can freeze the coil.
7. **Thermostat set too low** or continuous long runtimes combined with any of the above.
8. **Failed metering device (TXV/piston)** — feeds the coil incorrectly. Less common, needs a pro.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the **system OFF** so the coil can thaw:

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan to ON.** At the thermostat, set the mode to Off (or Fan Only) and the fan to ON. This blows room-temperature air over the coil to melt the ice safely — never chip or scrape it.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A gray, matted, or clogged filter is the #1 cause. Swap in a clean one of the correct size.
- **Look at your vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked by furniture, rugs, or closed doors. Closing too many vents starves the coil.
- **Check the breaker.** After thawing, confirm the AC and air-handler breakers are on and haven't tripped.
- **Check the condensate drain.** When ice melts, a lot of water arrives at once. Make sure the drain pan isn't overflowing and the drain line isn't clogged — a wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain end clears many clogs.
- **Confirm outdoor temperature.** If it's cool out (below ~60°F), the coil can freeze simply from running cooling; wait for warmer conditions.

If the coil re-freezes after a clean filter and full thaw, stop here — that points to refrigerant or a mechanical airflow fault, which is a pro job. Do not keep resetting or restarting a system that freezes repeatedly.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech should:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — measurements are meaningless on an iced coil.
2. **Check filter, coil cleanliness, and blower speed/operation.** They'll measure airflow (static pressure) to spot restrictions.
3. **Attach gauges and take refrigerant readings** — superheat and subcooling — to determine if the charge is low.
4. **Leak-search if refrigerant is low** (electronic detector, UV dye, or soap bubbles). Simply "topping off" without finding the leak is a red flag.
5. **Inspect the metering device (TXV/piston)** if airflow and charge look correct but freezing persists.
6. **Verify condensate drainage** so the eventual melt doesn't flood your home.

If a quote says "just add refrigerant" with no mention of a leak search, ask why — recurring low charge means a leak that will come back.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil or copper line, warm air from vents | Airflow restriction or low refrigerant | Turn off cooling, run fan, replace filter, let thaw | Measure airflow & refrigerant; find root cause |
| Freezes again right after a clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Stop running; call a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow at vents, coil freezes | Dirty coil or blower fault | Check/open vents, replace filter | Clean coil, test blower motor/speed |
| Water on floor near air handler | Melting ice + clogged drain | Vac the drain line, empty pan | Clear drain, add safety float switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC when it's too cold out | Wait for warmer temps | Confirm low-ambient limits |
| No cooling and no ice, blower running | May not be a freeze issue | See "Not cooling" guide | Full cooling diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + restart (DIY):** $0
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Condensate drain clear + float switch:** $100–$300
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM/variable-speed motors on Infinity systems run higher)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100–$350
- **Refrigerant recharge (after leak repair):** $200–$700+ depending on refrigerant type and amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,500+ (more if refrigerant type or matched coil availability is an issue)

A simple filter-and-thaw costs you nothing. Recurring freeze-ups tied to a refrigerant leak or coil replacement are where the bill climbs — get an itemized quote and a clear cause before approving major work.

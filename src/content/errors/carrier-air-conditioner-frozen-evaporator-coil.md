## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a numeric fault code — it's a physical condition where ice builds up on the indoor evaporator coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet). Most Carrier air conditioners do **not** flash a specific code for this. Instead you notice weak or no cold air, visible ice on the copper coil or refrigerant lines, or water pooling near the indoor unit as the ice melts.

On newer **Carrier Infinity** systems with a communicating control, you may see related fault messages for low airflow, low suction pressure, or low charge that point toward a freezing coil. Older Comfort and Performance units give no code at all — the frozen coil is the symptom you diagnose from.

The coil freezes when the refrigerant inside it gets too cold and too little warm air passes over it to keep it above 32°F. That happens from **restricted airflow** or **low refrigerant charge** — both of which need fixing, because the freezing itself can wreck the compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Starves the coil of airflow so it drops below freezing.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust cake the fins and block airflow even with a clean filter.
3. **Blocked return/supply vents or closed dampers** — too many closed registers reduce airflow across the coil.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — lower pressure means colder coil temperatures. This is a repair, not a "top-off."
5. **Failing blower motor or wrong blower speed** — not moving enough air (common after a board or motor swap).
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — outdoor temps below ~60°F can cause freezing; usually a homeowner behavior issue.
7. **Restricted or clogged condensate drain** backing up and disrupting airflow (less common, secondary factor).

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** At the thermostat, set the system to OFF and the fan to ON. This thaws the coil safely. Let it run 1–3 hours until all ice is gone.
- **Replace the air filter.** Pull it out — if you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many cases.
- **Open all supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking airflow. Never close more than a couple of registers.
- **Check the thermostat setting.** Don't run cooling when it's below 60°F outside.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** Look for standing water; a treatment tablet keeps the drain clear. Do not disassemble the unit.
- **Check the breaker.** After thawing, confirm the AC breaker is on before restarting.

Do **not** chip, scrape, or blow-dry ice off the coil — you can puncture the thin copper and cause a refrigerant leak. Do not open sealed refrigerant lines or add refrigerant yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will confirm the coil is fully thawed, then:

- Measure **airflow (static pressure)** across the coil and blower to check for restriction.
- Inspect and, if needed, clean the **evaporator coil** and blower wheel.
- Verify **blower motor speed** matches the system's tonnage (especially after prior repairs).
- Connect gauges to read **suction and head pressure** and check **superheat/subcooling** — this reveals whether refrigerant charge is correct.
- If charge is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just adding refrigerant.
- On Infinity systems, pull stored fault history to confirm airflow or pressure faults.

If a tech proposes "just adding a pound of refrigerant" without a leak search, push back — refrigerant doesn't get used up, so low charge always means a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter | Replace filter, thaw with fan ON | Confirm airflow after thaw |
| Ice returns after clean filter | Dirty coil / blower | Open vents, run fan | Clean coil & blower wheel |
| Ice + hissing or oily residue | Refrigerant leak | Shut cooling off | Leak search & repair, recharge |
| Ice after a recent repair | Wrong blower speed | None safe | Reset blower tap/speed |
| Freezing only in cool weather | Running AC too cold | Don't cool below 60°F out | Verify low-ambient limits |
| Water around indoor unit | Melting ice / clogged drain | Clear drain, use tablets | Clear/replace drain line |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and access):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90–$200
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (ECM motors run higher)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$500
- **Refrigerant recharge (R-410A):** $250–$700 depending on amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ (part cost has climbed with newer refrigerants)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250

A leaking coil that must be replaced and recharged is the most expensive outcome — on an older system, weigh it against replacing the whole unit.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code in the traditional sense — most Carrier air conditioners won't flash a number for it. Instead, you'll notice symptoms: weak or no cooling, ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, water pooling around the indoor unit, or a system that runs constantly without cooling the house.

The evaporator coil is the indoor coil that absorbs heat from your air. It's designed to run slightly above freezing. When something disrupts that balance — too little warm air moving across it, or too little refrigerant pressure — the coil surface drops below 32°F and condensation freezes into ice. That ice blocks airflow, which makes the problem worse in a spiral. On variable-speed **Infinity** systems, the user interface may log a low-airflow or system-performance fault, but the underlying issue is the same physics.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns starve the coil of warm air.
3. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — low pressure lowers the coil's boiling point, freezing it. This is a sealed-system repair.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust caked on the fins acts like insulation and blocks airflow.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor** — a weak blower or bad capacitor moves too little air.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating below about 60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — less a freeze cause, more a companion symptom; standing water can hint at long freeze-thaw cycles.
8. **Thermostat set too low or fan set wrong** — extreme low setpoints keep the compressor running long enough to ice up.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the system to FAN ONLY (or OFF) first.** Set the thermostat to fan "On" with cooling off so the blower thaws the coil without the compressor making more ice.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A clogged filter is the top cause. Swap in a clean one of the correct size.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's in cooling mode, the fan is set correctly, and the setpoint is reasonable (not set to 60°F).
- **Check the breaker.** If the air handler breaker tripped, the blower may have stopped — a fast route to a frozen coil.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the pan; a wet/dry vacuum on the drain line outlet can clear a soft clog.
- **Wait for a full thaw** (1–3 hours) before restarting cooling. Never chip or scrape ice off the coil.

If the coil freezes again within a day of a clean filter and clear vents, stop and call a technician — that pattern points to a refrigerant or blower problem you can't fix safely.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works airflow-first, then the sealed system:

- **Confirms the thaw** and inspects the filter, coil, and blower wheel for dirt buildup.
- **Measures airflow / static pressure** across the coil to find restrictions or a weak blower.
- **Checks blower motor and capacitor** amp draw and speed.
- **Attaches gauges** to read suction and head pressure and calculates superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct.
- **Leak-searches** with electronic detector or UV dye if the charge is low — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means a leak.
- **Inspects the metering device** (TXV or piston) and evaporator coil condition.

Be cautious of any quote to "just add refrigerant" without a leak search — that's a temporary patch, not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter | Replace filter, run fan to thaw | Verify airflow, check static pressure |
| Ice on copper suction line outdoors | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Coil freezes repeatedly | Blower or refrigerant issue | Fan only, then call pro | Test blower/capacitor, gauge system |
| Water around air handler | Long freeze-thaw or clogged drain | Vacuum drain line, replace filter | Clear drain, confirm coil not iced |
| No cooling, blower not running | Tripped breaker / bad blower | Reset breaker once | Replace motor/capacitor if faulty |
| Freezes only in mild weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoor | Turn off cooling on cool days | Confirm low-ambient limits |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **Air filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150–$250 professional
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $200–$500
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (ECM/variable-speed higher):** $450–$1,200
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800+ (higher for cased Infinity coils and newer refrigerants)

A frozen coil caused by a filter or vents is often a $0–$40 fix. Sealed-system causes are where costs climb — and where DIY ends.

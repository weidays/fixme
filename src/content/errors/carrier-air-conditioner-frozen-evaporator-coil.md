## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is a **condition**, not a fault code — most Carrier air conditioners will not flash a specific number for it. Instead you'll see ice or frost on the indoor coil (inside the air handler or furnace cabinet), on the copper refrigerant lines, or reduced/no cooling despite the system running constantly.

The evaporator coil is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your indoor air. It only works when **enough warm air flows across it** and the **refrigerant charge is correct**. When either of those is off, the coil temperature drops below freezing, moisture in the air condenses and freezes on the fins, and the ice sheet blocks airflow even further — a runaway cycle that ends with a solid block of ice and warm air from your vents.

Left running, a frozen coil can flood your floor when it thaws and, worse, can flood liquid refrigerant back to the compressor and damage it. That risk is why we rate this **pro** severity: thawing is DIY, but finding *why* it froze often needs gauges.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of airflow.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — furniture over returns, too many closed registers, or an undersized duct restrict airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the fins so they can't shed cold.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong fan speed** — an ECM or PSC blower running slow moves too little air.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — lower pressure means a colder coil that ices over.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating below ~60°F outdoor without a low-ambient kit.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up onto the coil**, or a stuck TXV/metering device (a pro diagnosis).

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — they resolve a large share of freeze-ups:

- **Turn the system off and thaw it.** Set the thermostat to **Off** or **fan-only** and let the ice melt completely (hours). Running fan-only pushes warm air over the coil and speeds thawing. Don't run cooling while iced.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or older than 1–3 months, swap in a clean one of the correct size. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or drapes. Keep the majority of registers open.
- **Check the thermostat setting.** Don't set it far below room temperature or run cooling on unusually cool nights.
- **Look at the condensate.** Check that the drain pan isn't overflowing and the drain line is clear; standing water plus ice suggests a clog. Drain tablets can help maintenance.
- **Check the breaker.** After thawing, confirm the outdoor unit's breaker is on.

If the coil re-freezes after a clean filter and open vents, stop running the AC and call a technician — continuing risks compressor damage.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Carrier tech should:

1. Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing (a frozen coil gives false readings).
2. **Measure airflow / static pressure** and inspect the blower wheel and motor speed to rule out an airflow problem first.
3. **Inspect and clean the evaporator coil** if it's fouled.
4. **Connect gauges** and check refrigerant pressures, then calculate **superheat and subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct — this is the key step to distinguish low refrigerant from airflow issues.
5. If refrigerant is low, **perform a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just topping off.
6. Check the **metering device (TXV/piston)** and condensate drain if airflow and charge look normal.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without checking the filter, coil, and blower — adding refrigerant to a leaking or airflow-starved system is a temporary and often improper fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, warm air from vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, clean coil |
| Frost on copper line at outdoor unit | Low refrigerant or airflow | Thaw and check filter | Gauge charge, leak search |
| Coil freezes again after new filter | Dirty coil or weak blower | Inspect return grilles | Clean coil, test blower motor |
| Water on floor near air handler | Thawed coil / clogged drain | Clear/monitor condensate drain | Clear drain, confirm charge |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Running AC in cold ambient | Avoid AC below ~60°F | Recommend low-ambient kit |
| Long run times, high bills, some ice | Refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Leak repair + recharge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (labor + parts vary by region and system):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900 (PSC) up to $600–$1,200+ (ECM).
- **Refrigerant leak search + minor repair + recharge (R-410A):** $500–$1,600+. Older **R-22** systems cost far more due to refrigerant scarcity — replacement is often more economical.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ depending on system size and matched coil.

If the coil froze from a leak *and* the compressor was damaged by liquid slugging, repair costs can approach the price of a new system — get a second opinion.

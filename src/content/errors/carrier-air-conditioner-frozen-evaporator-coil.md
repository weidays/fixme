## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a Carrier fault code in the traditional sense — most single-stage Carrier air conditioners have no diagnostic display, so you simply find a block of ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines, weak airflow, and no cooling. Carrier Infinity systems with a communicating control may log a related fault (low suction pressure or airflow lockout) at the wall control, but the underlying problem is the same.

The coil freezes when it gets colder than the moisture on it can handle. That happens for one of two reasons: **not enough warm air is moving across the coil** (an airflow problem), or **the refrigerant charge or pressure is wrong** (a refrigerant problem). Ice acts as insulation, so once it starts, cooling collapses and the freeze gets worse until you shut the system down.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — furniture over returns, closed registers, or a collapsed flex duct starves the coil of air.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and reduce airflow, especially if the filter has been neglected for years.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge lowers pressure and coil temperature, causing icing. This is a repair, not a "top-off."
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — reduced fan speed means less air over the coil. Common on ECM and PSC blowers.
6. **Clogged condensate drain (secondary effect)** — a plugged drain can trigger a float switch shutoff, but standing water and high humidity also feed ice buildup.
7. **Running the AC in cold weather** — cooling below roughly 60°F outdoor temperature can freeze the coil on many systems.
8. **Stuck or failing metering device (TXV/orifice)** — less common; overfeeds or underfeeds the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Shut off cooling first.** Set the thermostat to OFF for cooling and the fan to ON to thaw the ice. Never run cooling with a frozen coil.
- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open and unblock vents.** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by rugs, furniture, or drapes.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to a reasonable temperature and not calling for cooling in cold outdoor weather.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor blower breaker or air-handler switch is off, the fan can't move air.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for water overflowing the pan or a float switch that tripped. A wet/dry vac on the drain line outlet can clear a light clog.
- **Let it fully thaw** — 1 to 4 hours — before restarting cooling. Wipe or vacuum melted water so it doesn't overflow.

If it cools normally after a filter change and thaw, you're done. If it refreezes within a day or two, stop resetting it and call a pro — that pattern points to refrigerant or blower problems that DIY steps won't fix.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Confirms full thaw** and inspects the coil, filter, and blower wheel for dirt.
- **Measures airflow** — static pressure and blower speed — to rule out duct or motor problems.
- **Attaches gauges** and reads suction/head pressure and superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct or if there's a leak.
- **Leak-searches** with electronic detector or dye if refrigerant is low; a proper repair finds and fixes the leak before recharging.
- **Tests the blower motor and capacitor** for correct speed and amp draw.
- **Checks the metering device (TXV/orifice)** operation if pressures are abnormal but charge is correct.

A good quote names the specific cause — "low on charge from a leak at the coil," not just "needs freon." A tech who only tops off refrigerant without finding the leak hasn't fixed anything.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Replace filter, thaw fully | Verify airflow, clean coil if needed |
| Weak airflow from vents, some closed | Blocked/closed registers | Open all vents, clear returns | Check duct static pressure |
| Refreezes days after thaw, weak cooling | Low refrigerant / leak | Stop running; call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Coil frozen, blower runs slow or noisy | Failing blower / bad capacitor | Thaw and observe fan | Test motor/capacitor, replace |
| Water overflowing pan, unit shut off | Clogged condensate drain | Vacuum drain line | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Low outdoor temp cooling | Avoid AC below ~60°F | Add low-ambient controls if needed |
| Dirty coil despite clean filter | Coil fouling | None — coil access needed | Chemical coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$30 DIY.
- **Thaw and restart:** $0 DIY, or $90–$180 for a diagnostic service call.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450 depending on access.
- **Blower capacitor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Blower motor (ECM):** $400–$800 installed.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,600, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A costs more than legacy R-22 systems that may need conversion).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800 if the coil itself is leaking.

Prices vary by region and system size. Always get the specific cause in writing before approving refrigerant work.

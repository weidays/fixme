## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is a **symptom**, not a Carrier fault code. The evaporator coil sits in your indoor air handler or furnace and is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. When something disrupts the balance between airflow and refrigerant flow, the coil surface drops below freezing and condensation turns to ice.

Most Carrier residential ACs (Comfort, Performance, and many Infinity models) don't display a dedicated "frozen coil" code — you'll notice it as **weak or no cooling, ice on the copper lines, or water on the floor** as the ice melts. Some **Infinity** systems with a SAM or wall control may log an airflow or low-capacity fault that hints at the underlying cause, but the ice itself isn't a numbered error.

The two root categories are always the same: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant** in the system.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked return or supply vents** — closed registers, furniture over grilles, or too many closed doors starve the coil of airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat transfer.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — low pressure lowers the coil's boiling temperature and it ices over. This is a pro repair.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — reduced airflow, same result as a clogged filter.
6. **Running the AC when it's too cold outside** (below ~60°F / 16°C) — common in shoulder seasons.
7. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV / orifice)** — over-feeds or under-feeds the coil.
8. **Clogged condensate drain backing up onto the coil** — less common but possible.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. **First, turn the cooling OFF at the thermostat and set the fan to ON** — this thaws the coil safely.

- **Filter:** Pull it. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Set system to **Off / Fan On** to melt the ice. Don't run Cool until it's fully thawed (1–3 hours).
- **Vents and registers:** Walk the house and open every supply register; move rugs and furniture off return grilles.
- **Breaker:** If the indoor blower isn't running at all in Fan mode, check the furnace/air-handler breaker and the furnace door switch (it must be fully closed).
- **Condensate drain:** Check for water pooling around the indoor unit. If the drain is clogged, clear it with a wet/dry vac at the exterior drain outlet, and add drain tablets.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure the condenser isn't buried in leaves or debris (this affects overall performance).

Do **not** chip, scrape, or blow-torch the ice, and do not add refrigerant yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — you can't diagnose airflow or charge with ice present.
2. **Measure static pressure and airflow** across the coil and blower to catch a weak blower, dirty coil, or duct restriction.
3. **Check the blower motor amperage and run capacitor** (for PSC motors) or the ECM programming on variable-speed Infinity units.
4. **Attach gauges and take superheat/subcooling readings** to determine whether the charge is correct — a low reading points to a **refrigerant leak**.
5. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low. A reputable shop won't just "top off" and leave — that's illegal for non-trivial leaks and only delays failure.
6. **Inspect the metering device (TXV/orifice)** if airflow and charge both check out.

If a tech proposes adding refrigerant without finding *why* it's low, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Turn off Cool, Fan On to thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, inspect coil |
| Coil refreezes after filter change | Low refrigerant leak | Thaw, then stop running Cool | Leak search + repair, recharge |
| Weak airflow from vents, coil ices | Failing blower or bad capacitor | Confirm blower runs in Fan mode | Test motor amps, replace capacitor/ECM |
| Water on floor near air handler | Melting ice / clogged drain | Vac out condensate line, add tablets | Clear drain, confirm root cause |
| Ice only in cool spring/fall weather | Outdoor temp too low | Don't run AC below ~60°F outside | Confirm low-ambient limits |
| Dust-caked coil visible | Dirty evaporator coil | — (leave coil cleaning to pro) | Professional coil cleaning |
| Half the vents closed | Vent/duct restriction | Open all registers | Static pressure test, duct check |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Thaw + system reset (DIY):** $0.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200 if a pro does it.
- **Blower run capacitor:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor (PSC):** $400–$700; **ECM/variable-speed (Infinity):** $600–$1,200+.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $200–$500.
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R‑410A vs older R‑22 units are pricier).
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ if the coil itself is the leak source.

A frozen coil traced to a filter is nearly free; one traced to a refrigerant leak is one of the more expensive AC repairs — which is why proper diagnosis matters.

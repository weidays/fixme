## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a physical condition where ice forms on your Carrier system's indoor coil (inside the air handler or above the furnace). It's one of the most common reasons a Carrier AC stops cooling, and on Infinity systems it may show up indirectly as a low-pressure or airflow-related fault at the control board.

The evaporator coil gets cold on purpose — that's how it absorbs heat from your indoor air. But it should stay above freezing. When something drops the coil temperature below 32°F, condensation on the coil turns to ice. Ice then insulates the coil and blocks airflow, which makes it colder still, snowballing into a solid block of frost. Two root problems cause this: **not enough warm air moving across the coil** (airflow restriction) or **not enough refrigerant** (a leak or undercharge).

Because confirming a refrigerant charge requires gauges and EPA-certified handling, this is a **pro-level** repair once the basic airflow checks are ruled out.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of warm return air, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Low refrigerant / refrigerant leak** — undercharge lowers coil pressure and temperature, causing freezing. Requires a certified tech to diagnose and repair.
3. **Blower fan problems** — a failing blower motor, bad capacitor, or slipping belt (on older units) reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust and grime on the coil act as insulation and restrict airflow between the fins.
5. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns starve the system of airflow.
6. **Clogged condensate drain** — standing water can freeze around the coil and pan; also a symptom of ongoing freeze/thaw cycles.
7. **Running the AC in cold weather** — operating cooling when outdoor temps are below ~60°F can cause the coil to freeze.
8. **Faulty thermostat or metering device (TXV)** — less common; a stuck expansion valve can overfeed or underfeed the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do them **after** turning cooling off and letting the coil fully thaw.

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to Off (cool mode) but the fan to On. This blows room-temperature air over the coil to melt the ice faster and helps you avoid compressor damage.
- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen-coil cases.
- **Look at your vents.** Make sure supply registers throughout the house are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs, and that return grilles are clear.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint (not 60°F). Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breaker.** If the system tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the drain pan or a slow/blocked drain line. A wet-dry vac on the exterior drain outlet can clear a simple clog.
- **Look for ice.** Peek at the coil area and the refrigerant line (the larger insulated copper line) at the outdoor unit. Visible frost confirms the diagnosis.

If the coil refreezes after a clean filter and clear airflow, the cause is almost certainly refrigerant or blower-related — time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will not just "add refrigerant." Expect this sequence:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** — you can't measure a frozen system accurately.
2. **Verify airflow** — inspect filter, blower motor amp draw, blower wheel cleanliness, and static pressure across the coil.
3. **Inspect the coil** — check for dirt buildup and bent fins insulating the coil.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — read suction/head pressures and check superheat and subcooling against Carrier's spec for your model.
5. **Leak search if undercharged** — using electronic detectors, UV dye, or a nitrogen/soap test to find the leak source before adding refrigerant.
6. **Check the metering device (TXV/piston)** for correct operation.

**Sanity check:** If a tech proposes adding refrigerant without hunting for a leak, that's a red flag — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so a low charge means it leaked out.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow at vents | Dirty air filter | Replace filter, thaw coil | Verify airflow, check static pressure |
| AC not cooling, ice on outdoor line | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw, then call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Frozen coil returns days after thaw | Refrigerant leak or blower fault | Confirm clean filter, then call pro | Diagnose charge and blower motor |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Coil thawing / clogged drain | Clear drain line, thaw coil | Clear drain, confirm root cause |
| Little airflow, filter is clean | Blower motor or capacitor failing | Call pro | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Coil dusty and matted | Dirty evaporator coil | Light no-rinse coil cleaner | Professional coil cleaning |
| Freezing only in cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outdoors | Turn off AC in cold weather | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual costs vary by region and by Carrier model.

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thawing the coil (DIY):** $0 — just time and patience
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250 (pro) or ~$15 DIY with tablets
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Blower motor replacement:** $450–$1,200 (ECM/variable-speed motors on Infinity units cost more)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Minor leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on refrigerant type and amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,500–$3,000+ (often the case with a leaking coil, especially older R-22 systems)

Note: R-410A and older R-22 refrigerant costs differ significantly. R-22 is phased out and expensive — an R-22 leak often makes coil or system replacement the smarter long-term choice.

## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" is not a Carrier fault code on your thermostat—it is a physical condition where ice builds up on the indoor coil (the A-shaped or slab coil in your air handler or above your furnace). Modern Carrier Infinity systems may log a low-pressure or low-suction-related fault, but most homeowners discover this problem by symptoms: weak airflow, warm air from vents, water pooling near the indoor unit, or visible frost on the copper lines.

Ice forms when the coil surface drops below freezing. That happens for two broad reasons: **not enough warm indoor air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant to keep pressures where they belong**. Either way, condensation freezes instead of draining away, and the ice sheet then blocks airflow completely—making the problem worse fast.

This is rated **pro** because the two most damaging causes (low refrigerant/leak, failing blower) require gauges, leak detection, and EPA-certified refrigerant handling. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out airflow basics yourself.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A restricted filter starves the coil of warm return air.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — Furniture over registers or shut-off rooms reduce airflow below what the coil needs.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — Dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat transfer.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Low charge drops suction pressure and the coil freezes. This is a repair, never a "top-off" fix without finding the leak.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — Less air = colder coil. Common on ECM or PSC blower issues.
6. **Blocked condensate drain backing up around the coil** — Standing water can freeze and mask itself as a coil problem.
7. **Thermostat set too low or running fan-only in cold conditions** — Running cooling when it's already cool outside can freeze a marginal system.
8. **Stuck or failed TXV/metering device** — On TXV-equipped Carrier coils, a stuck valve starves or floods the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This is step one for any frozen coil. It stops making ice and thaws what's there. Wait 1–3 hours before doing anything else.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or older than 60–90 days, swap it. This alone fixes many frozen coils.
- **Open and unblock all supply and return vents.** Make sure no rugs, furniture, or closed doors are choking airflow. Never close off more than a couple of registers.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint (68°F or higher). Replace batteries if it's a battery model and the display is dim.
- **Check your breaker.** If the indoor or outdoor unit tripped, that can cause partial operation. Reset once—if it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Look at the condensate drain/pan.** If there's standing water, gently clear the drain line and vacuum the pan with a wet/dry vac. A backed-up drain often accompanies a freeze.
- **After thawing, run the system and watch.** If it freezes again within a day with a clean filter and open vents, you need a technician.

Do **not** open the refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. Those require a licensed pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will confirm the coil is fully thawed first, then:

- **Measure static pressure and airflow** to confirm the blower is moving rated CFM, checking the blower motor, capacitor, and wheel for dust buildup.
- **Attach gauges to read suction and head pressure** and calculate superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct.
- **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt, bent fins, and biofilm.
- **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low—reputable techs find the leak rather than just adding refrigerant.
- **Test the metering device (TXV or orifice)** on applicable models for correct operation.
- **Verify the condensate drain** is clear and the float switch (if equipped) is working.

Sanity check: if a quote says "just add refrigerant" with no leak search, push back. Low charge means a leak, and refilling without repair is a temporary and legally questionable fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Turn off cooling, fan ON, replace filter, open vents | Confirm airflow with static pressure test |
| Warm air even after thaw | Low refrigerant (leak) | Thaw fully, then call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Water pooling by indoor unit | Frozen coil dripping / clogged drain | Vacuum drain and pan | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Freezes again within a day | Failing blower / dirty coil | Verify filter clean, then call pro | Test blower motor & capacitor, clean coil |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Low charge or bad TXV | Avoid running cooling below ~65°F outdoor | Check charge, inspect metering device |
| Loud/weak blower + ice | Blower capacitor or motor fault | Note the symptom, call pro | Replace capacitor or blower motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and tonnage variation applies):

- **Filter replacement / vent clearing:** $0–$40 DIY.
- **Coil thaw + diagnostic service call:** $90–$200.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $75–$250.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **ECM/blower motor replacement:** $450–$1,000+.
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A costs vary).
- **Full evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,800+, higher if it's under a furnace or matched to a newer refrigerant.
- **TXV/metering device replacement:** $400–$900.

If your system uses older refrigerant or the coil is out of warranty and the unit is 12+ years old, ask your tech whether replacement makes more sense than a major coil or leak repair.

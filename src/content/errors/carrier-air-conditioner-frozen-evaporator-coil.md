## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numeric fault code on most Carrier systems—it's a physical condition you'll see (ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil) and feel (little or no cool air). On Carrier Infinity systems with communicating controls, you may see a related low-airflow or low-pressure notice at the wall control, but base Comfort and Performance units simply stop cooling well.

The evaporator coil is the indoor coil that absorbs heat from your home's air. It runs cold by design. When something disrupts the balance—too little airflow across the coil, or too little refrigerant inside it—the coil surface drops below freezing and condensation turns to ice. Once ice forms, it insulates the coil, cooling gets worse, and the freeze accelerates until you have a solid block.

The freeze is almost always a **symptom of another problem**, not the root cause. Your job (and your technician's) is to find what pushed the coil below freezing.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops the coil temperature and it ices over.
2. **Blocked return/supply vents or closed dampers** — starving the coil of warm return air.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins act like the filter restriction, insulating the coil.
4. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — not enough air being moved across the coil.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the second-biggest real-world cause. Low charge lowers coil pressure and temperature. This is a pro repair.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~62°F outdoor) — low outdoor temps can cause freezing.
7. **Clogged condensate drain causing a float/safety trip** — less a freeze cause, more a companion symptom that shuts the system.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV or piston)** — misregulates refrigerant flow into the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first—many frozen-coil calls end here.

- **Turn the system OFF or to FAN ONLY.** Set the thermostat to OFF for cooling, then fan to ON. This thaws the coil and prevents compressor damage. Never run cooling with a visible ice block.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A dirty filter is the #1 cause. Replace with a clean pleated filter (MERV 8–11 is a safe range for most Carrier systems).
- **Open all supply registers and clear return grilles.** Move furniture, rugs, and drapes away from vents. Don't close off rooms to "save energy"—it starves the coil.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's set to COOL, at a reasonable setpoint, and if it's battery-powered, that the batteries are good.
- **Check the breaker.** If the air handler tripped, that's worth noting—but only reset a breaker once. Repeated tripping means a pro visit.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Look for standing water in the pan or a full drain line. A wet/dry vac on the outdoor drain outlet can clear a clog. A tripped float switch will keep the system off until cleared.
- **Wait for a full thaw before restarting.** Let ice melt completely (1–3 hours). Then run the AC and watch: if it refreezes within a day, stop and call a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety float switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the *cause*, not just the symptom. Expect them to:

1. Confirm the coil is thawed before testing—you can't read pressures accurately on a frozen system.
2. Measure airflow: check filter, blower amperage, blower capacitor, and static pressure across the coil.
3. Inspect the evaporator coil for dirt, biofilm, and bent fins.
4. Connect gauges to read suction/head pressure and calculate superheat and subcooling to judge refrigerant charge.
5. If charge is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test)—finding the leak is essential, not just topping off.
6. Test the metering device (TXV/piston) operation.
7. Check the condensate drain and float switch.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search—refrigerant doesn't get used up, so low charge always means a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil / lines, weak airflow | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Turn off, thaw, replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Refreezes after clean filter | Dirty coil or low refrigerant | Thaw, monitor, then call pro | Clean coil or leak search + recharge |
| Little/no air from vents | Failing blower or bad capacitor | Thaw, listen for blower | Test/replace blower motor or capacitor |
| Warm air, hissing near coil | Low refrigerant (leak) | Shut off, call pro | Leak detection, repair, recharge |
| Water pooling near air handler | Melted ice + clogged drain | Vac out drain line | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Running AC below ~62°F outdoor | Stop cooling in cold weather | Verify low-ambient limits |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and system size):

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Coil thaw + monitoring:** $0 DIY / $90–$180 diagnostic visit
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Blower motor (ECM/PSC):** $400–$900+
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400
- **Leak repair + recharge (minor):** $500–$1,500
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $1,200–$2,500+

If your system is R-22 (older units) or the coil itself is leaking, ask whether repair still makes financial sense versus replacement—R-22 refrigerant is very expensive and no longer produced.

## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a numbered Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition where ice forms on the indoor coil (the A-coil above your furnace or inside the air handler). Most Carrier air conditioners, including base Comfort and Performance units, will **not** flash a code for this at all. Higher-end **Infinity** systems may log airflow or low-pressure related faults at the wall control, but the underlying problem is the same.

A coil freezes when its surface temperature drops below 32°F. That happens for one of two root reasons: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **too little refrigerant** in the system. Either way, moisture in the air condenses and freezes into a block of ice, which then blocks airflow entirely — so once it starts, it snowballs fast.

The result is an AC that runs, but blows little or no cool air, often with water pooling around the furnace or air handler once the ice melts.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricts airflow across the coil, dropping its temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked return or supply vents / closed dampers** — furniture, rugs, or too many closed registers starve the coil of air.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — undercharge lowers coil pressure and temperature. Requires a licensed tech; refrigerant is not a DIY item.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust film insulate the coil and reduce heat pickup.
5. **Failing or dirty blower wheel / weak blower motor** — reduced CFM even with a clean filter. Common on aging ECM motors.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** (below ~60°F outdoors) — outdoor temps too low for the system to operate correctly.
7. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/piston)** — meters refrigerant incorrectly, causing localized freezing.
8. **Clogged condensate drain triggering a float switch** — not a freeze cause itself, but often found alongside melting-ice water damage.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do them in order:

- **Turn cooling OFF, set the fan to ON.** This is step one for a suspected freeze — it thaws the coil without running the compressor. Give it 3–24 hours.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Walk the house and open every supply register**, and clear furniture or drapes off return grilles. Don't close more than a couple of vents.
- **Check the thermostat** — make sure it's set to Cool and a reasonable setpoint, not stuck in a mode that runs the compressor constantly.
- **Look at the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and confirm it's on. A tripped blower breaker leaves the compressor running with no airflow — a fast freeze.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan** for standing water once ice melts. Clear the drain line with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor termination, or drop in cleaning tablets.
- **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before running cooling again. Running it iced makes everything worse.

If the coil refreezes within a day of a clean filter and open vents, stop — you likely have a refrigerant or blower problem that needs a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should not just "add refrigerant." Expect them to:

1. **Confirm airflow first** — measure static pressure, inspect the filter, coil face, and blower wheel, and verify blower speed/CFM.
2. **Attach gauges and read pressures/temperatures** — checking superheat and subcooling against Carrier spec to determine whether the charge is correct.
3. **Leak-search if the system is low** — with electronic detector or dye. A proper repair fixes the leak, not just tops off refrigerant.
4. **Inspect the metering device (TXV/piston)** for correct operation.
5. **Verify the coil is clean** and check the condensate drain and float switch.

Red flag: a quote to "recharge" with no leak test and no airflow measurement. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up" — if it's low, it leaked out and will leak again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on refrigerant line / coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter or blocked vents | Fan ON to thaw, replace filter, open vents | Measure static pressure, check blower |
| Coil refreezes days after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call a pro | Leak search, repair, evacuate & recharge |
| Warm air, coil dirty and gray | Fouled evaporator coil | None (coil access is internal) | Professional coil cleaning |
| Weak airflow, clean filter, motor hums | Failing blower/ECM motor | Confirm breaker is on | Test/replace blower motor or module |
| Water around furnace after thaw | Clogged condensate drain | Clear line with wet/dry vac, tablets | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Freezes only in mild/cool weather | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Turn off AC in cool weather | Verify low-ambient limits |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + reset (DIY):** $0
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far pricier)
- **Blower motor / ECM module replacement:** $450–$1,000
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $500–$900
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ (higher if still under refrigerant/parts warranty vs. full retail)

Age matters: if the coil, compressor, and refrigerant type all point to an aging R-22 system, weigh repair against replacement before spending big on refrigerant work.

## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a fault code Carrier displays — it is a physical condition you or a technician diagnose by sight. The indoor evaporator coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) drops below freezing and ice forms on it, sometimes spreading down the copper lines to the outdoor unit.

A coil only freezes when one of two things is true: **not enough warm air is moving across the coil**, or **there is not enough refrigerant charge** to keep pressures normal. Both cause the coil surface temperature to fall below 32°F, so the moisture condensing on it turns to ice instead of draining away.

Once ice forms, it blocks airflow, which makes the freezing worse — a runaway cycle. That is why a lightly frosted coil quickly becomes a solid block of ice, and why your house stops cooling even though the system is running hard.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow starves the coil of warm return air.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too-few supply/return vents** — reduces the air volume across the coil.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and block airflow between the fins.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — lowers coil pressure and temperature. This is the most common *serious* cause and requires a technician.
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / bad capacitor** — the blower can't move enough air across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~60°F outdoor temp) — low outdoor temperatures drop coil pressures.
7. **Stuck or failing metering device (TXV / fixed orifice)** — feeds refrigerant incorrectly, dropping coil temperature.
8. **Oversized system short-cycling** — an AC too large for the home can freeze during brief runs.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system in a safe state. **First, turn the thermostat's cooling OFF and set the fan to ON** — this is the single most important step, because it thaws the coil while you inspect.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Set mode to OFF (cooling) and FAN to ON to speed thawing. Confirm you're not accidentally set to a very low temp that never satisfies.
- **Supply and return vents:** Open every register. Move furniture, rugs, or drapes off return grilles. A closed room can starve the coil.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for ice on the copper lines and confirm the outdoor fan and coil aren't buried in leaves or clogged with cottonwood fluff.
- **Condensate drain:** As ice melts, a lot of water drains out. Make sure the drain pan isn't overflowing. A wet/dry vacuum on the outdoor drain line clears clogs.
- **Breaker:** If the coil is a solid block of ice, leaving cooling OFF (fan ON) for several hours is normal. Don't repeatedly flip the breaker to force it back on.

If the coil refreezes within a day of a fresh filter and open vents, stop and call a technician — the remaining causes involve refrigerant or the blower.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the *why*, not just the ice. Expect them to:

1. **Verify the coil is fully thawed** before testing — you can't read pressures on a frozen system.
2. **Measure airflow** — check filter, blower speed, static pressure, and clean the evaporator coil if it's dirty.
3. **Attach gauges and read superheat/subcooling** to determine whether the charge is correct. Low charge with a clean coil points to a leak.
4. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low. They should find and fix the leak, not just "top off" refrigerant.
5. **Test the blower motor and capacitor** for correct amp draw and speed.
6. **Check the metering device** (TXV or orifice) operation on Infinity/Performance systems.

Be cautious of any quote that adds refrigerant without a leak search — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge always means a leak somewhere.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air, coil frosting | Dirty filter | Replace filter; set fan ON to thaw | None if resolved |
| House won't cool, ice on copper lines | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn cooling off, thaw, call pro | Leak search + repair + recharge |
| Coil dirty, fins packed with dust | Dirty evaporator coil | Turn off, let thaw | Professional coil cleaning |
| Some rooms hot, coil freezes | Closed vents / blocked returns | Open all vents and returns | Airflow/static pressure balance |
| Fan hums or runs slow, coil ices | Failing blower / bad capacitor | Turn off, thaw | Test/replace motor or capacitor |
| Freezes only on cool nights | Running AC below ~60°F outside | Don't run cooling in cold weather | Confirm no other fault |
| Refreezes after every thaw | Bad TXV / metering device | Turn off, call pro | Diagnose and replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY with a shop vac; $100–$250 as a service call.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400 depending on access.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Blower motor (ECM/PSC):** $400–$900, higher for Infinity variable-speed motors.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400.
- **Refrigerant recharge (R-410A):** $150–$600 depending on amount.
- **Small leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,000.
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $1,000–$2,500+; often more if the coil is on backorder or refrigerant is R-410A. A registered Carrier parts warranty may cover the coil part, but not labor or refrigerant.

## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a fault code Carrier displays on a control board — it's a physical condition you'll usually spot yourself: ice or frost on the copper coil and refrigerant lines inside your indoor air handler or furnace cabinet, weak or warm airflow at the vents, and sometimes water pooling around the unit as the ice melts.

The evaporator coil is where refrigerant absorbs heat from your home's air. It's designed to run cold but stay above freezing. When something reduces the amount of warm air moving across the coil — or drops the refrigerant pressure too low — the coil surface falls below 32°F and condensation freezes into a growing block of ice. That ice then blocks airflow completely, which makes the problem worse and can starve the compressor of returning refrigerant.

This is rated **pro** because while thawing and basic airflow fixes are DIY, the most common recurring cause (low refrigerant from a leak) requires EPA-certified handling and can't be legally or safely fixed by a homeowner.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. A choked filter starves the coil of warm return air.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — closing too many registers or blocked returns reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — reduces coil pressure and temperature; the classic "keeps freezing after I clean everything" symptom.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — years of dust bypassing the filter insulates the coil and disrupts heat transfer.
5. **Failing or dirty blower wheel / blower motor** — the blower can't move enough air across the coil.
6. **Thermostat set too low or running excessively long cycles** in cool, humid weather (especially overnight).
7. **Stuck or failing metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** — meters refrigerant incorrectly and lets the coil overcool.
8. **Undersized or restricted ductwork** — a chronic design issue that shows up as repeat freezing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — several fix the problem for free:

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** At the thermostat, switch the system to OFF (or Heat is fine) and set the fan to ON. This thaws the coil safely. **Do not run the AC/compressor while the coil is iced** — it can damage the compressor. Allow 1–3 hours to fully thaw.
- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the #1 cause. Note your filter size printed on the frame.
- **Check your supply and return vents.** Make sure registers are open and not blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed doors. Keep return grilles clear.
- **Check the condensate drain.** As the coil thaws, a lot of water will drain. Make sure the drain pan isn't overflowing and the drain line isn't clogged — a shop vac on the outdoor drain outlet clears many clogs.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor blower isn't running at all, confirm the furnace/air-handler breaker and switch are on. No blower = guaranteed freeze.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it isn't set unusually low (below 68°F) and that the fan setting works.

If the coil thaws, you replaced the filter, opened vents, and it stays frozen-free — you're done. **If it freezes again within a day or two, stop and call a pro.** Repeat freezing points to refrigerant or airflow problems you can't fix safely.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will work through airflow first, then refrigerant — you can sanity-check a quote against this:

1. **Confirm and thaw** — verify the coil is (or was) frozen and let it thaw before taking readings; measurements on an iced coil are meaningless.
2. **Inspect airflow** — check filter, coil cleanliness, blower wheel, and measure static pressure across the system to spot duct restrictions.
3. **Measure the blower** — verify the blower motor and speed tap/ECM programming are moving rated CFM.
4. **Connect gauges** — read suction and head pressures and check superheat/subcooling against Carrier's charging chart for your model and refrigerant.
5. **Leak search** — if the charge is low, they'll use an electronic detector or dye to find the leak rather than just "topping off," which is a temporary and increasingly restricted practice.
6. **Check the metering device** — confirm the TXV or orifice is metering correctly.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to add refrigerant without finding a leak first. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up" — if it's low, it leaked out.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, warm air at vents | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, thaw with fan ON | Check static pressure, blower CFM |
| Freezes again after new filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Thaw and call a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water around indoor unit | Coil thawing / clogged drain | Clear condensate drain | Confirm drain slope and trap |
| Coil frosts even with clean filter | Dirty coil or weak blower | Thaw; visually check coil | Clean coil, test blower motor |
| Freezes only overnight | Running too cold / humid cycles | Raise setpoint a few degrees | Verify charge and metering |
| Little/no airflow, blower silent | Blower not running | Check breaker/switch | Replace capacitor/motor/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Airflow fixes are cheap; refrigerant and mechanical repairs are not.

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 DIY.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY with a shop vac; $100–$250 pro.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400 professionally.
- **Blower capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Blower motor (ECM or PSC):** $400–$900 depending on model.
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $100–$350.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant amount; R-410A and newer R-454B charges vary in price.
- **Metering device (TXV) replacement:** $500–$900.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ including labor and refrigerant recovery/recharge.

Because refrigerant is involved in the most expensive scenarios, and Carrier's newer units use different refrigerants with different handling rules, get a written diagnosis before authorizing major work.

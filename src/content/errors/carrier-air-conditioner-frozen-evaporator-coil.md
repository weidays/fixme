## What this code means

Most Carrier air conditioners don't display a specific "frozen coil" fault code — instead you notice ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, weak airflow, water around the furnace/air handler, and rising indoor temperatures. Infinity systems may show low-cooling or airflow-related messages, but a frozen coil is usually a **physical symptom you find**, not a code the board reports.

A frozen evaporator coil means the indoor coil dropped below 32°F and moisture in the air froze onto it. Ice blocks airflow, which makes the coil even colder — a runaway cycle that ends with a solid block of ice, no cooling, and water dripping as it slowly melts.

The two root categories are almost always **not enough airflow across the coil** or **not enough refrigerant in the system**. Fixing airflow is often DIY. Refrigerant problems are a pro job — they usually mean a leak, and adding refrigerant without finding the leak is throwing money away.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricts airflow so the coil can't absorb enough heat and freezes.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow between fins.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — lowers coil pressure and temperature below freezing. Requires a pro to locate and repair.
5. **Failing blower motor or wrong blower speed** — not enough air moving across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** (below ~60°F outside) — outdoor conditions push the coil temperature too low.
7. **Clogged condensate drain backing up** — some systems shut down or reduce airflow, contributing to freezing.
8. **Stuck or failed metering device (TXV/orifice)** — overfeeds or underfeeds the coil, a pro diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — several are genuine DIY fixes.

- **Turn the system OFF, then set the thermostat to FAN ON (fan only).** This runs the blower without cooling and thaws the ice safely. Do NOT keep the AC running while frozen.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open and unblock all supply vents and returns.** Move furniture, rugs, and drapes off vents. Closing too many rooms starves the coil of airflow.
- **Verify the thermostat setpoint** isn't set unreasonably low, and that it's on COOL, not on during cold weather.
- **Check the breaker** for the air handler/furnace and outdoor unit; a tripped blower breaker stops airflow.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** If you see standing water or a clogged line, clear it with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain outlet.
- **Let the coil fully thaw before restarting cooling.** Restarting on ice just refreezes and stresses the compressor.

If it refreezes after a clean filter, open vents, and a full thaw — stop and call a pro. That points to refrigerant or a mechanical fault you shouldn't chase yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will confirm the freeze is fully thawed, then:

- **Measure airflow** across the coil and check blower speed/motor amperage and static pressure.
- **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt buildup and the filter/return path.
- **Connect gauges and check refrigerant charge** using superheat/subcooling — the key test for a leak or undercharge.
- **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or UV dye if the charge is low.
- **Test the metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** for proper operation.
- **Verify the condensate drain** is clear and the pan/float switch works.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or airflow check is a red flag — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge always means a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set fan-only to thaw, replace filter | Check charge, airflow, leak-search |
| Weak/no cool air, no visible ice yet | Clogged filter, closed vents | Replace filter, open all vents | Measure static pressure, blower speed |
| Refreezes after filter change | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — stop running AC | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Melting ice or clogged drain | Clear drain, mop up, thaw fully | Verify float switch, drain slope |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Outdoor temp too low | Don't run AC below ~60°F outside | Confirm charge, low-ambient limits |
| Coil dirty/matted with dust | Dirty evaporator coil | Light cleaning if accessible | Professional coil cleaning |
| Blower barely moving air | Failing blower motor/wheel | Check blower breaker | Replace motor, capacitor, or wheel |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Airflow fixes are cheap; refrigerant and mechanical repairs are not.

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thaw + diagnostic service call:** $75–$200.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450.
- **Refrigerant recharge (R-410A):** $200–$800 depending on amount.
- **TXV/metering device replacement:** $300–$650.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$900.
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $1,000–$2,500 with refrigerant.
- **Compressor damage from running frozen:** $1,500–$3,000+ — the reason not to run an iced-up AC.

Costs vary by region, model, and whether your system is still under Carrier's parts warranty — check your registration before authorizing a coil or compressor replacement.

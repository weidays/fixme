## What this code means

A "frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numbered fault code—it's a physical condition you'll see or feel: a block of ice on the indoor coil (the A-shaped coil above your furnace or inside the air handler), weak or no cold air at the vents, and often water on the floor as it melts.

Your Carrier AC works by boiling liquid refrigerant inside the evaporator coil. That process makes the coil cold. When something drops the coil temperature below freezing—almost always **too little warm air moving across it** or **too little refrigerant**—condensation on the coil turns to frost, then a solid block of ice. Ice blocks airflow, which makes it colder, which makes more ice. It snowballs fast.

Some Carrier Infinity and Performance systems with communicating controls may throw an airflow or low-charge related fault, but the ice itself is a symptom, not a self-reported code. This is rated **pro** because the two most common root causes (refrigerant leaks, failed blower components) require licensed service and EPA-certified refrigerant handling.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — the #1 cause. A clogged filter starves the coil of warm return air, dropping its temperature until it freezes.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers, blocked returns, or crushed ductwork reduces airflow the same way a dirty filter does.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the coil fins insulate it and restrict airflow, causing freezing over time.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. This is a leak, not "low on freon"—refrigerant doesn't get used up.
5. **Failing blower motor or wheel** — a weak, dirty, or failing blower (or a slipped/incorrect blower speed setting) moves too little air.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** — using cooling when it's below ~62°F outside can freeze the coil.
7. **Stuck or failing metering device (TXV/piston)** — an expansion valve that overfeeds or underfeeds refrigerant can ice the coil.
8. **Restricted condensate drain** backing up isn't a freeze cause, but a frozen coil often reveals a clogged drain when the melt overflows.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first—several fix the problem for free.

- **Turn the system OFF, set the fan to ON.** At the thermostat, switch the mode from Cool to Off, and set the fan from Auto to On. This runs the blower to thaw the ice without making more. Leave it for several hours to a full day.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. Note the size printed on the frame. This alone resolves a large share of frozen-coil calls.
- **Open all supply registers and clear returns.** Make sure no rugs, furniture, or closed doors are choking airflow. Homeowners often close vents to "save energy"—it does the opposite here.
- **Check the thermostat setting.** Don't set cooling below about 68°F, and don't run cooling when it's cold outside.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan.** Once thawed, look for standing water. You can clear a visible drain line with a wet/dry vacuum at the outdoor termination and add drain tablets.
- **Check the breaker.** If the indoor unit tripped, that's a clue for the technician, not something to keep resetting.

After a full thaw, replace the filter and restart cooling. **If it freezes again within a day, stop and call a pro**—you have a root cause the safe checks didn't fix.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified Carrier tech should:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing (a frozen coil gives false readings).
- Measure airflow and static pressure to catch duct restrictions, a dirty coil, or a weak blower.
- Inspect the filter, blower wheel, and evaporator coil condition.
- Connect gauges and take **superheat and subcooling** readings to judge the refrigerant charge and metering device operation.
- If charge is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just topping off—topping off a leak is a temporary, EPA-discouraged fix.
- Check blower speed taps or ECM/communicating settings against Carrier's spec for your model and coil.

A good quote names the specific root cause (e.g., "leak at the coil, needs replacement") rather than vaguely "adding refrigerant."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked vents | Replace filter, open vents, thaw with fan ON | Verify airflow, check static pressure |
| Freezes again days after filter change | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw fully, keep filter clean | Professional coil cleaning |
| Warm air, higher energy bills, refreezing | Low refrigerant (leak) | None—stop running unit | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water on floor near furnace/air handler | Melting ice + clogged drain | Vacuum drain line, add tablets | Clear/repair drain, check pan |
| Weak airflow even with clean filter | Failing blower motor/wheel | Listen for weak/noisy blower | Test/replace motor or clean wheel |
| Coil freezes only in cool weather | Running AC when it's cold out | Don't cool below ~62°F outdoors | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Thaw + restart (DIY):** $0.
- **Condensate drain clearing (pro):** $75–$250.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $300–$700 (PSC), $600–$1,100 (ECM/variable-speed common on Infinity).
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$450.
- **Leak repair + recharge (minor):** $500–$1,200 depending on refrigerant type and amount.
- **Evaporator coil replacement (leaking coil):** $1,000–$2,500+; higher if the coil matches an R-410A or newer-refrigerant system.
- **TXV/metering device replacement:** $500–$1,000.

Systems using older R-22 refrigerant cost significantly more to recharge and may make coil replacement or a system upgrade the smarter spend—ask your technician.

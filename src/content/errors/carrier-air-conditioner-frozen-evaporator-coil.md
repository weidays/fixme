## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is not a Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition where ice forms on the indoor evaporator coil (inside the air handler or furnace cabinet). Carrier air conditioners don't flash a specific display code for this; instead you notice weak or warm airflow, ice on the copper lines, water around the furnace/air handler, or the system short-cycling.

The underlying cause is always the same physics: the coil gets colder than 32°F. That happens when either **not enough warm air moves across the coil** (airflow problem) or **the refrigerant is too low or expanding wrong** (refrigerant problem). Condensation on the cold coil then freezes into a growing block of ice, which further chokes airflow and makes the problem worse.

Because refrigerant work and blower diagnostics require gauges, EPA certification, and sealed-system tools, most root causes here are **pro-level**. But the safe first steps — thawing and checking airflow — are things a homeowner can and should do first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Closed, blocked, or too-few supply/return vents** — reduces the air volume across the coil.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm on the fins insulate the coil and cut airflow.
4. **Failing or slow blower motor / wrong fan speed** — not enough CFM across the coil (common on ECM motor faults or a bad capacitor on PSC motors).
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — lower pressure means a colder coil that freezes. R-410A and older R-22 systems both do this.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather (below ~62°F outside)** — head pressure drops and the coil freezes; common with early-season or nighttime cooling.
7. **Blocked or slow condensate drain** — a full pan and high humidity can accelerate icing, and standing water refreezes.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV or fixed orifice)** — meters refrigerant incorrectly, over-cooling the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the system OFF first.** Set the thermostat to cooling OFF, but set the fan to **ON** — running the blower with the compressor off helps thaw the coil faster and safely.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, swap it. This alone fixes many frozen-coil cases.
- **Open and unblock all supply vents and returns.** Move furniture, rugs, and drapes off registers. Don't close too many rooms off.
- **Check the thermostat setting.** Very low setpoints (60–65°F) during cool weather encourage freezing — raise it to 70–72°F.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** If there's standing water, clear the drain line and pan (drain tablets help prevent recurrence). Watch for overflow while the ice melts.
- **Check the breaker.** If the outdoor unit tripped, that's a separate issue — don't repeatedly reset it.
- **Let the coil thaw completely** (can take several hours to a full day) before restarting cooling. Never chip or scrape ice off the coil — you'll damage the fins or tubing.

If the coil re-freezes after a clean filter, open vents, and a full thaw, the cause is internal — stop and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified Carrier tech will:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before any testing (frozen coils give false readings).
2. **Measure airflow** — check blower speed, static pressure, and verify the correct CFM per ton for your system.
3. **Inspect the evaporator coil** for dirt, and the return/supply ductwork for restrictions or crushed flex duct.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and check superheat/subcooling against Carrier's charging chart for your model and refrigerant (R-410A vs R-22).
5. **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low — a low charge always means a leak, since refrigerant isn't consumed.
6. **Test the metering device (TXV)** for proper operation if pressures are off but airflow and charge check out.
7. **Verify blower motor amperage and capacitor** (or ECM module) if airflow is the culprit.

A good quote should name the specific cause (e.g. "1.5 lb low on R-410A with a leak at the evaporator") — not just "add refrigerant," which is a temporary patch on a leaking system.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil, weak airflow | Dirty air filter | Replace filter, thaw with fan ON | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Warm air from vents, coil frosted | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, thaw, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Coil freezes in cool weather | Low outdoor temp cooling | Raise thermostat, avoid cooling below 62°F | Confirm charge and metering |
| Water around furnace/air handler | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain line, add tablets | Flush drain, check pan/pump |
| Repeated freezing after clean filter | Dirty coil or slow blower | Thaw, then call pro | Clean coil, test blower/capacitor |
| Freezing plus loud/humming blower | Failing blower motor or bad capacitor | Turn off, call pro | Replace capacitor or blower motor |
| Charge correct but coil still ices | Faulty TXV / metering device | None — pro required | Replace TXV or orifice |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and system size):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40 — often the only cost.
- **Coil thaw + diagnostic service call:** $80–$200.
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor (PSC or ECM):** $400–$900+ (ECM modules run higher).
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair:** $200–$1,500+ depending on leak location.
- **Refrigerant recharge:** $150–$600 for R-410A; **R-22 systems** run far higher due to phase-out pricing, sometimes $500–$1,200+.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800.
- **Full evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+ if the coil itself is leaking.

If a leaking coil is involved on an older R-22 system, weigh repair against replacing the whole system — the economics often favor replacement.

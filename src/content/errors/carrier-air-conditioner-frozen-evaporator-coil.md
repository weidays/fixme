## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a numeric fault code — it's a physical condition your Carrier air conditioner shows through weak cooling, ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines, and sometimes water around the indoor unit once the ice melts. On Infinity systems with a communicating thermostat, you may see a low-suction or airflow-related fault, but most Carrier systems simply stop cooling well.

The evaporator coil is the indoor cold coil that absorbs heat from your home's air. It's designed to run just above freezing. When airflow drops or refrigerant pressure falls too low, the coil temperature dives below 32°F, and humidity from the air freezes onto it. The ice then blocks airflow further — a runaway loop that ends with a solid block of ice and no cooling.

Because the two root causes (low airflow and low refrigerant) require different fixes, and refrigerant work is EPA-regulated, this is rated a **pro** repair once basic airflow checks fail.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature until it freezes.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow, even with a clean filter.
3. **Closed, blocked, or too few supply/return vents** — reduces airflow the system needs to stay above freezing.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — lower pressure means a colder coil that ices up. This is a repair, not a "top-off."
5. **Failing or slow blower motor / wheel** — including variable-speed motor faults on Performance and Infinity units, or a dirty blower wheel.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below ~60°F outdoors can freeze the coil; most thermostats and outdoor sensors should prevent this.
7. **Restricted metering device (TXV/orifice)** — a stuck expansion valve starves the coil and freezes it.
8. **Blocked condensate drain contributing to poor operation** — less a freeze cause and more a symptom you'll notice once ice melts.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system or the electrical cabinet.

- **Turn off cooling first.** Set the thermostat to **OFF**, or to **FAN ON / cool OFF**. Running the fan without cooling helps thaw the coil faster.
- **Let the coil fully thaw** — 1 to 24 hours. Put towels down; melting ice produces a lot of water.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or older than 60–90 days, swap it. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Open and unblock all vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles aren't closed, covered by rugs, or blocked by furniture.
- **Check the thermostat** — confirm it's set to cool at a reasonable temperature (68–72°F, not 60°F) and, for battery models, that batteries are fresh.
- **Check the breaker** — if the system tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the condensate drain and pan** — clear standing water; a wet/dry vac on the drain line outlet can clear a clog.
- **After thawing and a new filter, restart cooling.** If it freezes again within a day, you have a deeper problem — call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier technician follows the airflow-then-refrigerant path so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirms the ice is fully thawed** before testing — you can't diagnose a frozen system.
2. **Measures airflow and static pressure** across the coil and checks the blower motor and wheel for cleanliness and correct speed (important on ECM/variable-speed units).
3. **Inspects the evaporator coil** for dirt or biofilm blocking airflow.
4. **Attaches gauges to read suction and head pressure** and takes superheat/subcooling readings — this reveals a low charge or a metering-device problem.
5. **Leak-searches** with electronic detector or dye if the charge is low. A responsible tech repairs the leak, not just adds refrigerant.
6. **Checks the metering device (TXV or orifice)** for restriction if pressures point that way.

Be cautious of any quote that adds refrigerant without a leak search — that's a temporary fix that leaks out again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/no cooling, ice on indoor coil | Dirty filter, low airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Verify airflow, static pressure |
| Ice on the copper lines outside | Low refrigerant charge | Turn off, thaw, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Freezes again days after filter change | Dirty coil or blower issue | None — call pro | Clean coil/blower, test motor |
| Water around indoor unit | Melted ice / clogged drain | Vac drain line, add tablets | Clear drain, confirm no leak |
| Cooling drops on cool nights only | Running AC below ~60°F out | Raise thermostat setpoint | Check low-ambient/sensor logic |
| Poor cooling + hissing near coil | Restricted TXV/metering device | None — call pro | Test superheat, replace TXV |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual pricing varies by region and dealer.

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Coil thaw + restart (DIY):** $0
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250 (or DIY for the cost of a vac)
- **Evaporator coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$450
- **Blower motor / capacitor replacement:** $250–$750 (variable-speed ECM motors higher, $600–$1,000+)
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant amount
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$800
- **Evaporator coil replacement (if corroded/leaking):** $900–$2,500

If the coil itself is leaking and out of warranty, get a second opinion — replacement cost may push you toward evaluating the whole system's age.

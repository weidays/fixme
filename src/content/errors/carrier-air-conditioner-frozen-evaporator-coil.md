## What this code means

Carrier air conditioners do not have a dedicated numeric fault code for "frozen evaporator coil" the way furnaces flash ignition or pressure-switch codes. Instead, a frozen coil is a **symptom** you notice directly — ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, weak or warm airflow, water on the floor, or the system running nonstop without cooling.

The evaporator coil freezes when its surface temperature drops below 32°F while moisture in the air condenses on it. That almost always comes down to two root problems: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant** in the system. Both make the coil too cold, and frost snowballs into a solid block of ice.

On **Infinity** systems with a communicating control (SAM/Infinity thermostat), you may see logged faults related to low airflow or abnormal pressures, but the ice itself is a physical condition, not a stored code. Behavior varies by board, so treat any logged code as a clue, not a diagnosis.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature below freezing.
2. **Blocked return or supply vents / closed dampers** — same effect as a dirty filter: not enough air across the coil.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and reduce heat transfer.
4. **Failing or slow blower motor / capacitor** — weak airflow, especially on ECM or PSC blower issues.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — lowers coil pressure and temperature. Requires a pro.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** (below ~60°F outdoors) — outdoor conditions push the coil below freezing.
7. **Thermostat set too low or fan set to a very low speed** for extended runtimes.
8. **Kinked or restricted refrigerant lines / failing metering device (TXV)** — a pro-level diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the system to FAN ONLY (or OFF at the thermostat) and let the coil thaw.** Do not keep running cooling with ice present — this protects the compressor.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A gray, matted, or clogged filter is the number-one cause. Use the size printed on the old one.
- **Confirm all supply vents are open and unblocked** by furniture or rugs, and that return grilles are clear.
- **Check the thermostat:** make sure it isn't set unreasonably low, and if it's programmable, verify the fan setting.
- **Check the breaker** for the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor unit; a tripped breaker can leave the fan off while ice persists.
- **Look at the condensate drain and pan.** As the coil thaws, a lot of water is released. Clear a clogged drain line and empty an overflowing pan (a wet/dry vac on the drain outlet can pull a clog).
- **After a full thaw (1–3 hours), restart cooling** with a fresh filter. If it freezes again quickly, stop and call a pro — that points to refrigerant or airflow problems inside the equipment.

Do **not** attempt to add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or bypass any safety controls. Refrigerant work requires EPA certification.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before testing (running on ice gives false readings).
- Measure **static pressure and airflow** across the coil to catch a dirty coil, weak blower, or duct restriction.
- Inspect the blower motor, wheel, and capacitor for proper speed and amperage.
- Take **superheat and subcooling** readings with gauges to determine if the charge is correct, low, or if the metering device (TXV/fixed orifice) is failing.
- Perform a **leak search** (electronic sniffer, UV dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low.
- Inspect the evaporator coil surface and the condensate drain.

Sanity check: if a tech proposes adding refrigerant, they should also be **searching for the leak** — topping off a leaking system is a temporary fix, not a repair. Ask where the leak is.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on indoor coil / lines, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Thaw, replace filter, reopen vents | Verify airflow, clean coil if needed |
| Warm air from vents, coil iced | Low airflow or low charge | Thaw & swap filter; if it refreezes, stop | Measure superheat/subcooling, leak search |
| Water on floor near air handler | Thawing ice + clogged drain | Clear drain line, vac the clog | Clear drain, install/replace condensate pump |
| Refreezes within hours of thaw | Low refrigerant or bad TXV | None — call pro | Leak search, recharge, repair metering device |
| Weak airflow, humming blower | Blower motor/capacitor fault | Check breaker only | Test/replace capacitor or blower motor |
| Freezes in cool weather (<60°F out) | Running AC too cold outside | Switch to fan/off | Advise on low-ambient operation |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and unit variation applies):

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10–$40
- **Thaw + restart (DIY):** $0
- **Professional coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Condensate pump replacement:** $150–$350
- **Blower capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor (ECM) replacement:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant recharge (with leak repair):** $300–$1,000+ depending on refrigerant type and amount
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$1,500+ (higher on Infinity/high-SEER matched coils)

A frozen coil caught early (filter/airflow) is often a $0–$40 fix. Refrigerant and coil problems are where costs climb.

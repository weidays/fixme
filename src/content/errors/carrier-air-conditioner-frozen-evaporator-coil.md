## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" isn't a numbered Carrier fault code — it's a physical condition your indoor coil develops when its temperature drops below freezing during operation. Ice forms on the coil and refrigerant lines, blocking airflow and killing cooling capacity. On Infinity and some Performance systems you may see reduced-airflow or low-charge messages at the thermostat, but most Carrier ACs have no dedicated code for this. You'll notice it as weak or no cooling, ice on the copper lines at the outdoor unit, or water pooling around the indoor furnace/air handler as the ice melts.

The root problem is always one of two things: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **too little refrigerant**. Both drop the coil below 32°F and condensation freezes instead of draining away.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricts airflow so the coil gets too cold.
2. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow, same effect as a bad filter.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — undercharged systems run the coil below freezing. Very common on older units.
4. **Closed, blocked, or too many shut supply/return vents** — starves the coil of return air.
5. **Weak or failing blower motor / wrong blower speed** — not enough CFM across the coil.
6. **Running the AC in cool weather** (below ~60°F outdoors) — low outdoor temps drop coil temperature into the freezing range.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — standing water can refreeze; also a symptom of an already-freezing coil.
8. **Failed metering device (TXV or piston)** — over-restricts refrigerant flow. Less common, needs a tech.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do — no refrigerant handling, no opening the sealed system:

- **Turn cooling OFF, fan ON.** Set the thermostat to "Off" for cooling but "On" for the fan. This thaws the coil safely by pushing room-temperature air across it. Don't chip or scrape ice.
- **Check the air filter.** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause.
- **Look at your supply and return vents.** Open any closed registers and remove furniture or rugs blocking them. Keep at least 80% of vents open.
- **Check the breaker and thermostat.** Confirm the system has power and the thermostat is set to a sensible temperature (not, say, 60°F while it's cool outside).
- **Check the condensate drain and pan** at the indoor unit for standing water or overflow. Clear a visible clog and drop in a drain tablet.
- **After the coil is fully thawed** (no ice anywhere on lines or coil), restart cooling. If it freezes again within a day, stop and call a pro — that points to low refrigerant or a blower problem.

Do not add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or run the unit repeatedly while frozen.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech works from airflow to refrigerant so they don't misdiagnose a cheap fix as an expensive one:

- **Confirms the coil is thawed** before testing — readings are meaningless with ice present.
- **Inspects filter and evaporator coil** for restriction, then checks return/supply static pressure to measure actual airflow.
- **Tests blower motor amperage and speed tap** (or ECM programming on Infinity systems) to confirm proper CFM.
- **Attaches gauges and measures superheat and subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct. Low charge with high superheat points to a leak.
- **Runs a leak search** (electronic sniffer, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low, rather than just topping it off.
- **Checks the metering device (TXV/piston)** and condensate drain.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search or airflow check, get a second opinion — topping off a leaking system is a temporary and non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, ice on indoor coil | Dirty filter | Replace filter, thaw coil | Verify static pressure |
| Ice on lines, filter clean | Dirty evaporator coil | Thaw, inspect coil | Deep-clean coil |
| Freezes again after thaw | Low refrigerant / leak | Stop running unit | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Some rooms warm, coil freezes | Closed/blocked vents | Open registers, clear vents | Check duct/blower CFM |
| Weak air from all vents | Failing blower motor | Confirm fan runs | Test/replace blower motor |
| Water around furnace, mild ice | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, add tablet | Flush drain line, check pan |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Low outdoor temp operation | Don't run AC below ~60°F | Confirm no other fault |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Your actual cost depends on region, unit age, and access.

- **Filter replacement:** $10–$40 DIY.
- **Coil thaw:** $0 — just time and the fan running.
- **Professional evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor replacement:** $400–$700 (PSC) up to $600–$1,000+ (ECM/Infinity variable-speed).
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A). A leaking evaporator coil replacement can run $1,000–$2,500+.
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800.
- **Service call / diagnostic:** $80–$180, often applied to the repair.

If your system uses older R-22 refrigerant, recharge costs climb sharply and full replacement is often the smarter spend.

## What this code means

Carrier air conditioners do not display a dedicated "frozen evaporator coil" error code the way a furnace shows numbered faults. Instead, you notice it as reduced cooling, ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines, water pooling around the indoor unit, or — on some Infinity systems — a low-airflow, low-suction, or pressure-related fault logged at the control.

A frozen evaporator coil means the indoor coil has dropped below freezing and moisture in the air is condensing into ice on it. Once the coil is iced over, airflow stalls, cooling collapses, and the ice keeps growing. Almost every cause traces back to one of two problems: **not enough warm air moving across the coil**, or **not enough refrigerant in the system**.

This is a **pro-level** repair. You can safely thaw the coil and rule out simple airflow issues, but the common root causes — refrigerant leaks and internal blower problems — require a licensed technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow lets the coil get too cold.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. Requires EPA-certified repair.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil** — dust and biofilm insulate the coil and choke airflow.
4. **Weak or failing blower motor / capacitor** — reduced fan speed starves the coil of warm air (common on ECM or PSC blowers).
5. **Closed, blocked, or too many closed supply/return vents** — reduces total airflow across the coil.
6. **Collapsed or undersized ductwork / dirty blower wheel** — same airflow-starvation effect, harder to spot.
7. **Running the AC in cool weather** — operating below roughly 60°F outdoors can freeze the coil.
8. **Faulty metering device (TXV/piston)** — over-feeds or under-feeds refrigerant to the coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the cooling **turned off** and only the fan running (thermostat to "Fan On," cooling to "Off") to thaw the coil safely:

- **Turn cooling off, fan on.** This melts the ice without slugging the compressor. Wait until all ice is gone — 1 to 3 hours minimum.
- **Replace the air filter** if it is gray, matted, or overdue. This alone fixes many freeze-ups.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it is set to a reasonable temperature (not, say, 60°F) and the fan setting is correct.
- **Open all supply registers and return grilles.** Closing rooms to "save energy" starves the coil.
- **Look for meltwater and clear the condensate drain.** A frozen coil dumps water when it thaws; make sure the drain pan and line aren't overflowing and the condensate pump (if you have one) runs.
- **Check the breaker** for the indoor air handler / furnace — if the blower isn't running, the coil can't get airflow.

Do **not** chip or scrape ice off the coil, add refrigerant, or repeatedly restart a unit that keeps icing. If a clean filter and full thaw don't fix it, stop and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech confirms the root cause instead of just recharging refrigerant. Expect them to:

- Verify the coil is fully thawed before testing.
- Measure **airflow** — blower amp draw, static pressure, and fan speed settings; inspect the blower wheel and coil for dirt.
- Attach gauges and read **suction and head pressures**, then calculate **superheat and subcooling** to judge the refrigerant charge and metering device.
- If low on charge, **leak-search** (electronic sniffer, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) rather than just topping off — a recharge without leak repair will re-freeze within weeks.
- Inspect the TXV/piston and check the metering device operation.
- On Infinity systems, pull any logged fault history from the control to confirm the pattern.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" with no leak search or airflow check, ask why — that's a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, dirty filter | Airflow restriction | Fan-only thaw, replace filter | None if it stays fixed |
| Keeps freezing with clean filter | Low refrigerant / leak | Thaw, then stop | Leak search + repair + recharge |
| Ice + weak airflow at vents | Dirty coil or blower | Thaw, open all vents | Clean coil/blower, test motor |
| Water pooling indoors | Coil thawed, drain clogged | Clear condensate line/pump | Replace pump, clear drain |
| Blower not running | Motor/capacitor/breaker | Check breaker | Replace capacitor or ECM motor |
| Freezes only in cool weather | Low ambient operation | Turn off AC below 60°F | Add low-ambient controls if needed |
| Poor cooling, normal filter | Faulty TXV/piston | Thaw, then stop | Replace metering device |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $90–$200.
- **Coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Blower motor capacitor:** $150–$350.
- **ECM blower motor:** $500–$900.
- **Condensate pump replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$450.
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing varies).
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $400–$800.
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (higher if the coil is out of warranty).

Costs vary by region, refrigerant type, and whether your coil or compressor is still under Carrier's parts warranty — have your model and serial number ready.

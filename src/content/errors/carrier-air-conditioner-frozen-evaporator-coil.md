## What this code means

A frozen evaporator coil isn't a numeric fault code — it's a physical condition where the indoor coil (inside your air handler or furnace cabinet) drops below freezing and builds a layer of ice. On most Carrier air conditioners there's no dedicated "frozen coil" display code, so you usually discover it as weak or no cold air, ice on the copper lines, or water dripping from the indoor unit as the ice melts.

On some **Carrier Infinity** systems the control board may log a related fault — low refrigerant charge, low airflow, or a compressor lockout — that points toward freezing rather than naming it directly. The underlying physics is always the same: not enough heat is reaching the coil to keep the refrigerant above freezing, so condensation on the coil turns to ice.

We've flagged this **pro** because the two most common root causes — low refrigerant and sealed-system problems — legally and practically require an EPA-certified technician. There are safe DIY checks, but the fix often is not DIY.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops coil temperature until it freezes.
2. **Blocked return vents, closed supply registers, or collapsed ductwork** — same airflow-starvation effect as a dirty filter.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — reduces coil pressure/temperature and causes freezing. Never "just top off" refrigerant without finding the leak.
4. **Dirty evaporator coil** — a layer of dust insulates the coil and reduces heat transfer.
5. **Failing blower motor or wrong fan speed** — weak airflow across the coil, common with Infinity/ECM motors set incorrectly after service.
6. **Running the AC in cold weather** (below ~62°F outdoors) — head pressure drops and the coil freezes.
7. **Clogged condensate drain** — a backed-up drain can trip a safety float or worsen icing conditions on some setups.
8. **Failed thermostatic expansion valve (TXV) or metering device** — meters too much or too little refrigerant, causing frost.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the system to FAN ONLY (cooling OFF)** at the thermostat. This starts thawing the coil and protects the compressor. Do this first.
- **Check and replace the air filter.** A gray, clogged filter is the #1 cause. Use the correct size and a MERV rating your system allows.
- **Confirm supply registers are open** and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or curtains.
- **Check the thermostat** — make sure it's on COOL with a reasonable setpoint, and swap batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Look at the breaker.** If the outdoor unit tripped, that can leave the indoor coil in a freezing state; note it for the tech but don't repeatedly reset a breaker that keeps tripping.
- **Inspect the condensate drain and pan.** If water is pooling, clear a visible clog at the drain line with a wet/dry vacuum and add drain tablets.
- **Wait for full thaw** (up to 24 hours) before running cooling again. Never chip or scrape ice off the coil.

If the coil freezes again after a clean filter and clear airflow, stop and call a pro — that pattern points to refrigerant or a metering problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier technician will:

1. **Confirm the coil is fully thawed** before testing — a frozen coil gives false readings.
2. **Measure airflow** and inspect the blower wheel, ECM/PSC motor, and duct static pressure. On Infinity systems they'll verify the correct airflow (CFM) programming.
3. **Read superheat and subcooling** with gauges to determine if the charge is correct — this distinguishes low refrigerant from an airflow problem.
4. **Leak-search** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low, so they fix the leak instead of just recharging.
5. **Inspect the evaporator coil surface** for dust buildup that insulates it.
6. **Test the TXV/metering device** if superheat readings are abnormal.
7. **Pull any stored faults** on Infinity/Performance control boards for airflow or charge-related codes.

Sanity check: any quote that recommends adding refrigerant without a leak search is a red flag. Low charge means a leak — refrigerant doesn't get "used up."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on copper lines, weak cold air | Dirty filter / low airflow | Set to FAN, replace filter, open vents | Measure static pressure, check blower |
| Coil re-freezes after clean filter | Low refrigerant (leak) | Stop running, call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water dripping indoors | Coil thawing / drain clog | Clear visible drain clog, vacuum line | Clear drain, check float switch |
| Frost only in cool weather | Running AC when too cold out | Turn off AC below ~62°F | Verify no other fault; advise operation |
| Warm air + outdoor unit off | Compressor/breaker issue | Note it, don't repeat-reset breaker | Diagnose electrical/compressor |
| Dusty coil visible, poor cooling | Dirty evaporator coil | Nothing safe DIY | Professional coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; regional and Infinity-model variation applies):

- **Air filter replacement:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Evaporator coil cleaning:** $150–$450
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Blower motor (ECM):** $450–$900
- **TXV / metering device replacement:** $350–$750
- **Full evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+

Note: systems using older R-22 refrigerant cost significantly more to recharge, which often tips the decision toward coil or system replacement.

## What this code means

"Leaking water" is a symptom, not a numbered fault code. Goodman condensers and most Goodman indoor coils and air handlers have no leak code to display.

While cooling, the indoor evaporator coil pulls moisture out of the air. On a humid day, a typical home system removes several gallons of water. That water drips into a drain pan under the coil, then leaves through a condensate line to a floor drain, the outdoor ground or a condensate pump.

When you see water around the furnace, the air handler or a ceiling below an attic unit, something in that path has failed. The pan may be overflowing, the line may be blocked or disconnected, or the coil may be icing and then melting.

If your installer fitted a float switch, it cuts cooling when the pan fills. In that case the complaint may be "AC stopped working" instead of a puddle. The switch usually resets on its own once the water drains. Whether you have one depends on the installation.

If water is leaking from a furnace during heating season, see the furnace leak page listed under Related codes instead. That condensate comes from a high-efficiency furnace and is handled differently.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged condensate drain line.** Algae, mold slime, dust and debris build up in the line or its trap. This is by far the most common cause. The pan fills and overflows, or trips a float switch.
2. **Dirty air filter causing coil freeze and meltoff.** Low airflow lets the coil ice up. When the system cycles off, the ice melts faster than the pan and drain can handle. Water also drips from places outside the pan.
3. **Failed or unplugged condensate pump.** Common in basements and attics with no gravity drain. Possible failures include a seized pump, a stuck float, a clogged check valve, a tripped pump safety or a loose power cord.
4. **Disconnected, cracked or poorly sloped drain piping.** PVC joints can separate. A line installed with too little downhill slope, or with a sag, holds water and clogs. A missing or incorrect trap can also stop the drain working properly, especially on draw-through air handlers.
5. **Cracked or rusted drain pan.** This is more likely on older coils with metal pans. The secondary (auxiliary) pan under attic units can also rust through.
6. **Low refrigerant or other causes of coil freezing.** A refrigerant leak or a failing blower freezes the coil and produces meltwater. Diagnosis covered in the frozen evaporator coil and not-cooling pages.
7. **Unit not level, or sweating ducts and plenum.** A tilted coil or air handler sends water past the pan's drain. Uninsulated supply ducts or plenums can also drip condensation in humid spaces. This can look like a unit leak.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn cooling off at the thermostat.** This stops more water being made. Leave the fan on "Auto" or turn it to "On" briefly to help any ice melt. Mop up water and protect anything nearby.
- **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it is gray or clogged. A dirty filter is a leading cause of icing and meltoff leaks.
- **Look for ice.** If you can see frost on the copper line at the indoor unit, let the system defrost for several hours with cooling off. Do not chip at ice. Ongoing icing needs a technician.
- **Check the condensate line outlet.** Find where the drain ends: outside near the foundation, at a floor drain or at a pump. If it is dry while the system runs, the line is probably clogged.
- **Clear the line from the outside end.** Attach a wet-dry shop vacuum to the outdoor end of the line. Seal the gap with a rag or tape and run the vacuum for one to two minutes. Then pour a cup of distilled white vinegar or water into the indoor access port or tee, if your system has one with a removable cap.
- **Check the condensate pump, if you have one.** Confirm it is plugged in and the outlet works. If the reservoir is full and the pump is silent, it needs service or replacement by a technician.
- **Check the secondary pan and drain (attic units).** If the auxiliary pan under the unit has water in it, the primary drain is failing. Water dripping from an eave or overflow line above a window is a warning sign of the same problem.
- **Check the breaker and float switch outcome.** If the AC shut off, it may restart on its own once the drain clears. Make sure the breakers and service switch are on. Do not tamper with or bypass a float switch.
- **Seat the panels.** Make sure the access panels on the indoor unit are fully closed. Gaps can pull in humid air and cause sweating.

Stop and call a technician if any of the following apply:

- the line won't clear
- the pan is cracked
- water is near wiring
- the coil keeps icing

## How a technician will diagnose it

A technician should:

- **Inspect the drain path.** They check the primary pan, trap, slope and fittings, clear the line with nitrogen, a pump or a vacuum, and flush it.
- **Check the pan and coil.** They look for cracks, rust and dirt on the coil, and confirm the unit is level. On many Goodman coils this means removing the coil access panel.
- **Test the condensate pump.** This covers the float, motor, check valve and safety switch.
- **Test the float or condensate safety switch.** They confirm it is wired to cut cooling and that it resets when the water drains.
- **Measure airflow and refrigerant charge if icing is suspected.** This includes static pressure and the blower. They also take superheat and subcooling readings to rule out low refrigerant.
- **Look for duct or plenum sweating.** They check insulation in unconditioned spaces.

A fair quote should name the specific failed part. Be cautious of a recommendation to replace the whole system for what turns out to be a clogged drain.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water pooling around indoor unit, drain outlet dry | Clogged condensate line | Vacuum the outdoor end, flush with vinegar | Clear and flush the line, fix trap or slope |
| AC suddenly stops, pan full of water | Float switch tripped by clog | Clear the drain, wait for restart | Clear the drain, test float switch |
| Ice on the copper line, water after shutoff | Dirty filter or low airflow | Replace filter, let coil thaw | Check blower and static pressure, clean coil |
| Ice returns with a clean filter | Low refrigerant | Turn cooling off, call for service | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Basement pump full and silent | Failed condensate pump | Check power and plug | Replace the pump or its check valve |
| Water in attic secondary pan or dripping from an eave | Primary drain or pan failure | Turn cooling off, check the line | Clear the drain, replace the pan if cracked |
| Constant drip from a pipe joint | Disconnected or cracked PVC | Turn cooling off, catch the water | Re-glue or replace the fitting |
| Water appears on ducts away from the unit | Duct or plenum sweating | Seat panels, check registers are open | Insulate and seal the ducts |
| Leak persists after the drain is cleared | Cracked pan or unit not level | Turn cooling off | Level the unit, replace the pan or coil |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges and vary by region and access. Attic and crawlspace work costs more.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Homeowner drain vacuum, vinegar and filter | $0 – $30 |
| Professional drain line clearing and flush | $100 – $250 |
| Float or condensate safety switch installed or replaced | $100 – $250 |
| Condensate pump replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Drain line repair or re-pipe (slope or trap fix) | $150 – $500 |
| Secondary or auxiliary pan replacement | $150 – $450 |
| Primary drain pan replacement (where serviceable separately) | $300 – $800 |
| Coil cleaning | $150 – $400 |
| Refrigerant leak repair and recharge | $300 – $1,500+, depending on the leak and refrigerant type |
| Evaporator coil replacement (labor-heavy, may be partly covered by parts warranty) | $1,200 – $2,800+ |

Goodman parts warranties often cover the coil for registered units, but labor is usually extra.

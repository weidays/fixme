## What this symptom means

"Breaker keeps tripping" is not a code the American Standard control board displays. It's a symptom. The circuit breaker feeding the outdoor condenser, a 240V double-pole breaker in your main panel, opens because it sensed too much current or a short to ground.

Standard single-stage and two-stage American Standard condensers have no board that logs this event. When the breaker trips, the unit simply loses power. Nothing auto-resets: the unit stays dead until someone resets the breaker.

AccuComfort variable-speed Platinum models are different. Their drive board may store fault codes that help a technician narrow down the cause. Those codes vary by model, so check the service facts sheet inside the unit's control panel cover.

Reset the breaker once. If it trips again, this is professional work. The causes are inside the condenser's electrical compartment, at high voltage.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

This order reflects how often each cause shows up overall. When the breaker trips matters, though. If it trips instantly on reset, suspect a grounded compressor or a wiring short first (causes 2 and 5), not the capacitor. Trips at startup or after minutes of running fit the order below more closely.

1. **Failing run capacitor.** A weak or failed dual capacitor makes the compressor and fan motor draw high current or stall at startup. This is the most common cause, especially after hot summers.
2. **Compressor drawing high amps or shorted to ground.** Possible problems include:
   - a seized or "locked rotor" compressor
   - worn windings
   - a winding shorted to the shell
   
   A grounded compressor usually trips the breaker instantly.
3. **Outdoor fan motor failure.** If the fan motor is seized, or its bearings are dragging, it overheats and pulls excess current. The outdoor coil also can't shed heat, which pushes compressor amps up. Our page "Outdoor fan not spinning" covers the fan side of this.
4. **Dirty outdoor coil or blocked airflow.** Head pressure rises, so the compressor works harder and draws more current until the breaker trips. A clogged indoor filter is a different problem. It mainly lowers suction pressure and can freeze the indoor coil, and it rarely trips the condenser breaker on its own.
5. **Wiring faults.** Examples include:
   - a burned or pitted contactor
   - loose or corroded terminals
   - rodent-chewed wires
   - a damaged whip, the conduit between the disconnect and the unit
   
   Water intrusion in the disconnect box also counts here. A wiring short to ground can trip the breaker instantly.
6. **Breaker problem or wrong size.** A worn breaker, a loose panel lug, or a breaker smaller than the unit's nameplate minimum circuit ampacity requires.
7. **Refrigerant system issues.** Overcharge or a restriction raises pressure and amperage. These are found only with gauges.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Reset the breaker once.** Switch it fully OFF, then ON. Then note whether it trips instantly, when cooling starts, or after minutes of running. Do not reset it a second time.
- **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. This is good maintenance, but a clogged filter rarely causes a breaker trip by itself, so don't expect a filter change to fix the problem.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's set to Cool with a reasonable setpoint, and replace its batteries if it uses them. This rules out erratic short calls.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from outside the cabinet.** Clear leaves, grass clippings and debris from around the unit, and keep about 2 feet of clearance. Do not open panels or reach into the fan.
- **Check indoor vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked.
- **Check the condensate line.** Confirm it isn't backed up or overflowing near the indoor unit or furnace.
- **Note any warning signs.** Watch for burning smells, buzzing or humming from the outdoor unit, or scorch marks on the panel. If you notice any of these, leave the breaker off and say so when you call.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will kill power and verify it's off before opening the electrical compartment. A typical diagnosis then includes these steps:

- Inspect the contactor, terminals and wiring for burning, pitting or loose connections.
- Test the dual run capacitor with a capacitance meter against its µF rating. Being more than about 6% low is a common replacement threshold.
- Megohm or ohm-test the compressor windings (C-S, C-R, S-R) and test each winding to ground. A reading to ground means a shorted compressor.
- Check the fan motor windings and spin the blade by hand, with power off, to check for a seized motor.
- Restore power and measure running amps on the compressor and fan with a clamp meter. Compare them to the nameplate RLA and FLA. Locked-rotor amps at startup that never drop indicate a stuck compressor.
- Verify that the breaker size matches the nameplate. Check the panel lug tightness and breaker condition.
- Read the drive board fault history on variable-speed models.
- Connect gauges only if electrical tests pass, to check for high head pressure or overcharge.

If a quote says "compressor replacement" without winding or amp readings, ask for them before agreeing.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trips instantly when breaker is reset | Shorted compressor or wiring short to ground | Leave breaker off; don't reset again | Ohm/megohm test windings and wiring; repair short or replace compressor |
| Trips when cooling starts; outdoor unit hums | Failed capacitor or locked compressor | Reset once, then leave off | Test and replace capacitor; add hard-start kit or diagnose compressor |
| Trips after 10–30 minutes of running | Overload from dirty coil, weak capacitor or fan motor | Clear debris around unit | Clean coil; test capacitor and fan motor; check amps and pressures |
| Outdoor fan not turning, then trip | Seized fan motor or bad capacitor | Turn off power; don't touch blade | Replace fan motor and/or capacitor |
| Burning smell or scorch marks | Burned contactor or terminal | Leave off; call promptly | Replace contactor; repair damaged wiring |
| Trips on very hot afternoons only | High head pressure, worn breaker or undersized circuit | Keep unit clear | Measure amps; check breaker and wire size; check charge |

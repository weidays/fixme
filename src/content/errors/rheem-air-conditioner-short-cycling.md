## What this code means

"Short cycling" is not a single numbered fault on most Rheem air conditioners. It is a symptom: the compressor starts, runs briefly, shuts off, and restarts again within minutes, without properly cooling or dehumidifying the house.

What stops the cycle depends on the unit:

- **Basic single-stage units:** Short cycling is driven by the thermostat, a failing component, or the compressor's internal overload. No code is shown.
- **Units with high- or low-pressure switches:** A tripped switch opens the control circuit and stops the compressor.
  - Many Rheem boards count repeated pressure-switch trips and enter a soft or hard lockout.
  - A hard lockout typically holds until power is cycled.
- **Two-stage, variable-speed and EcoNet units:** These may flash a diagnostic code on the outdoor board or show an alert on the EcoNet thermostat. The code identifies the trip source. Codes differ by board, so use the chart on your unit's panel label.

Rheem controls usually enforce an anti-short-cycle delay of about 3 to 5 minutes between compressor starts. If the outdoor unit waits a few minutes before restarting, that is normal. Repeated brief run cycles are not.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat issues.** Common culprits are a thermostat placed near a supply register, in sun, or near a heat source. Low batteries, a too-tight cycle-rate or swing setting, or a loose thermostat wire can also cause it.
2. **Restricted airflow.** This includes a clogged filter, closed supply registers, blocked returns or a dirty evaporator coil. These can trip the low-pressure switch or lead to coil icing.
3. **Dirty outdoor coil or poor condenser airflow.** High head pressure trips the high-pressure switch or the compressor's internal overload, especially on hot days.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** This trips the low-pressure switch, which on many Rheem models leads to an eventual lockout.
5. **Failing run capacitor or contactor.** The compressor overheats and cuts out on its internal overload, then restarts once it cools.
6. **Faulty pressure switch or control board.** A switch can open falsely, or a board can drop the compressor signal.
7. **Oversized system.** The unit cools the house quickly but never runs long enough. This shows up as clammy humidity, with no failed part.
8. **Failing compressor.** Common signs are high amp draw, overheating, and repeated overload trips.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it is set to Cool with the setpoint a few degrees below room temperature.
  - Replace the batteries if it uses them.
  - Make sure no lamp, sunlight or supply vent is hitting it.
  - Leave cycle-rate or installer settings alone unless your manual covers them.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it is gray or clogged.
- **Vents and returns:** Open all supply registers and clear furniture or rugs off returns.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look from outside the unit only. Clear leaves, grass and debris to give about 2 feet of clearance. Do not open the panel or spray inside.
- **Condensate:** Check that the drain line is flowing and the pan or float switch is not tripped. On many setups, a full float switch cuts power and causes erratic shutdowns.
- **Breaker and disconnect:** Make sure the breaker and outdoor disconnect are fully on.
- **One reset:** Turn the thermostat off, switch off the AC breaker for 5 minutes, then restore power. This clears a board lockout.
- **When to call:** If short cycling returns, stop resetting and call a technician. If you see ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines, turn cooling off and run the fan only until service arrives.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician will typically:

- **Check thermostat behavior:** Watch it call for cooling and confirm it holds the call steadily at the board. A dropping or chattering call points to the thermostat or wiring.
- **Read board codes:** Retrieve any flashing diagnostic or EcoNet fault history to identify a high-pressure, low-pressure or overload trip.
- **Measure pressures and temperatures:** Use gauges or probes to check superheat and subcooling against Rheem's charging chart. Low charge means a leak search is due before refilling.
- **Test electrical components:**
  - Run capacitor against its µF rating.
  - Contactor contacts.
  - Compressor amp draw and winding resistance.
- **Test pressure switches** for continuity and verify proper trip points.
- **Inspect both coils** for dirt and check blower static pressure and airflow.
- **Evaluate sizing** with a load calculation if no part has failed and the system simply satisfies too fast.

Be skeptical of a quote that adds refrigerant without checking for a leak. Also question a recommendation to replace the compressor when no amperage, winding or capacitor testing has been done.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 2–5 minutes, house not cool | Thermostat placement or setting | Check settings, replace batteries, remove nearby heat sources | Relocate the thermostat, repair wiring |
| Short cycles and airflow is weak | Clogged filter or blocked returns | Replace the filter, open vents | Clean the evaporator coil, check static pressure |
| Ice on the line set or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set cooling off and run the fan only | Find and repair the leak, recharge |
| Outdoor unit cuts out on hot afternoons | Dirty condenser or high head pressure | Clear debris around the unit | Clean the coil, test the high-pressure switch and fan |
| Compressor hums, stops, then restarts later | Weak capacitor or overload trip | One breaker reset only | Replace the capacitor or contactor, test the compressor |
| Board flashes a code, then locks out | Pressure-switch trips counted by the board | Note the code, do one power reset | Diagnose the trip source, replace the switch or board |
| Cools fast but home feels humid | Oversized system | None | Load calculation, adjust blower settings or advise on resizing |

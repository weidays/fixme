## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single flashing code. It describes a Trane heat pump that runs, or tries to run, in cooling mode but doesn't bring the indoor temperature down. A heat pump cools by pumping heat out of the house through the outdoor unit. Anything that stops the outdoor compressor, starves the system of refrigerant or airflow, or leaves the reversing valve in heating position will cause it.

How Trane systems report this depends on the model:

- **Single-stage and two-stage units** (XR, XL, and most Value/Comfort models) usually show no code at all. The unit simply runs warm.
- **Units with a defrost or pressure board** may show LED flashes for high-pressure or low-pressure trips. On many boards, pressure trips reset automatically after a delay. Some lock out after repeated trips until you cycle power.
- **Communicating variable-speed systems** (XV18/XV20i with ComfortLink II) log specific fault codes on the thermostat. If yours does, give that code to your technician.

This page covers cooling mode only. For heating failures, see Trane Heat Pump Not Heating.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat setting or power problem.** The thermostat isn't set to Cool, the setpoint is above room temperature, the batteries are dead, or the outdoor unit's breaker or disconnect is off.
2. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow.** Restricted airflow drops coil temperature, the indoor coil freezes, and cooling stops.
3. **Failed run capacitor.** This is the most common outdoor-unit failure in hot weather. The compressor and/or outdoor fan hums but won't start.
4. **Failed or pitted contactor, or no 24V call reaching the outdoor unit.** The indoor fan runs, but the outdoor unit stays silent.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** The unit runs constantly, the lines or indoor coil ice over, and supply air is only slightly cool.
6. **Dirty outdoor coil or failed outdoor fan motor.** Heat can't be rejected outside, so the high-pressure switch trips, often on the hottest afternoons.
7. **Reversing valve or solenoid fault.** The valve stays in heating position, so the unit blows warm air in Cool mode. Trane heat pumps energize the valve (O terminal) for cooling, so a failed solenoid coil or wiring fault produces exactly this symptom.
8. **Clogged condensate drain tripping a float switch.** The float switch cuts the cooling call if one is installed.
9. **Compressor failure.** This is the least common cause but the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Set it to Cool with the setpoint at least 3°F below room temperature and the fan on Auto. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank. Wait 5 minutes, since many Trane units have a built-in compressor delay.
- **Breakers and disconnect:** Check that both the indoor air handler or furnace breaker and the outdoor unit's double-pole breaker are on. Confirm the outdoor disconnect next to the unit is fully seated or switched on. Reset a tripped breaker **once only**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter is a leading cause of a frozen coil.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, set the thermostat to Off and the fan to On until it fully melts. Don't chip at the ice.
- **Vents and registers:** Open the supply registers and clear furniture away from return grilles.
- **Outdoor unit, from the outside only:** Clear leaves, grass clippings and debris from around the unit. Keep 2 feet of clearance. You can gently hose off the fins from the outside with the power off at the disconnect. Don't open the unit's panels.
- **Condensate line:** If the drain pan is full or the float switch has tripped, clear the drain line opening with a wet/dry vacuum at the outside end. Make sure all access panels on the indoor unit are seated.
- **One reset:** If the unit seems locked out, cycle power off for 5 minutes at the breakers, then restore it **one time**. If it doesn't cool afterward, call for service.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will typically:

1. Verify the thermostat call. They measure 24V at Y, and at O for the reversing valve, at the outdoor unit, and check for any stored fault codes on the board or communicating thermostat.
2. Check high voltage at the contactor and inspect the contacts for pitting.
3. Test the run capacitor's microfarad rating against its label. A reading more than about 6% low is a common replacement threshold.
4. Measure compressor and fan motor amp draw and compressor winding resistance.
5. Connect gauges and measure suction and liquid pressures, superheat and subcooling, and compare them against the Trane charging chart on the unit's panel.
6. Check the temperature split across the indoor coil (typically 16–22°F) and the static pressure and airflow.
7. If refrigerant is low, leak-search with an electronic detector, bubbles or a nitrogen pressure test. A good tech won't just "top off" the system without discussing the leak.
8. Verify reversing valve operation by checking line temperatures and solenoid coil voltage and resistance.

**Red flags in a quote:** a compressor condemned without winding and amp tests, repeated refrigerant top-offs with no leak search, or a full system replacement quoted for a capacitor-type symptom.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nothing runs at all | Thermostat, batteries, breaker | Check settings, replace batteries, reset the breaker once | Test transformer, low-voltage fuse, wiring |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Contactor, no 24V call, disconnect off | Confirm the outdoor breaker and disconnect | Test and replace contactor, trace control wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan not spinning | Failed run capacitor or fan motor | Shut off power and call | Test and replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Runs nonstop, air only slightly cool | Low refrigerant, dirty coil | Replace filter, rinse outdoor coil from outside | Leak search, repair, recharge to spec |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Dirty filter, low airflow, low charge | Set to Off with fan On to thaw, replace filter | Check airflow, blower, and charge |
| Blows warm air in Cool mode, outdoor unit running | Reversing valve or solenoid fault | Verify the thermostat is set to Cool | Test O signal and solenoid, replace coil or valve |
| Cools in morning, quits on hot afternoons | High-pressure trip, dirty outdoor coil, weak fan | Clear debris, rinse coil | Clean coil, test fan and capacitor, check charge |
| Water near indoor unit, cooling stopped | Clogged condensate, float switch tripped | Clear the drain line | Clean pan and trap, verify switch |
| Breaker trips when compressor starts | Shorted or grounded compressor, bad capacitor | Reset once only, then call | Megohm and winding tests, hard-start kit or replacement |

## Repair costs

Typical US installed prices, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Filter, thermostat batteries, clearing debris (DIY) | $0 – $40 |
| Service call or diagnostic fee | $90 – $200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Condensate drain clearing or float switch | $100 – $300 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350 – $800 |
| Outdoor coil cleaning | $100 – $250 |
| Leak search plus recharge (R-410A) | $400 – $1,500+, depending on leak location and pounds needed |
| Reversing valve solenoid coil | $150 – $400 |
| Reversing valve replacement | $1,000 – $2,500 |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200 – $2,800 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $3,500+ (often partly covered by Trane's parts warranty if registered) |

Older systems using R-22 refrigerant cost much more to recharge, and major repairs on them often make replacement the better value. Check your Trane registration status, since many units carry a 10–12 year limited parts warranty.

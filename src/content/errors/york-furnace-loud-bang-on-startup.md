> **Gas safety first — read this before anything else.** If you smell gas (a rotten-egg or sulfur odor) anywhere in the house, or you hear repeated booming from the furnace, **do not touch any switches, thermostats, breakers, phones or lights** — those can create a spark. Get everyone out of the house, then call your gas utility's emergency line or 911 **from outside** or from a neighbor's. Do not go back in until the utility or fire department says it's safe. Do not try to relight or re-run the furnace to see whether the noise happens again.

## What this code means

A loud bang on startup is not a flash code — it's a **physical symptom** your York furnace shows when something is wrong with how it lights or how the system expands under heat. There are two very different causes that sound similar to a homeowner:

1. **Delayed ignition (a combustion "boom").** Gas flows into the burner box but doesn't light immediately. During that delay, unburned gas accumulates. When it finally ignites, it lights all at once with a bang. This is the serious one — repeated delayed ignition can crack the heat exchanger.
2. **Thermal expansion of ductwork or the cabinet (an "oil-can" pop).** As warm air hits cold sheet metal, panels flex and snap back with a bang. Annoying, but not dangerous.

York furnaces don't log a dedicated fault code for a startup bang, so your control board may run normally or may post an **ignition/flame-proving fault** if the delayed light trips the sequence. If your board is flashing, match it to the related codes below. This page covers the noise itself.

**Treat a combustion bang as pro-level.** It points to burner, igniter, gas-mixture, or heat-exchanger issues that are inside the cabinet and outside the DIY boundary. If you believe the bang is coming from the furnace itself at the moment the burners light, the correct action is to **shut the furnace off at the furnace switch or breaker, leave it off, and call a licensed technician** — not to restart it and listen again.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Delayed ignition from dirty or misaligned burners.** Carbon, rust, or debris on the burner ports slows the flame from traveling across all burners, letting gas build before it catches. The most common cause of a true combustion bang.
2. **Thermal expansion (ducts/cabinet).** A genuine oil-can pop from sheet metal, not combustion. For homeowner-reported "loud bang on startup" this is an extremely common source of the complaint — plenty of bangs that sound alarming turn out to be a flexing supply plenum or duct panel. It's lower severity, but it is not rare.
3. **Weak or slow hot-surface igniter / spark electrode.** An aging igniter takes longer to reach light-off temperature, extending the gas delay before ignition.
4. **Incorrect gas pressure or mixture.** Manifold pressure set too high or too low changes how cleanly the burners light. This is set by a tech with a manometer.
5. **Blocked or restricted flue / venting.** Poor exhaust draft can disturb combustion and ignition timing, contributing to rough or delayed lights.
6. **Lazy flame crossover between burners.** Partially blocked crossover ports mean the far burners light late — a small internal bang each cycle.
7. **Cracked or deteriorating heat exchanger.** Often a *result* of chronic delayed ignition rather than a cause, but it changes airflow around the burners and can worsen the noise.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Stick to these — anything inside the cabinet is a technician's job.

- **If you smell gas or hear repeated booming, stop here.** Don't flip switches, leave the house, and call the gas utility or 911 from outside. Everything below assumes there is no gas odor.
- **If the bang is at the furnace cabinet at the moment the burners light, shut it down.** Turn the furnace off at the furnace switch or the breaker and **leave it off** until a licensed technician has seen it. Do not restart it to confirm the noise, and do not reset a lockout on a furnace you suspect is banging from delayed ignition — that invites another unburned-gas ignition event. Set the thermostat to Off as well, and use space heating safely elsewhere if the house gets cold.
- **Listen and locate (from a safe distance, once).** A bang at the furnace cabinet right at ignition = combustion (shut it off, call a pro). A pop from the ductwork a second or two *after* the blower starts, away from the furnace, = likely sheet-metal expansion.
- **Replace a dirty air filter.** Low airflow contributes to overheating and expansion noise. A clean filter is cheap and safe. Read the size printed on the edge of the old filter and buy that size — don't guess.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Heat and the setpoint is above room temperature so the furnace isn't short-cycling. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Look at your supply and return registers.** Make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture or rugs; restricted airflow aggravates expansion pops.
- **Breaker / furnace switch reset — only if the noise is confirmed duct expansion.** If you've established the pop is sheet metal in the ductwork (not a bang at the burner box) and the furnace has locked out, you may flip the furnace switch or breaker off, wait 30 seconds, and back on **once**. If it locks out again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting. Never use this reset on a furnace you suspect of delayed ignition.

**Do not** open the burner compartment, inspect the heat exchanger, adjust gas pressure, or clean burners yourself. Those are documented technician tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A sanity check for any quote you receive:

- **Observe a full ignition cycle** to confirm whether the bang is combustion or duct expansion.
- **Inspect and clean the burners and crossover ports**, looking for carbon, rust, or scale blocking the flame path.
- **Test the igniter/electrode** — ohm the hot-surface igniter or check spark quality and timing against spec.
- **Measure manifold gas pressure** with a manometer and compare to the York rating-plate value; adjust the gas valve if off.
- **Check draft and the flue/venting** for blockage or improper sizing.
- **Inspect the heat exchanger** with a camera and/or combustion analyzer for cracks caused by repeated delayed ignition.
- **Verify flame-sensor response time** so the board proves flame promptly.

Expect a diagnostic fee plus parts/labor. A good tech will tell you *which* of the above is at fault rather than blanket-replacing parts.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas odor (rotten egg/sulfur) anywhere in the house | Possible gas leak | Touch nothing, leave the house, call the gas utility or 911 from outside | Utility makes safe; tech repairs after clearance |
| Repeated booming from the furnace | Recurring delayed ignition | Leave the house and call the gas utility or 911 from outside; do not reset | Full combustion and heat-exchanger inspection |
| Sharp bang at cabinet right when burners light | Delayed ignition from dirty burners | Shut off at switch/breaker, leave it off, call a pro — do not relight | Clean/align burners, test ignition sequence |
| Bang plus a flashing ignition fault code | Slow igniter extending gas delay | Shut off at switch/breaker; note the flash count and the board's legend for the tech | Test/replace igniter, check flame proving |
| Metallic pop from ducts after blower starts | Sheet-metal thermal expansion | Confirm registers open, filter clean | Add duct bracing/insulation, check airflow |
| Rough or whooshing light, intermittent bang | Incorrect gas pressure or mixture | None — shut off and call; do not adjust gas | Measure manifold pressure, adjust gas valve |
| Bang worsening over weeks, sooty smell | Blocked flue or failing heat exchanger | Shut off furnace, leave it off, call a pro | Inspect flue/draft and heat exchanger |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **New air filter (DIY):** $10–$40.
- **Burner cleaning / adjustment:** $100–$250 as part of a tune-up.
- **Hot-surface igniter replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Gas valve / manifold pressure adjustment:** $100–$300 (included in some service calls).
- **Flue/venting correction:** $150–$600 depending on the fix.
- **Duct bracing or insulation (for expansion pops):** $150–$500.
- **Heat exchanger replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+, or often a trigger to replace an older furnace entirely.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $100–$250, frequently credited toward the repair.

A startup bang left unaddressed can turn a cheap burner cleaning into a heat-exchanger replacement, so get it looked at early.

## Related codes

A note on flash counts: York furnace boards (also sold under the Johnson Controls, Luxaire and Coleman names) **do not use one universal flash scheme**. The same number of flashes can mean different things on different control-board generations — a given count may be documented as ignition lockout on one board and as a different fault on another. Read the flash-code legend printed on the label inside the blower door of *your* furnace before acting on any count you find online.

## What this code means

"Keeps shutting off" is not a single fault code. It describes a furnace that starts heating and then shuts down before the thermostat is satisfied. On American Standard gas furnaces, this almost always means a safety control stopped the burners, and the integrated control board records why.

To narrow it down, look at the LED on the control board through the sight glass in the lower door, without removing the door. Count the flashes and compare them to the diagnostic label inside the door.

**Flash meanings vary by board generation.** The list below shows codes that are common on many American Standard boards, but it is not universal. **The label on your blower door is the authority.** If your label gives a different meaning for a flash count, go by the label.

Common on many boards:

- **2 flashes:** failed ignition or flame loss, ending in a lockout. On most boards, this is the code you will see when the burners light, lose flame during a call for heat, and the board gives up after its retries.
- **3 flashes:** pressure switch or inducer problem.
- **4 flashes:** open limit circuit, which usually means overheating. On some boards, the rollout and auxiliary limit switches share this same 4-flash indication.
- **6 flashes:** board-dependent.
  - On some boards, 6 flashes means a rollout or auxiliary limit trip.
  - On others, it means reversed polarity or a poor ground.

  Check your label to see which applies.
- **8 flashes (some newer boards only):** low flame signal. Where it exists, it is often a warning while the furnace keeps running, not the shutdown code. Older Freedom-era boards and many single-stage boards don't use it at all.

**Rollout switches:** a tripped rollout switch shows whatever code your door label assigns to rollout. That may be the shared 4-flash limit code, a separate code such as 6 flashes, or another count.

Detailed pages for several of these codes are listed under Related codes.

**What happens after a shutdown:**

- **Limit trips:** the limit switch resets itself once the heat exchanger cools. The board then retries.
- **Flame or ignition faults:** most boards retry a few times within one call for heat. After that, they enter a soft lockout, typically shown as 2 flashes. On many boards, the lockout clears on its own after about an hour, or when power is cycled. Lockout timing and retry counts differ between board generations, so your door label is the authority.

If the LED shows a steady or slow normal pattern and no fault, the shutdown is probably coming from the thermostat side rather than the furnace.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Overheating from restricted airflow (high-limit trip).** A dirty filter, closed or blocked registers, a dirty blower wheel, or a weak blower motor or capacitor lets the heat exchanger get too hot. The limit opens, the burners shut off, and the blower keeps running. This is the leading cause of mid-cycle shutdowns.

2. **Weak or lost flame signal (dirty or failing flame sensor).** The burners light and run for seconds to minutes. Then the gas valve closes because the board can no longer prove flame. Poor grounding of the burner assembly can cause the same result.
   - After its retries, most boards lock out and show 2 flashes.
   - Some newer boards may also show 8 flashes as a low-flame-signal warning.

3. **Pressure switch opening mid-cycle.** The switch can open because of:
   - a partially blocked vent or intake, such as ice, nests or debris
   - a cracked or water-filled pressure hose
   - a weakening inducer motor
   - condensate backing up in a high-efficiency unit

   When the switch opens, the board drops the gas valve.

4. **Thermostat issues.** Possible culprits include low batteries, a loose connection, a thermostat mounted near a heat source, or a cycle-rate setting that is too aggressive. A faulty thermostat can also simply stop calling for heat.

5. **Rollout or auxiliary limit trips.** Flame rolling out of the burner area can come from a blocked heat exchanger or flue. This is a serious condition. Depending on the board, it may show as the shared limit code or as its own code, so check your door label. Some rollout switches must be manually reset, but **only a technician** should do that, after finding the cause.

6. **Failing control board or loose wiring.** Intermittent board relays or loose low-voltage or high-voltage connections can drop the burners randomly. This is less common. A technician should confirm it only after ruling out the causes above.

7. **Blocked or cracked heat exchanger.** A blocked or cracked heat exchanger can trip limits repeatedly even when airflow is good. This is uncommon, but it is the most expensive outcome. A cracked heat exchanger can also let carbon monoxide (CO) into the home.
   - Keep working CO alarms on every level of the house.
   - If a CO alarm sounds, leave the house right away and call 911 or your gas utility from outside.

   An oversized furnace more often causes short cycling than limit trips. See the short cycling page under Related codes.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Replace the air filter.** A clogged filter is the number one cause of limit trips.
  - Hold the filter up to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it.
  - Match the size printed on the frame.
  - Make sure the airflow arrow points toward the furnace.
  - Avoid very high-MERV filters unless your system was designed for them.
- **Open registers and clear returns.** Aim for at least 80% of supply registers open. Keep return grilles free of furniture, rugs and curtains.
- **Check the thermostat.**
  - Replace the batteries.
  - Confirm it is set to HEAT and the setpoint is a few degrees above room temperature.
  - Make sure it isn't in direct sun or near a lamp, TV or supply register.
- **Check the furnace switch and breaker.** Make sure the service switch near the furnace is ON and the breaker hasn't tripped. Reset a tripped breaker once. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Make sure the blower door is fully seated.** A loose door can open the door safety switch and cut power. Press it firmly into place. Don't remove panels to look inside.
- **Inspect the outside vent pipes (high-efficiency furnaces).** Look at the PVC intake and exhaust where they leave the house. Clear snow, ice, leaves or nests you can reach from outside, without disassembling anything.
- **Check the condensate drain (high-efficiency furnaces).** Confirm the visible drain line isn't kinked or clogged. Also check that any condensate pump isn't full or sitting in water.
- **Do one reset only.**
  1. Note the LED flash count before resetting.
  2. Turn off power at the furnace switch for 30 seconds.
  3. Turn it back on.

  If the furnace shuts off again, leave it off and call a technician. Repeated resets can mask a dangerous condition.

If you smell gas at any point, leave the house, don't operate switches, and call your gas utility's emergency line or 911 from outside. If a CO alarm sounds, leave the house and call 911 or your gas utility from outside.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should follow roughly this sequence:

1. **Read the fault history.** They check the board LED and, on communicating models, the stored fault codes. They interpret the flash count using the label for your specific board.
2. **Measure temperature rise.** They compare return and supply temperature against the range on the furnace rating plate. A rise above the range points to an airflow problem or limit issue.
3. **Check airflow.** This includes static pressure, blower wheel cleanliness, blower speed tap settings, and the blower motor and capacitor.
4. **Test the limit and rollout switches.** They test continuity and look for signs of rollout, such as scorching or discoloration near the burners.
5. **Measure flame signal.** They read the flame sense current in microamps, then clean or replace the flame sensor and verify the burner ground.
6. **Check the pressure switch circuit.** They measure inducer draw, inspect the hoses, check the vent and intake for restriction, and confirm the condensate trap drains properly.
7. **Check gas pressure and burner condition.** This confirms the furnace isn't overfired.
8. **Inspect the heat exchanger and check combustion.** This is done if limits keep tripping despite good airflow.
9. **Inspect the board and wiring.** This is the last step, after the other causes have been eliminated.

Be cautious of a quote to replace the control board or the whole furnace if the tech hasn't taken these three readings:

- temperature rise
- static pressure
- flame signal

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burners shut off, blower keeps running, restarts minutes later | High-limit trip from overheating | Replace filter, open registers, clear returns | Measure temperature rise and static pressure, clean blower, check motor and capacitor, test limit |
| Burners light, run briefly, then shut off; after several tries LED shows 2 flashes (some newer boards may show 8 flashes as a low-flame warning) | Weak or lost flame signal, ending in lockout | One reset only | Clean or replace flame sensor, check burner ground |
| Inducer runs, burners drop out; LED shows 3 flashes | Pressure switch opening | Clear outside vent terminations, check condensate drain | Test inducer, hoses and pressure switch; clear vent or trap |
| Shuts off at random with no fault code | Thermostat or power interruption | Replace batteries, check settings, reseat blower door, check breaker | Check thermostat wiring, door switch and board connections |
| Won't restart after shutdown; scorch marks visible; LED shows the rollout code on your door label | Rollout switch tripped | Leave it off; don't reset | Find the cause of rollout, inspect flue and heat exchanger |
| Shuts off repeatedly even with a new filter | Blocked or cracked heat exchanger, or failing blower | Call a technician; keep CO alarms working and leave the house and call 911 or your gas utility if one sounds | Combustion analysis, heat exchanger inspection, blower testing |

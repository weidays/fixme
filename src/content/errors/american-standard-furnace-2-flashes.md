## What this code means

Two flashes on your American Standard furnace's diagnostic LED means the control has entered a **system lockout because the number of ignition retries or recycles was exceeded**. In plain terms, the furnace tried to light (or relit) more times than the board allows within one call for heat, gave up, and shut down for safety.

This lockout is **not permanent**. The documented behavior is an **automatic reset after approximately 1 hour**, at which point the control will attempt the ignition sequence again. You can also force a reset once by cycling power. If the furnace lights and runs normally after that, a one-time hiccup may have caused it. If it locks out to 2 flashes again, the root cause is still present and the unit needs professional diagnosis.

Because the 2-flash code is triggered by whatever prevented a **clean, sustained flame** across several attempts, the fix is almost always inside the cabinet — which is technician territory.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty flame sensor** — the most common trigger. The sensor can't confirm flame, so the board recycles and eventually locks out.
2. **Weak or misaligned igniter (hot surface igniter)** — degraded igniters fail to light the burners reliably across retries.
3. **Restricted airflow** — a badly clogged filter or blocked return can cause repeated recycles as the furnace overheats and drops out.
4. **Flame-proving / grounding issues** — poor burner ground, corroded sensor wiring, or a compromised connection interrupting flame signal.
5. **Marginal inducer or pressure-switch behavior** — an inducer or venting condition that intermittently satisfies then loses proof during retries.
6. **Control board fault** — a failing IFC that miscounts or mistimes the ignition sequence.

Note: flash-code legends differ slightly between board revisions. Your technician will confirm the code meaning against the label on the burner-compartment door — don't open that compartment yourself — so treat the ranking above as general guidance rather than a diagnosis of your exact unit.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do:

- **Thermostat:** Make sure it's set to Heat with the setpoint above room temperature. If it's battery-powered, install fresh batteries.
- **Air filter:** Pull and inspect it. If it's gray, matted, or clogged, replace it — a starved furnace can recycle itself into lockout.
- **Breaker / furnace switch:** If tripped, reset the breaker once. You may also cycle the furnace's service switch **once** to reset the lockout.
- **Vents and registers:** Confirm supply and return grilles throughout the house are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate line (high-efficiency models):** Check for standing water or an obvious blockage at the drain; a full condensate trap can interrupt operation.

**Do not** open the cabinet or the burner compartment, and do not touch the flame sensor, igniter, wiring, or any safety switch. If you reset once and it locks out again, stop and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

- Verify the flash count against the legend on the burner-compartment door label and read the stored flash/fault history from the IFC to confirm it's a retry/recycle lockout, not a neighboring code.
- Watch a full ignition sequence to see where it fails — igniter glow, gas valve open, flame establish, flame proof.
- **Clean or replace the flame sensor** and verify microamp flame-signal readings against spec.
- Inspect and test the **hot surface igniter** resistance and output.
- Verify **burner ground** and check sensor/valve wiring and connections.
- Confirm airflow, inducer operation, and pressure-switch proving through the cycle.
- Test the **control board** if all upstream components check out.

A quote should name the specific failed part and the measured reason — not just "replace the board."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ignites then shuts off, retries, locks out | Dirty flame sensor | Replace filter, reset once | Clean/replace flame sensor, verify flame signal |
| No flame at all across attempts, then lockout | Weak hot surface igniter | Reset once, confirm thermostat | Test and replace igniter |
| Short cycling before lockout | Restricted airflow / clogged filter | Replace filter, clear vents | Inspect blower, ductwork, limit switch |
| Runs briefly then drops out repeatedly | Bad burner ground / wiring | None | Repair ground, connections, wiring |
| Lockout with unusual inducer noise | Marginal inducer / pressure proving | None — note the noise for the technician | Test inducer and pressure switch |
| Random relocks after all else checks out | Failing control board | Reset once | Diagnose and replace IFC |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Filter replacement (DIY):** $10 – $40
- **Thermostat batteries (DIY):** $5 – $15
- **Service diagnostic / trip fee:** $90 – $180
- **Flame sensor clean or replace:** $80 – $250
- **Hot surface igniter replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Pressure switch replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Inducer motor replacement:** $400 – $1,200
- **Integrated furnace control board:** $400 – $1,200

The 2-flash lockout most often resolves at the low end — a flame-sensor cleaning — so beware of quotes that jump straight to a board or inducer without documented readings. Conversely, a genuine inducer or control-board replacement can legitimately reach four figures once labor is included.

## Related codes

- **1 flash / continuous on** — normal operation or system status (varies by board).
- **3 flashes** — pressure switch / draft-proving fault.
- **4 flashes** — open thermal protective device, typically a limit (varies by board revision).
- **Flame-sensed / flame-proving codes** — related to the same ignition sequence but reported separately.

Because American Standard board revisions can assign codes differently, the flash count should be matched to the legend on your furnace's burner-compartment door label — a step for the technician on site, not a reason to open the compartment yourself.

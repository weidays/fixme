## What this code means

"Breaker keeps tripping" is not a code shown on a Carrier thermostat or control board. It is a symptom: the circuit breaker feeding your heat pump shuts off power because it sensed too much current or a short.

A Carrier split heat pump usually has two separate high-voltage circuits:

- **The outdoor unit.** This circuit powers the compressor and the outdoor fan.
- **The indoor air handler or fan coil.** This circuit powers the blower and, on most systems, the electric auxiliary or emergency heat strips.

Which breaker trips is the single most useful clue.

**Reset behavior:** A breaker never resets by itself. It holds off until someone switches it back on. Carrier's own controls behave differently:

- Infinity/Evolution systems may also show a high-current or compressor fault on the user interface. Those fault-history codes help a technician.
- Variable-speed (Greenspeed/inverter) models may show drive fault codes on the outdoor board.

If the breaker trips within seconds of being reset, there is likely a hard short. If it trips after minutes or hours of running, the cause is more likely an overload or a weakening part.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** This is the most common cause on single- and two-stage units. Without proper capacitor support, the compressor or fan motor struggles to start, draws locked-rotor current and trips the breaker. A bulging or leaking capacitor is a classic sign.
2. **Hard-starting or shorted/grounded compressor.** Worn bearings, failing windings or a short to ground make the compressor draw far too much current at startup. A grounded compressor usually trips the breaker instantly.
3. **Pitted or welded contactor and loose or burned connections.** Burned contacts and loose lugs at the contactor, the breaker or the disconnect cause arcing and heat that trip the breaker. Outdoor connections are also exposed to moisture and pests.
4. **Auxiliary heat strip faults.** This applies when the indoor breaker is the one tripping. A shorted heating element, a failed sequencer or relay, or a melted terminal block is a common cause in winter.
5. **Outdoor fan motor failure.** A seized or shorted fan motor can trip the breaker directly. It can also make the compressor overheat and overload because the coil is not being cooled.
6. **Damaged wiring.** Rodent-chewed, chafed or water-damaged wiring in the outdoor unit or the whip can short to ground.
7. **Worn or incorrectly sized breaker.** Breakers weaken with age. A breaker that is undersized for the unit's rating can also trip under normal load. Check the nameplate "Max Fuse/Breaker" value against the installed breaker.
8. **Heavy operating load.** A very dirty outdoor coil, an incorrect refrigerant charge, or a badly iced coil can raise compressor amperage until the breaker trips. These are usually contributing factors rather than the sole cause.
9. **Inverter drive fault (variable-speed Infinity/Greenspeed only).** A failed drive board or one of its components can short the supply. This does not apply to single- or two-stage models.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Identify which breaker tripped.** Check whether it is the outdoor unit (heat pump/AC) breaker or the air handler/electric heat breaker, and write it down for the technician.
- **Reset it once only.** Turn the thermostat to OFF first. Then flip the breaker fully to OFF and back to ON. Wait about 5 minutes before setting the thermostat back to Heat or Cool, because many Carrier controls have a built-in compressor delay. If the breaker trips again, leave it off.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it is not set to Emergency Heat by mistake, since that runs the heat strips at full load. Replace the batteries if the display is weak or blank.
- **Replace a dirty air filter.** A clogged filter overheats the heat strips and the blower, which can trip the indoor breaker.
- **Look at the outdoor unit without touching anything inside.** Clear away leaves, snow, ice buildup around the base, or debris blocking the coil. Make sure the exterior panels are seated.
- **Check the condensate line and indoor drain pan.** Water overflow near the air handler can contribute to electrical faults. Clear a visibly clogged condensate drain line if you can do it without opening the cabinet.
- **Watch for warning signs.** A burning smell, buzzing, humming without starting, or scorch marks on the breaker or disconnect mean you should keep the power off and call a technician.

Do not open the outdoor unit or air handler cabinet. Do not touch the disconnect internals, and do not replace the breaker yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically do the following:

- **Inspect the electrical path.** They check the breaker, the outdoor disconnect, the whip and the lugs for heat damage. They also confirm that breaker size matches the nameplate MCA (minimum circuit ampacity) and max breaker rating.
- **Test the capacitor.** They measure capacitance in microfarads (µF) with a meter and compare it to the rating on the label.
- **Check the compressor windings.** They measure resistance across the C, S and R terminals. They also run an insulation-resistance (megohm) test to ground to detect a grounded compressor.
- **Measure amperage draw.** They clamp the compressor and fan motor during startup and while running, and compare readings to the RLA and LRA values on the nameplate.
- **Inspect the contactor.** They check the contactor for pitting or welding, and on two-stage models they check the compressor's staging solenoid.
- **Test the fan motor.** They check the windings and bearings and confirm the motor spins freely.
- **Check the heat strips** if the indoor breaker trips. They measure element resistance and check each element to ground, plus the sequencers, relays and limit switches.
- **Read the fault history.** On Infinity and Evolution systems, they pull fault history from the user interface and outdoor board. On Greenspeed models, they check the inverter drive status codes.
- **Check operating pressures** if electrical tests pass but amperage is high. A refrigerant charge problem must be handled by an EPA-certified technician.

**Sanity check on quotes:** Be cautious of a quote to replace the compressor or the whole system that is not backed by a winding or megohm reading. Ask what readings led to the diagnosis.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor breaker trips instantly on reset | Grounded compressor or shorted wiring | Leave the breaker off | Megohm test of the compressor, inspect the wiring, repair or replace |
| Outdoor unit hums, then the breaker trips | Failed capacitor or hard-start compressor | Reset once, then leave off | Test and replace the capacitor, consider a hard-start kit |
| Trips after running a while, worse in hot weather | Fan motor failure, dirty coil, overload | Clear debris from around the unit | Test the fan motor, clean the coil, check amperage and charge |
| Indoor breaker trips in cold weather | Shorted heat strip or failed sequencer/relay | Replace the filter, make sure Emergency Heat is off | Test the elements and relays, replace the heater parts |
| Burning smell or scorched breaker | Loose or burned lug, failing breaker | Keep the power off | Repair connections, replace the breaker or disconnect |
| Old breaker trips at normal load | Worn or undersized breaker | Note the breaker size and age | Verify against the nameplate, replace the breaker |
| Variable-speed unit trips the breaker | Inverter drive fault | Reset once, note any codes | Diagnose the drive with Carrier's procedures, replace the board |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, parts and labor included:

| Repair | Cost range |
|---|---|
| Service call and diagnosis | $90–$250 |
| Run or dual capacitor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Hard-start kit | $200–$450 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Breaker replacement (electrician or HVAC tech) | $150–$400 |
| Disconnect or whip replacement | $200–$500 |
| Wiring repair (rodent or chafe damage) | $150–$600 |
| Outdoor fan motor replacement | $350–$800 |
| Heat strip element or sequencer/relay | $200–$700 |
| Inverter drive board (variable-speed Infinity/Greenspeed) | $800–$2,000+ |
| Compressor replacement | $2,000–$4,500+ |

Parts may be covered under warranty if the system is registered, but labor often is not.

If the compressor is grounded on an older system, compare the repair quote against the cost of a new heat pump. A new system typically runs $6,000–$12,000+ installed.

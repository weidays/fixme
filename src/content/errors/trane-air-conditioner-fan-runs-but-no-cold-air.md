## What this code means

"Fan runs but no cold air" is a symptom, not a numbered error code. The indoor blower moves air through your vents, but the cooling side of your Trane AC is not doing its job. First rule out the thermostat settings. Once those are correct, the problem is almost always at the **outdoor condenser**. Either the compressor is not running, or it is running without enough refrigerant to absorb heat.

This page focuses on that specific pattern: steady airflow inside with warm or room-temperature air at the registers. For a broader look at weak or partial cooling, see the related pages listed at the end.

XR units generally lack communicating diagnostics and give no fault display, whether they are single-stage or two-stage. A technician has to test them to find the cause. Communicating XV and XL systems often log an alert on the ComfortLink II thermostat. Write down any alert code before you call, because it speeds up diagnosis.

There is normally no lockout to clear. A compressor stopped by its internal thermal overload restarts by itself once it cools. Some communicating-system alerts clear automatically, and others hold until a technician clears them.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat set to Fan On, or set wrong.** The thermostat may be in the wrong mode, the setpoint may be too high, or the fan may be on "On" with no cooling call. In each case the blower runs and nothing cools. This is the most frequent cause and the easiest to rule out, so check it first.
2. **Failed run/start capacitor.** This is the most common Trane condenser failure, especially after heat waves. With a weak capacitor, the compressor hums but will not start, or the outdoor fan won't spin. The indoor blower keeps running normally.
3. **Outdoor power interrupted.** A tripped condenser breaker or an open disconnect box next to the unit cuts power to the outdoor unit. The indoor blower is on a separate circuit, so it keeps running.
4. **Faulty contactor.** The contactor is the switch that powers the condenser. Pitted contacts, an open coil, or insects stuck between the contacts can stop the outdoor unit from starting even though the thermostat is calling for cooling.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** The compressor runs, but the air comes out only slightly cool or warm. Trane spine-fin and some aluminum coils are known to develop leaks. A low charge can also freeze the coil.
6. **Low-voltage control problem.** Several faults keep the condenser from getting its call to run:
   - a blown low-voltage fuse on the indoor control board (typically 3- or 5-amp)
   - a broken thermostat wire to the condenser
   - a tripped pressure switch

   On some models, a float or condensate safety switch can also cut the call.
7. **Compressor overheating on its internal overload.** A dirty condenser coil, a failing outdoor fan motor, or high heat makes the compressor shut itself off. It cycles back on after it cools.
8. **Failed compressor.** The compressor may have a shorted or grounded winding, or it may be mechanically seized. This is the least likely cause and the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Set the system to **Cool**, put the setpoint at least 3°F below room temperature, and set the fan to **Auto**. With the fan on "On," the blower runs all the time, even when there is no cooling call. Replace the thermostat batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Breakers:** At the main panel, find the AC/condenser breaker. It is usually a double-pole 240V breaker. If it is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Open only the normal access cover of the box near the condenser. Make sure the pull-out block or handle inside is fully seated and set to On. Pulling or reseating that handle or block is the intended homeowner action. You will also use it to cut power before rinsing the coil. Do not open the box beyond its normal access cover or touch any wiring.
- **Air filter:** Replace a dirty filter. A clogged filter can freeze the coil and cut cooling.
- **Look and listen at the condenser (panels on, don't touch anything):** Note whether the fan is spinning, whether the unit is silent, or whether it hums or clicks. Pass this on to your technician.
- **Around the unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and stored items so there are at least 2 feet of clearance around the condenser. You can gently hose debris off the outside of the fins. First turn the power **off at the disconnect** by pulling the handle or block through the normal access cover. Never use a pressure washer.
- **Condensate line:** Your system may have a float switch, and the drain pan or line may be full. If so, clear the line at the accessible cleanout, for example with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain end. A full pan can shut off cooling.
- **Registers:** Open the supply and return vents in the rooms you use.

After any reset, give the system **5 minutes or more** before expecting cooling. Many Trane systems have a built-in compressor delay. If there is still no cold air after one attempt, set the thermostat to Off and call for service. Running it longer risks damaging the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically work through these steps:

1. **Confirm the call.** They verify 24V at the condenser contactor coil (Y and C terminals) and check the control board fuse and thermostat wiring. On a communicating system, they read the stored alerts.
2. **Check line voltage.** They confirm 240V at the disconnect and contactor and inspect the contactor points.
3. **Test the capacitor.** They measure capacitance in microfarads against the rated value, usually allowing ±6%. They also check for bulging or leaking.
4. **Test the compressor.** They check winding resistance (common, start, and run) and test for a short to ground. They also take an amp draw at startup to tell a locked rotor from a tripped overload.
5. **Measure refrigerant performance.** They take suction and liquid pressures, superheat, and subcooling against Trane's charging chart. They also measure the temperature split across the indoor coil, which is normally about 16–22°F.
6. **Leak search, if the charge is low.** They use an electronic detector, bubbles, or nitrogen pressure testing. Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a short-term fix.

Ask for the test results behind any quote:

- If a quote says "needs refrigerant" but includes no pressure, superheat, or subcooling readings, ask for them.
- If it says "bad compressor," ask for the winding resistance and ground test results.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower runs all the time, no cooling | Thermostat fan set to On, or no cooling call | Set the mode to Cool and the fan to Auto, and replace the batteries | Check the thermostat wiring and the board |
| Outdoor unit completely silent | Tripped breaker, open disconnect, blown control fuse, or failed contactor | Check the thermostat mode, reset the breaker once, and check the disconnect | Test 24V and 240V, then replace the fuse or contactor |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan not spinning | Failed capacitor | Turn the thermostat to Off to protect the compressor | Test and replace the capacitor, then check the fan motor |
| Outdoor fan spins, compressor silent, air warm | Bad capacitor section, compressor on overload, or failed compressor | Turn off, wait an hour, and try once | Check the amp draw and windings, then replace the capacitor or compressor |
| Air only slightly cool, runs nonstop | Low refrigerant from a leak | Replace the filter and clear around the unit | Check pressures and superheat/subcooling, find and repair the leak, then recharge |
| Cools briefly, then blows warm | Overheating from a dirty coil or weak fan motor | Clear debris and gently rinse the outside of the coil with the disconnect pulled | Clean the coil, then test the fan motor and capacitor |
| Warm air with a full drain pan | Float switch cutting the cooling call | Clear the condensate line | Clear the drain and verify the float switch |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $80 – $200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150 – $450 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Control board fuse / thermostat wire repair | $100 – $250 |
| Leak search | $200 – $600 |
| Leak repair plus recharge (R-410A or R-454B) | $400 – $1,500+ |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350 – $800 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100 – $250 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $3,500+ (may be partly covered by Trane's parts warranty if registered) |
| New condenser (if the compressor or coil fails on an older unit) | $4,000 – $8,000+ |

Check your registration status before approving a major repair. Registered Trane units typically carry 10 years of parts coverage, and some models have longer coverage on the compressor. Labor is usually not covered. Older units that use R-22 are costly to recharge, so replacement is often the better value.

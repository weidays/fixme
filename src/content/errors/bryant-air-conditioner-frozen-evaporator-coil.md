## What this code means

"Frozen evaporator coil" is a condition, not a numbered Bryant fault code. The indoor coil sits above your furnace or inside the air handler. When it gets colder than 32°F, moisture in the air freezes on it instead of draining away as water.

Common signs include:

- Frost or ice on the copper suction line (the larger, insulated line) at the outdoor unit or near the furnace.
- Weak airflow from the supply vents.
- Warm air from the vents, or water around the furnace as the ice melts.

Most Bryant air conditioners have no sensor that detects icing, so nothing on the unit resets or locks out. The ice simply builds until the cause is fixed.

Behavior varies by model:

- **Evolution communicating systems** with variable-speed equipment may log related alerts on the Evolution Connex control, such as low suction pressure or blower airflow faults.
- **Standard single-stage and two-stage systems** with conventional thermostats usually show nothing.

This page covers icing specifically. For broader cooling failures without ice, see the separate Bryant AC not-cooling guide.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter.** This is the most common cause. Too little warm air crosses the coil, so the coil temperature drops below freezing.
2. **Closed or blocked supply vents and return grilles.** Furniture, rugs or many closed registers reduce total airflow.
3. **Dirty evaporator coil.** Dust and biological growth on the coil fins restrict airflow and reduce heat transfer. This often happens when the system ran without a filter.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Low charge drops coil pressure and temperature below freezing. Bryant systems do not "use up" refrigerant, so low charge means there is a leak.
5. **Blower problems.** Examples include a failing blower motor or motor capacitor, a blower speed or airflow setting that is too low for the system's tonnage, or a dirty blower wheel. On Evolution systems, airflow setup is configured in the control.
6. **Restricted refrigerant metering device or liquid line.** This includes a clogged filter-drier or a restricted TXV or piston, which starves the coil of refrigerant.
7. **Running in cool weather.** Running the AC when it is below about 60–65°F outside, or setting the thermostat very low for long periods, can let the coil drop below freezing. This is especially likely when airflow is already marginal.
8. **Ductwork restrictions.** Collapsed flex duct, undersized returns, or a closed zone damper can all cut airflow.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thaw it first.** At the thermostat, set the system mode to **Off** and the fan to **On**. Let the coil thaw completely, often 1 to 3 hours and sometimes longer. Don't chip at the ice, and don't use a heat gun or hair dryer near the coil.
- **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty or if you can't easily see light through it. Use the size printed on the old filter's frame, and make sure the airflow arrow points toward the furnace or air handler. Avoid very restrictive high-MERV filters unless your system was designed for them.
- **Open vents and clear returns.** Make sure supply registers are open throughout the house. Return grilles should not be blocked by furniture, curtains or rugs.
- **Review thermostat settings.** Confirm the thermostat is in Cool mode with a reasonable setpoint, around 72–78°F. Avoid running the AC on cool nights. Replace the thermostat batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Watch the condensate drain.** As the ice melts, check that water is flowing to the drain or condensate pump and that the pan isn't overflowing. If the drain line has a cleanout cap, you can flush it with a cup of water or white vinegar. If a float switch shut the system off, it should restart once the water drains.
- **Check the breaker and disconnect.** Confirm the furnace or air handler switch and both AC breakers are on.
- **Seat the panels.** Make sure the furnace or air handler doors are closed and fully seated.
- **Test, then stop.** Once the coil is fully thawed, run cooling and check the suction line over the next few hours. **If ice comes back with a clean filter and open vents, stop and call a technician.** Repeatedly thawing and restarting risks damaging the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically:

- Confirm the coil is fully thawed before measuring anything. Readings taken on an iced coil are meaningless.
- Measure **total external static pressure** and check temperature drop across the coil to evaluate airflow.
- Inspect the blower wheel, motor and capacitor, and verify the blower speed or airflow configuration matches the system's tonnage.
  - On Evolution systems, they will review the Connex fault history and airflow settings.
- Inspect the evaporator coil face for dirt. This may require removing an access panel or the coil cover.
- Connect gauges and measure **suction pressure, superheat and subcooling**, then compare them to the Bryant charging chart on the outdoor unit's panel.
  - TXV systems are charged by subcooling.
  - Piston systems are charged by superheat.
- If the charge is low, perform **leak detection** with an electronic detector, bubble solution, or a nitrogen pressure test. Common leak spots are the evaporator coil, Schrader valves and brazed joints.
- Check the filter-drier and metering device for restriction, using temperature drop across the drier or abnormal pressure readings.

Be wary of a quote that just "adds refrigerant" with no leak search. Also question one that condemns the compressor before airflow and charge have been verified.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil and suction line, weak airflow, dirty filter | Restricted airflow from the filter | Fan On and system Off to thaw, then replace the filter | None, unless the coil refreezes |
| Ice returns within hours with a clean filter | Low refrigerant from a leak | Turn the system off and call for service | Leak search, repair, evacuate and recharge to spec |
| Weak airflow at all vents, filter clean | Dirty coil or blower wheel, wrong blower speed | Open all registers and clear the returns | Clean the coil and blower wheel, set correct airflow |
| Blower runs slowly, hums or is intermittent | Failing blower motor or capacitor | Turn the system off and call | Test and replace the capacitor or motor |
| Freezing only on cool nights or at very low setpoints | Running in low outdoor temperatures | Raise the setpoint, don't cool when it's cool outside | Verify charge and airflow if it persists |
| Ice plus water leaking at the furnace | Meltwater overwhelming the drain | Thaw, flush the drain line, check the pump | Clear a blocked trap, replace the pump or float switch |
| Ice forming at one point on the liquid line | Restricted filter-drier or metering device | Turn the system off and call | Replace the drier or TXV, then recover and recharge |
| Evolution control shows a low-pressure or airflow alert | Charge or airflow issue (codes vary by model) | Note the code, thaw, check the filter | Diagnose from the fault history and correct the cause |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor (regional variation applies):

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter (DIY) | $10–$40 |
| Service call and diagnosis | $90–$250 |
| Evaporator coil cleaning | $150–$500 |
| Blower motor capacitor | $120–$300 |
| Blower motor (PSC) | $350–$700 |
| Blower motor (ECM or variable-speed) | $600–$1,500 |
| Leak search | $150–$500 |
| Minor leak repair (Schrader valve, brazed joint) | $200–$600 |
| Filter-drier replacement (includes refrigerant recovery and recharge) | $250–$600 |
| TXV replacement | $400–$1,200 |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$2,800+ |

**Refrigerant recharge** is charged per pound and depends on the refrigerant type:

- **R-410A:** about $75–$150 per pound installed.
- **Newer R-454B systems:** pricing varies.
- **Older R-22 systems:** often $150+ per pound, which usually makes replacement worth considering.

**Warranty:** Coils are often covered under Bryant's 10-year registered parts limited warranty, but labor and refrigerant usually are not. Check your registration status before approving a coil replacement.

## What this code means

On Lennox integrated furnace controls (IFC) that display alphanumeric E-codes, the documented meaning of **E125** is: **the control failed its internal self-check — replace the control.**

This is different from codes that point to an external part (like an igniter, pressure switch or gas valve). With E125, the control is reporting a fault it found in *itself*, and the manufacturer's stated remedy is replacement of the control rather than repair of some other component.

**Whether you will ever see "E125" at all depends on which control generation your furnace has.** Newer SureLight/iComfort-style boards with a 7-segment display or a communicating interface show alphanumeric E-codes and can report this fault in words. Older Lennox boards that signal faults by flashing an LED do not display E-codes at all, so the same class of internal-control problem would appear differently — or not as a numbered code — on those units. Exact code wording can also vary by board revision. Always match the code you see against the diagnostic chart printed on your furnace's blower-door label or in the literature for your specific control before assuming a meaning.

Because a control that cannot verify itself cannot safely sequence ignition, the furnace typically will not run, or will lock out, while this fault is active. A single power cycle may clear a one-time electrical glitch. If the code comes back, treat it as a real control fault and hand it to a technician — do not keep resetting the furnace.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed control board — hardware or internal memory (most likely).** The self-check failure is, by definition, a fault the board found in its own circuitry or stored memory. Whether the root cause is a damaged component or corrupted internal memory, the board has judged itself unreliable, and the documented response is the same: replace the control.
2. **A power surge or brownout event.** A voltage spike or dip can damage the board's logic and trip the self-check. If a storm or utility event preceded the code, this is a common trigger.
3. **Moisture or corrosion on the board.** Condensate leaks, flue moisture or a humid cabinet can corrode traces and connectors, causing the self-check to fail intermittently, then permanently.
4. **Loose or faulty low-voltage/24 VAC supply to the board.** Unstable power feeding the board can cause it to fail its startup check. A technician verifies clean, correct voltage before condemning the board.

If the code appears only once and never returns after a single power cycle, it may have been a transient event. A recurring fault points to a failed control.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do. Everything else here is technician work.

- **Check your thermostat.** Confirm it's set to Heat and the temperature is above room temperature. If it uses batteries, replace them.
- **Replace a dirty air filter.** A clogged filter can cause other symptoms; rule it out with a clean filter.
- **Cycle the furnace power once.** Turn the furnace breaker (or the switch on the unit) off, wait 30 seconds, and turn it back on. This is your **one allowed reset** to clear a possible transient glitch. **Do not repeat it.** Repeatedly resetting a gas furnace that has locked itself out forces repeated ignition attempts on a system that shut down for a reason, and that is unsafe.
- **Confirm the furnace switch is on.** The service switch (looks like a light switch near the furnace) must be in the ON position.
- **Look for obvious water.** If you see water pooling near the furnace or a full condensate line, that moisture can affect the board — note it for your technician. You may clear a visibly clogged **external** condensate drain line or trap, and wipe up water around the float switch area outside the cabinet. **Do not remove any panel and do not reach inside the cabinet** — a suspected water-damaged board is live electrical equipment and is strictly technician territory.
- **Check visible vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return registers aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.

If the code returns after one power cycle, **stop and call a licensed HVAC technician.** Do not open the cabinet or attempt board repairs.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. **Read the code and history** stored on the board, confirm which control generation is installed, and confirm the fault is current, not a stored past event.
2. **Verify incoming power** — line voltage and the 24 VAC control supply — to rule out a power problem masquerading as a board failure.
3. **Inspect the board and connectors** for corrosion, burnt traces, moisture, or scorch marks.
4. **Check grounding and polarity** on the furnace, since electrical faults can stress the board.
5. **Power-cycle and re-run the self-check** to see whether the fault clears or immediately returns.
6. **Confirm the board is unrecoverable** and replace it with the correct genuine Lennox IFC for that model and revision, then re-verify a full heat cycle.

A good tech won't just swap the board blindly — they'll confirm clean power and no moisture source first, so the new board doesn't fail the same way.

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor):

- **One-time power cycle (transient glitch):** **$0** if it clears and never returns.
- **Diagnostic / service call:** **$90 – $180** typical.
- **Control board (IFC) replacement:** **$450 – $1,200** installed. Communicating and variable-speed (SLP-class) boards sit at the top of that range — the part alone frequently exceeds $500. Genuine Lennox boards carry a premium, and the revision must match the model.
- **Correcting a power/moisture source** (e.g., condensate leak, loose connection) alongside the board: add **$100 – $300** depending on the fix.

If the furnace is under Lennox parts warranty, the board itself may be covered — you'd pay labor only. Ask your technician to check the serial-number warranty status before quoting.

## Related codes

Neighboring Lennox E-codes cover things like line-voltage problems, low control voltage, and polarity or grounding faults — but the exact number-to-meaning mapping **varies by control board generation and revision**, and older LED-flash boards don't use E-codes at all. Rather than guess, read the diagnostic chart printed on your furnace's blower-door label or in the installation literature for your specific control, or have your technician confirm the code against Lennox documentation for that board.

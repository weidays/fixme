## What this code means

"Ice on refrigerant line" is not a fault code that your Trane AC displays. It is a visible symptom: frost or ice forming on the copper refrigerant lines, usually the larger insulated **suction line** that runs between the indoor coil and the outdoor unit. Sometimes the frost appears at the service valves on the outdoor unit.

That line is designed to run cold, about 40–50°F. Ice means the refrigerant inside is evaporating below 32°F. The usual causes are too little heat reaching the indoor coil (an airflow problem), too little refrigerant (a leak), or a restriction or metering fault.

This page covers what ice on the **line set** specifically tells you. If the indoor coil itself is a block of ice, see the Frozen evaporator coil page. That page covers the coil-side causes in more depth.

Some points about how the system behaves:
- Ice on the line usually means ice is also building on the evaporator coil, even if you cannot see it yet.
- **Frost only on the small liquid line**, often near a filter-drier or a kinked spot, points strongly to a restriction at that location rather than an airflow problem.
- **Frost at the outdoor unit's suction service valve** with the indoor airflow normal leans toward low charge.
- Most Trane conventional units do not lock out for this condition. Some variable-speed (XV) and communicating systems can trip a low-pressure safety and show an alert. The behavior depends on the model and the board.
- The ice does not "reset" on its own. It melts when cooling stops, but the system will refreeze if the cause remains.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Restricted airflow across the indoor coil.** A dirty air filter is the most common trigger, followed by closed or blocked supply registers, blocked return grilles, or a dirty evaporator coil. Less heat reaches the coil, so its temperature drops below freezing and the cold spreads down the suction line.
2. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Low charge drops suction pressure and the saturation temperature. Frost often starts at the outdoor service valve or at the coil inlet. Leaks in Trane indoor coils are well documented, especially formicary corrosion in older copper coils, along with leaks at flare and brazed joints.
3. **Indoor blower problems.** A failing blower motor or capacitor, a dirty blower wheel, or an incorrect fan speed setting can all reduce airflow. On variable-speed air handlers, the cause may be a misconfigured airflow (CFM) setting or a failing ECM motor.
4. **Metering device or line restriction.** A TXV that is stuck closed or has a failed bulb, a clogged piston orifice, a plugged filter-drier, or a kinked line set can starve the coil. This cause typically produces frost right at the point of restriction.
5. **Running at low outdoor temperatures or low thermostat settings.** Running cooling when it is below roughly 60–65°F outside, or setting the thermostat very low for long periods, can push coil temperatures below freezing. This is especially likely on oversized systems.
6. **Undersized or leaky return ductwork.** The coil does not get enough air volume even with a clean filter. This is common after renovations or in systems that were never sized properly.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Turn cooling off and set the fan to ON.** At the thermostat, set the system to OFF and the fan to ON. Running the blower melts the ice faster and protects the compressor. Allow 2–4 hours. Do not chip or scrape the ice, and do not use heat guns.
2. **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it is gray, clogged, or has been in place more than 1–3 months. Make sure the airflow arrow points toward the blower.
3. **Open the supply registers and clear the returns.** Make sure most of the vents are open. Move furniture, rugs, or curtains off return grilles.
4. **Check thermostat settings and batteries.** Avoid very low setpoints, such as below about 68°F, for long run times. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or the unit acts erratic. Do not run AC cooling on cool nights in the 50s.
5. **Watch the condensate as the ice melts.** A lot of water will drain as the ice melts. Make sure the drain line is flowing and that the secondary pan or float switch isn't full. If water is overflowing, see the Leaking water page.
6. **Check that the exterior panels are seated.** A loose blower door can pull in unfiltered air, which dirties the coil. Some units also won't run the blower properly with the door loose. Press the panels firmly into place, but do not open them.
7. **Restart and observe.** Once the line and coil are fully thawed, run cooling again. If frost reappears within hours or a day, stop using the AC and call a technician. Running a system that keeps freezing risks compressor damage.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified HVAC technician should do most of the following. You can use this list to sanity-check a diagnosis or quote.

- **Confirm full thaw first.** Readings taken on a frozen system are misleading.
- **Measure airflow.** This includes static pressure across the system, temperature drop across the coil (typically 16–22°F), blower speed and settings, the blower wheel condition, and the coil face for dirt.
- **Test blower components.** The tech will check motor amperage, the run capacitor (on PSC motors), and ECM motor and board operation.
- **Read pressures and temperatures.** The tech will use gauges or probes to calculate **superheat** (for piston systems) and **subcooling** (most Trane TXV systems are charged by subcooling per the unit nameplate). Low subcooling with high superheat points to undercharge. High subcooling with high superheat points to a restriction.
- **Check for a temperature drop at a restriction.** A noticeable temperature change across the filter-drier or a frost spot on the liquid line indicates a blockage.
- **Hunt for leaks if the charge is low.** Methods include an electronic leak detector, bubble solution, a nitrogen pressure test, or UV dye. Indoor coils, Schrader valves, and service valve cores are common leak points. A good tech finds the leak instead of just "topping off."
- **Evaluate the ductwork.** The tech checks for undersized returns or disconnected duct runs.

**Red flags in a quote:** a recharge with no leak search, repeated annual "top-offs," or a recommendation for a full system replacement before any pressures or airflow have been measured.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on the suction line plus weak airflow from the vents | Dirty filter, blocked returns or registers | Thaw with the fan on, replace the filter, open the vents | Clean the coil or blower if airflow is still low after the filter change |
| Frost at the outdoor service valve; the house cools poorly | Low refrigerant from a leak | Turn cooling off and run the fan to thaw | Leak search, repair, evacuation, and recharge to Trane spec |
| Ice returns within a day despite a new filter | Blower motor, capacitor, or settings problem | Confirm the filter and vents are clear, then call | Test the motor and capacitor, check ECM/CFM settings, replace parts |
| Frost on the small liquid line at one spot | Clogged filter-drier, kinked line, or stuck TXV | Turn cooling off | Diagnose the restriction, replace the drier or TXV, repair the line |
| Freezes only on cool nights or very low setpoints | Low load or oversized system | Raise the setpoint and avoid cooling below about 60°F outside | Verify the charge and airflow; discuss sizing or controls |
| Ice plus water overflowing near the indoor unit | Melting ice exceeding the drain capacity | Check and clear the condensate line, empty the secondary pan | Clear a clogged drain or trap, check the float switch |
| Thermostat shows a pressure or airflow alert (communicating models) | Low-pressure safety trip | Record the alert and do one power reset only | Read the fault history and fix the underlying charge or airflow problem |

## Repair costs

These are typical US price ranges for 2026, including parts and labor unless noted. Prices vary by region and by refrigerant type. R-410A and the newer A2L refrigerants (R-454B, R-32) have different pricing.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Replace air filter (DIY) | $10 – $40 |
| Service call / diagnostic fee | $80 – $200 |
| Evaporator coil and blower wheel cleaning | $150 – $500 |
| Blower run capacitor | $120 – $300 |
| PSC blower motor replacement | $400 – $900 |
| ECM / variable-speed blower motor | $800 – $2,000 |
| Leak search (electronic, nitrogen, or dye) | $150 – $500 |
| Minor leak repair (Schrader core, flare, braze joint) | $150 – $600, plus the recharge |
| Refrigerant recharge (R-410A, per typical job) | $200 – $800+, depending on pounds needed |
| Filter-drier replacement | $250 – $600 |
| TXV replacement | $600 – $1,500 |
| Line set repair or replacement | $300 – $2,000+ |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,500 – $3,500+ |
| Return duct correction | $500 – $2,500+ |

Many Trane coils carry a 10-year registered parts warranty, often 12 years on some lines. Check your registration before you pay for a coil. Labor is usually not covered unless you bought an extended plan.

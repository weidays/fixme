## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a numbered fault code. Most Amana split-system air conditioners have no outdoor diagnostic display. When cooling stops, you diagnose from what the system is and isn't doing:

- **Is the indoor blower moving air?**
- **Are the outdoor fan and compressor running?**
- **Is there ice on the indoor coil or the refrigerant lines?**

Some Amana models do show codes when cooling fails:

- **Communicating models**, such as the AVXC20 with ComfortBridge or units paired with a communicating thermostat, show codes on the outdoor board LED or the thermostat.
- **Some two-stage units** have a control board that flashes pressure-switch or compressor-protection faults.

If your unit has a status LED, record the flash pattern before you cut power. It speeds up diagnosis considerably.

Because the causes include electrical components and refrigerant, this page is rated **pro**. Homeowners can rule out the simple causes, but most real fixes happen inside the cabinet.

**Related page:** For an Amana heat pump that won't heat, see "Amana Heat Pump Not Heating." Heat pumps share many of the outdoor-unit causes below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow.** Reduced airflow lowers capacity and can freeze the indoor coil. This is the single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat setting, dead batteries, or wiring issue.** Check for the wrong mode, a setpoint above room temperature, or a blank screen.
3. **Tripped breaker or outdoor disconnect switched off.** The indoor blower runs, but the outdoor unit is dead.
4. **Failed run capacitor.** The outdoor unit hums but the fan or compressor won't start. This is the most common outdoor component failure.
5. **Failed or pitted contactor.** The outdoor unit gets no power, or it won't shut off.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** Signs include weak cooling, long run times, and ice on the suction line or coil. Amana evaporator coils are known leak points, as are flare and Schrader valve connections.
7. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil.** High head pressure can trip the high-pressure switch on equipped models.
8. **Condensate safety (float) switch tripped.** On many installs, this switch cuts power to the thermostat circuit when the drain clogs.
9. **Outdoor fan motor failure.** The compressor overheats and shuts off on its internal overload.
10. **Indoor blower motor or blower control failure.** This includes ECM motors on variable-speed models.
11. **Compressor failure or a stuck compressor.** This is the least common cause and the most expensive.

**About a frozen evaporator coil:** Ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines is a consequence, not a separate cause. It usually comes from restricted airflow (cause 1), low refrigerant (cause 6), or a weak indoor blower (cause 10). Thawing the coil restores cooling only temporarily until the underlying cause is fixed.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat.** Set it to **Cool** and **Auto**, with the setpoint at least 3°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank. Then wait 5 minutes, because many units have a built-in compressor delay.
- **Air filter.** Pull it out. If it's gray or clogged, replace it with one of the same size.
- **Breakers.** Check the furnace/air handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Outdoor disconnect.** Confirm the switch or pull-out block near the outdoor unit is in place and turned on. Don't open the box beyond the pull-out.
- **Vents and outdoor clearance.** Open all supply registers and clear the return grilles. Remove leaves, grass, and debris from around the outdoor unit, keeping about 2 feet of clearance. Don't spray or touch the coil fins while the unit has power.
- **Ice.** If you see ice, set the system to **Off** with the fan on **On** until it fully melts. Then retry.
- **Condensate.** Check the drain line and pan for standing water. If your system has a condensate pump, make sure it runs. A clogged drain trips the float switch. Flushing the accessible drain line with a cup of vinegar is fine.
- **Panels.** Make sure the furnace or air handler blower door is fully seated. A loose door switch can shut down the whole system.
- **One power reset.** If the unit has a status LED and seems locked out, record the flash pattern first. Then cycle power once. Don't repeat it.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech usually works through these checks:

1. **Verify the thermostat call.** They confirm 24V on Y and G at the indoor board and at the outdoor contactor.
2. **Check line voltage and the contactor.** They confirm 240V at the disconnect and contactor, and inspect the contacts for pitting.
3. **Test the capacitor.** They measure microfarads against the label rating. Dual run capacitors more than 6% under rating are typically replaced.
4. **Measure amp draw.** They check the compressor and fan motor against the nameplate RLA/FLA to spot motor or compressor faults.
5. **Read refrigerant pressures and temperatures.** They gauge the system and calculate superheat and subcooling using Amana's charging chart. Most Amana units with TXVs are charged by subcooling.
6. **Search for leaks if the charge is low.** They use an electronic detector, bubble solution, or a nitrogen pressure test. The evaporator coil, Schrader valves, and flare connections are common leak points.
7. **Check airflow.** They inspect the blower, test the motor, check static pressure, and look at coil cleanliness.
8. **Read board codes on equipped models.** On communicating or board-equipped units, they read the fault history.

**Red flags in a quote:**

- Adding refrigerant without a leak search, especially more than once.
- Condemning the compressor without amp, winding, and pressure tests.
- Recommending full system replacement for a capacitor or contactor failure.

Also ask whether the unit is still under Amana's parts warranty. Many registered units carry a lifetime compressor warranty, and many carry 10-year parts coverage.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nothing runs, thermostat blank | Dead batteries, tripped breaker, float switch, blower door open | Replace batteries, reset breaker once, check drain, seat panel | Check transformer, low-voltage fuse, wiring |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped outdoor breaker, disconnect off, contactor, thermostat wiring | Check breaker and disconnect | Test contactor, 24V signal, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan doesn't spin | Failed capacitor or fan motor | Turn off at thermostat to protect compressor | Replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Runs constantly, weak cooling | Dirty filter, dirty condenser, low refrigerant | Replace filter, clear debris around unit | Clean coil, leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Off/Fan On to thaw, replace filter, open vents | Check charge and blower, repair leak |
| Water near furnace, system off | Clogged condensate drain tripped float switch | Clear or flush accessible drain, check pump | Clear trap, replace pump or switch |
| Outdoor unit starts then shuts off quickly | Overheating compressor, high-pressure trip, bad capacitor | Clear around unit, power reset once | Pressure and amp tests, fan motor, coil cleaning |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted compressor or motor, wiring | Don't reset again | Electrical diagnosis, component replacement |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter (DIY) | $5–$40 |
| Thermostat batteries / DIY thermostat | $5 / $30–$250 |
| Service call / diagnostic | $75–$200 |
| Run capacitor | $120–$400 |
| Contactor | $150–$400 |
| Condensate drain clearing or float switch | $100–$250 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $300–$750 |
| Leak search + repair + recharge (R-410A) | $400–$1,500+ |
| Indoor blower motor (PSC / ECM) | $400–$700 / $600–$1,500 |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$2,800 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,000+ (parts may be covered by warranty; labor usually isn't) |

**Notes on refrigerant costs:**

- Refrigerant prices vary widely.
- R-22 systems (older Amana units) are costly to recharge.
- Newer A2L-refrigerant units (R-32 on current Amana models) require A2L-certified service practices.
- If the unit is older than about 12–15 years and needs a coil or compressor, compare the repair cost against replacement.

## What this code means

"Runs constantly" is a symptom, not a numbered fault code. Whether your unit shows any diagnostic information varies by model and control board. Many Amana air conditioners have no display, so the unit simply keeps running.

The symptom shows up in two different ways, and each points to a different set of causes:

1. **It runs and cools, but never reaches the setpoint.** The system is losing capacity, or the house gains heat faster than the AC can remove it.
2. **It keeps running when it shouldn't.** The outdoor unit keeps going after the thermostat is satisfied or switched to OFF. This is an electrical control problem, usually a stuck contactor or a shorted thermostat wire.

Some long run times are normal:

- **Two-stage and inverter models:** Amana's two-stage and variable-speed units (for example, the AVXC inverter series) are designed to run long cycles at low capacity. Long run time alone is expected on these models.
- **Units with onboard diagnostics:** Depending on the model and control board, these may store fault codes that a technician can read. There is nothing to "reset."
- **Extreme heat:** Near the unit's design temperature, a correctly sized system can run continuously for hours.

Because this is a condition rather than a lockout, nothing auto-resets. The unit runs until the cause is fixed or power is removed.

This page focuses on run-time and shutoff problems. For a unit that runs but blows warm air, see the related pages listed at the bottom.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or restricted airflow.** Reduced airflow cuts capacity, so the system runs longer. Severe restriction can lead to a frozen coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil.** Cottonwood, grass clippings and dust block heat rejection. Capacity drops and run times stretch out.
3. **Thermostat problems.** Possible issues include:
   - Setpoint too low for the conditions
   - Fan set to ON (the blower runs nonstop, even though cooling cycles)
   - Poor thermostat location, such as in sun or near a heat source
   - Failing thermostat or dead batteries
4. **Stuck or welded contactor in the outdoor unit.** The compressor and outdoor fan keep running even with no call for cooling. This is the classic cause of "it won't shut off."
5. **Low refrigerant charge, almost always from a leak.** Reduced capacity causes long cycles and poor cooling. The suction line may ice up.
6. **Shorted thermostat wire.** A damaged wire between the thermostat, the air handler and the outdoor unit keeps the cooling call energized.
7. **Duct leaks or disconnected ducts.** Ducts in hot attics, if leaking or disconnected, waste cooled air.
8. **Undersized system or high heat load.** This applies after additions, new windows or poor insulation. It is a design issue, not a failure.
9. **Weak compressor or failing components on inverter models.** The system loses capacity. On models with onboard diagnostics, this usually shows up in the fault history.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Check the thermostat.**
  - Confirm it is set to COOL, with fan on AUTO (not ON).
  - Make sure the setpoint is realistic. In extreme heat, most systems hold about 18–20°F below outdoor temperature.
  - Replace the batteries if the thermostat uses them.
  - Make sure it isn't in direct sun or next to a lamp or TV.
- **Test whether it shuts off.** Set the thermostat to OFF and wait 5 minutes.
  - If the outdoor unit keeps running, turn the AC off at the breaker or the outdoor disconnect and leave it off. This needs a technician.
- **Replace or inspect the air filter.** If it's gray or clogged, replace it with the correct size.
- **Open supply registers and clear return grilles.** Make sure furniture and rugs aren't blocking them.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from outside the cabinet.**
  - Clear leaves, weeds and debris, leaving about 2 feet of clearance.
  - Gently hosing the fins from the outside, with power OFF at the disconnect, is generally acceptable.
  - Don't remove panels or straighten fins aggressively.
- **Check for ice.** If you see ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, turn cooling off and run the fan only until it melts. Then call a technician.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Make sure the drain line or pan isn't overflowing. On many units, a tripped float switch shuts cooling off rather than making it run longer.
- **Check the exterior panels.** Confirm the air-handler and outdoor-unit panels are seated properly.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A technician will generally work through these steps:

1. **Confirm the complaint.** They check whether the outdoor unit stops when the thermostat call ends, by verifying 24V at the contactor coil.
2. **Check for a stuck contactor or shorted wire.** If the unit runs with the call removed, they inspect for welded or pitted contactor points or a shorted Y wire.
3. **Measure temperature split.** They measure supply and return temperatures. A typical target is about 16–22°F, depending on humidity.
4. **Check airflow.** They inspect the filter, measure static pressure, verify blower speed settings and check the indoor coil for dirt.
5. **Check refrigerant charge.** They connect gauges and calculate superheat and subcooling according to the metering device. Amana units with a TXV are typically charged by subcooling.
6. **Leak search if the charge is low.** A reputable technician should find and repair the leak rather than just topping off. Methods include an electronic detector, bubbles or nitrogen pressure testing.
7. **Inspect the condenser coil and fan.** They check for coil cleanliness and confirm the fan is running at proper speed.
8. **Read fault history on models with onboard diagnostics.** Depending on the model and control board, they pull fault codes from the thermostat, the outdoor board or a service app.
9. **Assess ducts and load.** They inspect for duct leaks and may run a Manual J load calculation if the system seems undersized.

A trustworthy quote names the specific measurement that justified the repair. For example: "subcooling 3°F vs 10°F target," or "contactor points welded."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs long, house warm, filter gray | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Check static pressure and coil if the problem persists |
| Outdoor unit runs with thermostat OFF | Stuck/welded contactor or shorted Y wire | Shut off breaker/disconnect | Replace contactor; trace and repair wiring |
| Blower runs nonstop, compressor cycles | Fan set to ON | Set fan to AUTO | Check fan relay/board if AUTO doesn't fix it |
| Long run, weak cooling, ice on lines | Low refrigerant or airflow problem | Turn off cooling, run fan to thaw | Leak search, repair, recharge; airflow check |
| Long run, outdoor coil matted with debris | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris, gently rinse from outside with power off | Chemical coil cleaning |
| Runs nonstop only on hottest days | Normal design limit or undersizing | Raise setpoint slightly, close blinds | Load calculation if it happens in mild weather |
| Some rooms hot, attic ducts | Duct leaks/disconnects | Open registers | Duct sealing or repair |
| Inverter unit runs constantly with poor cooling | Component fault or low charge | Check thermostat for messages | Read fault history, diagnose per Amana service data |
| Thermostat blank or erratic | Dead batteries or failed thermostat | Replace batteries or thermostat | Check wiring and transformer |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices, including labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter replacement | $10–$40 DIY |
| Service call / diagnostic | $80–$200 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Thermostat wire repair | $100–$300 |
| Thermostat replacement | $150–$450 installed (communicating thermostats cost more) |
| Refrigerant leak search | $200–$500 |
| Recharge, R-410A | $200–$600+ |
| Recharge, newer R-454B systems | Often costs more |
| Leak repair (brazed joint or valve core) | $200–$800 |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$2,800 |
| Duct sealing | $300–$2,000, depending on access and extent |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,000+ |

A full refrigerant leak job (leak search, repair and recharge) typically adds up to about $600–$1,900 or more. Major component replacements such as an evaporator coil or compressor can reach $3,000 or more.

Amana's warranty coverage varies by model. Select premium models, such as the ASXC and AVXC, carry a lifetime unit replacement limited warranty for compressor failure. Other models carry a 10-year unit replacement or 10-year parts limited warranty, subject to registration. Check your specific model's coverage with Amana's warranty lookup. Labor is usually not covered.

## What this code means

"Compressor not running" is a symptom, not a numbered fault code. The compressor is the large black dome inside your Carrier outdoor unit, and it is what actually produces cold air. When it isn't running, the indoor blower still pushes air but the air isn't cooled. The outdoor fan may or may not be spinning.

This page covers the compressor itself and the parts that start it. Related problems have their own pages:

- If the outdoor fan is also dead, see "Carrier AC Outdoor Fan Not Spinning."
- If the breaker trips, see "Carrier AC Breaker Keeps Tripping."
- For general warm-air complaints, see "Fan runs but no cold air."

How the system reports a problem depends on the model:

- **Single- and two-stage Carrier ACs** have no diagnostic display on most models. The thermostat simply calls for cooling, and the compressor never engages.
- **Units with a control board, including Infinity and Evolution communicating systems,** may flash a status LED on the outdoor board or show a fault on the thermostat. Examples include high or low pressure lockout and compressor or inverter faults.

Lockout behavior also varies:

- Many boards retry automatically after a time delay.
- After repeated pressure-switch trips within one call for cooling, some boards hold a lockout until power is cycled.
- Internal compressor overloads reset on their own once the compressor cools. That can take 30 minutes to several hours.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** This is the most common cause by far, especially after hot spells. The compressor hums or does nothing while the fan may still run.
2. **Bad or pitted contactor.** The relay that sends 240V to the compressor sticks or burns its contacts. If it doesn't pull in, neither the fan nor the compressor runs.
3. **Tripped internal thermal overload.** The compressor overheated, often from a dirty condenser coil, low charge or a weak capacitor, and shut itself off. It resets when it cools.
4. **Open high- or low-pressure switch.** Low refrigerant from a leak, a blocked condenser, or a failed outdoor fan trips the switch. The board then holds the compressor off or locks it out.
5. **No 24V call reaching the outdoor unit.** Possible reasons include thermostat settings, a damaged low-voltage wire, a blown control fuse on the indoor board, or a float or condensate safety switch that has opened because the drain is clogged.
6. **Electrical or wiring faults.** A burned terminal, a loose connection, or a failed hard-start component can stop the compressor.
7. **Inverter or drive fault (variable-speed Infinity models).** The drive board or its sensors fail or detect a fault. The thermostat or board LED usually reports a specific code.
8. **Failed compressor.** Seized bearings, a locked rotor, or shorted or open windings. This is least common, but it's the most expensive cause.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL, with the setpoint at least 3°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is weak or blank. Then wait about 5 minutes, because the anti-short-cycle delay is normal.
- **Breakers and disconnect:** Check the AC breaker in your main panel and the outdoor disconnect switch beside the unit. If a breaker has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Air filter:** Replace a dirty filter. Heavy restriction can contribute to freezing and pressure trips.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate line:** Many systems have a float switch that shuts down cooling when the drain backs up. If the drain pan has standing water, or there is a visible clog at the drain outlet, clear the outlet or call for service.
- **Outdoor unit surroundings:** Clear leaves, grass and debris from around the outside of the unit. Keep about 2 feet of clearance. Do not open the unit.
- **One reset:** If your system has locked out, turn the thermostat off and switch off the breaker for 5 minutes, then restore power. Do this only once. If the compressor still doesn't run, or it starts and then stops again, call a technician.

If the outdoor unit is humming loudly without running, smells burnt, or feels very hot, turn the system off at the thermostat and leave it off until it has been serviced.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will typically work through these steps:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling.** They measure 24V at the outdoor unit's contactor coil, then trace back to the thermostat, the indoor board fuse, and any float or pressure switches if voltage is missing.
2. **Check the contactor.** They verify it pulls in and measure line voltage in and out to look for pitted or burned contacts.
3. **Test the capacitor.** They measure microfarads against the rated value on the label. Anything outside about ±6% (or the stated tolerance) gets replaced.
4. **Read the board LED or thermostat fault history** on models that have them. This identifies pressure-switch or inverter faults.
5. **Measure compressor windings** across C, S and R with the power off. This checks for continuity, an open overload, or a short to ground.
6. **Measure amp draw** on startup to check for locked-rotor amps, which indicate a seized compressor.
7. **Connect gauges** if the pressure switch or overheating points to refrigerant. They check charge, superheat and subcooling, then leak-search if the system is low.
8. **Inspect the condenser coil and outdoor fan.** Airflow problems can cause compressor overheating.

If a quote jumps straight to "replace the compressor" without capacitor, winding and amp-draw readings, ask for those readings. Also get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan runs, compressor silent, warm air | Failed run capacitor or tripped overload | Turn off for 30-60 minutes, then try again once | Test and replace capacitor; check for overheating cause |
| Unit hums, nothing spins | Weak capacitor or locked compressor | Turn off at thermostat immediately | Test capacitor, add hard-start kit, check windings and amps |
| Outdoor unit completely silent | No 24V call, open contactor, or tripped breaker | Check thermostat, batteries, breaker and disconnect | Trace 24V, check indoor board fuse, replace contactor |
| Compressor starts then stops after minutes | Pressure switch or overload trip | Replace filter, clear debris around unit | Check refrigerant charge, clean coil, test fan and switches |
| No cooling and water in drain pan | Float or condensate safety switch open | Clear the condensate line outlet | Clear drain fully, verify switch operation |
| Thermostat or board shows a fault code (Infinity/Evolution) | Pressure lockout or inverter fault | Note the code; do one power reset | Diagnose drive board, sensors, compressor |
| Breaker trips when compressor tries to start | Shorted compressor or wiring fault | Do not reset again | Test windings to ground, inspect wiring |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges, including parts and labor. Costs vary by region and by whether the visit is an emergency call.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $90 – $250 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150 – $450 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Hard-start kit | $200 – $450 |
| Pressure switch replacement (refrigerant work required) | $250 – $600 |
| Indoor control board fuse / wiring repair | $100 – $300 |
| Outdoor control board (two-stage or communicating) | $400 – $1,000 |
| Inverter / drive board (Infinity variable-speed) | $800 – $2,000+ |
| Leak repair and recharge | $300 – $1,500+ |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $3,500+ |
| Full condenser replacement | $3,500 – $7,500+ |

Carrier compressors often carry a 10-year parts warranty on registered units, but labor and refrigerant are usually extra. On older systems that use R-22, or on units more than 12-15 years old, replacing the whole condenser is often the better value than replacing the compressor.

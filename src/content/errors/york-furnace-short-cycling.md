## What this code means

Short cycling isn't a single York error code — it's a *behavior*. Your furnace lights, runs briefly, then shuts off well before the thermostat is satisfied, and repeats this cycle over and over. Depending on what's triggering it, the control board may flash a fault code, or show nothing at all if the thermostat is the culprit.

Whatever the trigger, the common thread is that the burner keeps getting interrupted by a safety or signal problem. The most frequent cause is the high-limit switch opening because the furnace is overheating — usually from restricted airflow. Other causes include a weak flame-sensor signal that drops out mid-cycle, an oversized furnace, or a thermostat issue.

**About flash codes:** York residential furnaces (and their Coleman and Luxaire equivalents) have used several different integrated furnace controls across model lines and production years, and the flash-code numbering is *not* consistent between them. A count that means a pressure-switch fault on one board can mean something entirely different on another. Do not rely on a generic list you find online — including this one. Count the flashes, then read the legend printed on the furnace door or the inside of the blower-door panel of your own unit. On many York controls 4 flashes indicates an open limit or rollout circuit, which is the code most often associated with overheating short cycles, but even that should be confirmed against your unit's own chart.

Whether the furnace recovers on its own depends on the trigger. A high-limit trip typically auto-resets once the heat exchanger cools, letting the cycle repeat. Repeated trips will eventually drive many controls into a lockout that holds until power is cycled — but the lockout is a symptom, not the problem.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — the #1 cause. Restricted airflow overheats the heat exchanger and trips the high-limit, shutting the burner down early.
2. **Blocked or closed supply/return vents** — too many closed registers or blocked returns starve airflow, same overheating effect as a dirty filter.
3. **Dirty or failing flame sensor** — a weak flame signal causes the board to drop the burner seconds after ignition, then retry, producing rapid cycling.
4. **Failing high-limit switch** — a limit that opens prematurely (or has drifted) trips before the furnace is truly overheated.
5. **Thermostat problems** — poor location (near a supply vent), dead batteries, loose wiring, or a cycle setting that's too aggressive. On an older mechanical thermostat that's the heat anticipator; on a digital or smart thermostat it's the cycles-per-hour or cycle-rate setting in the installer menu. Check which type you have before assuming either applies.
6. **Blower motor or capacitor fault** — a blower that won't move enough air (slow or stalled) lets heat build up and trips the limit.
7. **Oversized furnace** — satisfies small heat demands quickly and shuts off, a design issue rather than a repair.
8. **Heat exchanger damage** — not a typical cause of classic short cycling. A crack or heavy soot more often shows up as flame disturbance, a rollout trip, or a carbon monoxide concern. Treat it as something a technician may *find* while inspecting a furnace that cycles, not as a likely explanation on its own — and if it is found, it matters enormously for safety.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps you should attempt yourself:

- **Replace the air filter.** This is the single most common fix. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, swap it for the correct size. Try running the furnace with a brand-new filter.
- **Open supply and return vents.** Walk the house and make sure registers are open and unobstructed by furniture or rugs. Keep most of them open — closing too many starves airflow.
- **Check the thermostat.** Replace the batteries if it uses them. Make sure it's set to Heat and the temperature is above room temp. If it sits in direct sun or near a vent, that can cause false short cycles. If it's a digital thermostat with a cycles-per-hour setting, note what it's set to so you can tell the technician; if it's an older mechanical thermostat with a heat anticipator, leave the adjustment alone and let a pro set it.
- **Check the breaker and furnace switch.** Confirm the furnace breaker is on and the service switch (looks like a light switch near the unit) is in the On position.
- **Clear the condensate line (high-efficiency models).** A clogged condensate drain or full trap can trip a safety and interrupt cycles. If you can see standing water or a blocked drain line, clear the visible blockage.
- **One reset only.** If the board is locked out, cycle power once at the furnace switch. That clears the lockout and the flashing code, but it fixes nothing — if the underlying fault is still present, the furnace will trip again. If it locks out a second time, stop and call a pro; repeated resets can damage components.

Do **not** open the furnace cabinet to clean the flame sensor, test switches, or touch wiring — that's technician work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will work in roughly this order, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the symptom and read the flash code** on the control board, interpreting it against the legend for that specific control — not a generic chart.
2. **Measure temperature rise** across the furnace and compare it to the rating-plate spec. A high rise points straight to an airflow problem.
3. **Check airflow components** — filter, blower wheel for dirt buildup, blower motor speed, and the run capacitor.
4. **Test the flame sensor** with a microamp meter; a reading well below spec means it needs cleaning or replacement.
5. **Test the high-limit switch** for continuity and verify it opens/closes at the right temperature.
6. **Inspect the heat exchanger** for cracks or soot.
7. **Evaluate thermostat setup** — location, cycle rate or anticipator, and wiring.
8. **Assess sizing** if everything else checks out and the unit simply cycles on small loads.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs 2-5 min then shuts off, restarts | Overheating from clogged filter | Replace filter, open vents | Measure temp rise, check blower/limit |
| Burner lights, drops out after a few seconds, retries | Dirty/failing flame sensor | One reset only | Clean or replace flame sensor, test microamps |
| Cycles, then locks out with a code flashing | Depends on the code — look it up on your unit's own legend; an overheating/limit trip is the most common | New filter, open registers; note the flash count | Interpret the code for that control, test limit, inspect blower & exchanger |
| Cycles with weak or no airflow from vents | Blower motor or capacitor fault | None | Test/replace capacitor or blower motor |
| Rapid on/off, no fault code | Thermostat location, batteries, or cycle rate | Replace batteries, check placement | Adjust cycle rate/anticipator, repair wiring, relocate stat |
| Short cycles only on mild days | Oversized furnace | None | Evaluate sizing & staging options |
| Cycling with water near the unit | Clogged condensate or a venting/pressure fault | Clear visible drain blockage | Clear trap, test pressure switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges for 2026, parts and labor installed unless noted:

- **Air filter:** $8–$40 DIY. The cheapest and most common fix.
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 DIY.
- **Flame sensor cleaning:** $100–$225. Replacement sensor: $150–$350 installed.
- **High-limit switch:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Blower run capacitor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Blower motor:** $450–$1,800 installed. PSC motors sit at the low end; ECM motors and ECM control modules routinely run over $900.
- **Control board:** $400–$1,200 installed, depending on the control used in your model.
- **Heat exchanger:** $1,000–$2,500+ (often pushes toward furnace replacement; check warranty).
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 installed, or $25–$250 DIY for the thermostat itself.
- **Service/diagnostic call:** $90–$200, often credited toward the repair.

Prices vary widely by region, by how accessible your furnace is, and by after-hours rates. Get a written quote before authorizing a major part.

If a cracked heat exchanger is found, the furnace should be shut down until repaired or replaced — it's a carbon monoxide risk.

## Related codes

If your board is flashing a specific code during the short cycle, count the flashes and check the legend on your furnace door first, since the numbering differs between the controls York has used. Then read the matching page:

- **York Furnace 4 Flashes: Open Limit Switch Causes & Fixes** — on many York controls this is the open limit/rollout code, and it's the one most often behind overheating short cycles.
- **York Furnace 3 Flashes: Pressure Switch Open Causes & Fixes** — an airflow or venting fault that interrupts cycles on the controls that use this numbering.
- **York Furnace Won't Ignite: Causes, Diagnosis & Fixes** — if cycles stop producing any flame at all, or the control locks out after repeated failed ignition attempts.

If your flash count doesn't match any of the above, that's expected — it most likely means your furnace uses a different control board. Go by the legend on the unit and give the technician both the flash count and the furnace model number.

## What this code means

This isn't a numbered fault code. It's a symptom: the compressor section is getting a call to cool, but the condenser fan on top of the outdoor unit isn't turning.

You may notice any of these:
- A humming or buzzing from the outdoor unit
- Warm air from the vents
- An outdoor unit that cycles off on its own after a few minutes, when the compressor overheats and trips its internal overload

On standard single-stage and two-stage Amana condensers, the fan is a PSC motor powered through the contactor. It needs a run capacitor to start, usually a dual capacitor shared with the compressor. On variable-speed and communicating models, the fan may be an ECM motor controlled by the outdoor board, and some of these log a fault in the control's history. Because behavior and codes vary by model, consult your unit's literature rather than assuming one code applies.

There is no fan-specific lockout to reset on conventional models. The compressor's internal overload resets on its own once it cools, which is why the unit may seem to "try again." Repeated overheating cycles damage the compressor. **Shut the system off at the thermostat until it's repaired.**

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** This is the most common cause on Amana condensers. If the fan side of the dual capacitor fails, the motor hums but won't start. A bulging or leaking top is a sign, but many failed capacitors look normal.
2. **Failed condenser fan motor.** Common causes are worn bearings (the blade is stiff or seized), an overheated winding (the motor's internal overload is open), or, on ECM models, a failed motor module.
3. **Contactor or wiring fault.** Pitted contactor points, a burned or loose fan lead, or rodent damage can leave the compressor running while the fan gets no power, or vice versa.
4. **Obstruction or physical damage.** Debris, ice, a bent fan guard, or a loose blade on the motor shaft can stop the fan from turning.
5. **Outdoor control board or communication fault (variable-speed/communicating models only).** On these models the board, not the contactor, commands the fan. A board or signal fault can stop it. This does not apply to basic single-stage units.

If the whole outdoor unit is silent (no hum and no compressor), the problem is more likely power or control signal. See the Not Cooling page instead.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:**
  - Confirm it's set to COOL, with the setpoint several degrees below room temperature.
  - Replace the batteries if it has them.
- **Breakers:** Check the indoor breaker panel for tripped AC and furnace breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the disconnect box near the condenser is fully closed and switched on.
- **Look, don't touch:** From outside the unit, check whether sticks, leaves, or a toy are visibly lodged in the fan guard. Don't reach through the guard. Turn the system off at the thermostat and the disconnect before clearing anything loose from the top grille.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter won't stop the outdoor fan, but it adds strain and can freeze the indoor coil.
- **Condensate:** Check that the indoor drain line isn't backed up and that no float switch is tripped. These shut down the whole system, not just the fan.
- **Panels:** Make sure the outdoor unit's service panel is seated and closed.

If the fan still doesn't spin, **turn the AC off at the thermostat** and call a technician. Don't push-start the blade, and don't open the electrical panel. It contains high voltage, and capacitors stay charged even after the power is off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will typically work through these steps:
1. Kill power at the disconnect and **safely discharge the capacitor**.
2. Spin the blade by hand to check for stiff or seized bearings.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter on its microfarad setting. A reading more than about 6% (some use 10%) below its rating means it's failed.
4. Check the voltage at the contactor and at the fan motor leads while the system is calling for cooling. Inspect the contactor points.
5. Check the fan motor windings for open or shorted circuits. Note whether the motor is hot (its internal overload may be open).
6. On ECM/communicating models, read the outdoor control's fault history and check the board's fan output and communication wiring.
7. After the repair, confirm the fan amperage and refrigerant operating pressures, so that a compressor that overheated isn't left with hidden damage.

Red flags in a quote:
- Recommending a new compressor or a whole new system without testing the capacitor and fan motor first.
- Charging refrigerant for a fan problem.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor unit hums, fan blade still | Failed fan side of the run capacitor | Turn system off at thermostat; call a pro | Test and replace the capacitor |
| Fan blade stiff or won't turn freely; motor hot | Seized bearings or failed fan motor | Turn system off; don't force the blade | Replace the fan motor (and capacitor if it's weak) |
| Compressor runs, fan dead, no hum from motor | Open motor winding, burned lead, or contactor fault | Check breaker and disconnect once | Test voltage, repair wiring, replace contactor or motor |
| Fan starts then stops after a few minutes | Motor overheating on its internal overload, or weak capacitor | Turn system off | Test capacitor and motor amp draw; replace as needed |
| Visible debris or a bent guard touching the blade | Obstruction | With power off at the disconnect, remove loose debris from outside the grille | Straighten or replace the guard, re-secure the blade |
| Variable-speed model, fan off, fault shown on thermostat | ECM motor or outdoor board fault | Note the fault and power-cycle once at the breaker | Read fault history; diagnose the ECM motor or control board |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $75 – $200 |
| Run capacitor (single or dual) | $120 – $350 |
| Contactor | $150 – $350 |
| Wiring repair | $100 – $300 |
| PSC condenser fan motor | $350 – $700 (parts may be partly covered by warranty) |
| ECM condenser fan motor (variable-speed models) | $600 – $1,200+ |
| Outdoor control board (communicating models) | $400 – $900+ |

Running the AC with a dead fan can ruin the compressor, a repair that often costs $1,800 – $3,500 or more. Shutting the unit off promptly is the cheapest fix you can make.

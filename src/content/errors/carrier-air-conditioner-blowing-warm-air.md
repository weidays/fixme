## What this code means

"Blowing warm air" is a symptom, not a numbered Carrier fault code. When your Carrier AC blows warm air, the indoor blower is running and air is coming out of the vents. That air feels room temperature or warm instead of cool.

This page covers one specific case: air that is clearly warm, usually because the outdoor unit is not doing its job. That is different from two related problems:

- Air that is only slightly cool. See Carrier AC Not Cooling.
- Air that is moving with no cooling, which is usually confirmed at the outdoor unit. See Carrier AC Fan Runs But No Cold Air.

When the air feels warm, the most common causes are:

- **A thermostat or mode mix-up.** This includes a heat pump or dual-fuel system calling for heat by mistake.
- **No power at the outdoor unit.**
- **A failed start component.**
- **A significant refrigerant loss.**

On a furnace-paired system, the air can also feel warm for another reason: the blower moves air across a warm furnace heat exchanger right after a heating cycle.

How the system recovers depends on the equipment:

- **Standard (non-communicating) Carrier condensers** have no fault display. Some boards have a time-delay feature that holds the compressor off for about 5 minutes after power interruptions or short cycles.
- **Infinity/Evolution communicating systems** may log a fault on the wall control or show it on the outdoor control board LED. Whether that fault clears on its own or holds until power is cycled depends on the specific fault and model.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat set wrong.** The thermostat is in HEAT, AUTO with a low heat setpoint, or FAN ON with the system OFF. The setpoint may also be above room temperature. Dead batteries can blank the display so it stops calling for cooling.
2. **Outdoor unit has no power.** The dedicated outdoor breaker has tripped, or the disconnect switch near the condenser is off or its fuses have blown. The indoor blower is on a separate circuit, so it keeps running and blows uncooled air.
3. **Failed run capacitor.** This is the most common component failure on Carrier condensers. The compressor and/or outdoor fan hum but won't start, or the compressor trips on its internal overload.
4. **Failed or pitted contactor.** The contactor is the relay that switches the outdoor unit on. When it fails, the outdoor unit never starts even though the thermostat is calling for cooling.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** Leaks in the coil or line set (including formicary corrosion on evaporator coils) leave too little refrigerant to absorb heat. The air gets gradually warmer, and the coil may freeze first.
6. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil or failed outdoor fan motor.** Heat can't be rejected, so pressures climb. The high-pressure switch (where equipped) or the compressor overload then shuts the compressor off while the indoor blower keeps running.
7. **Thermostat wiring or low-voltage problem.** A broken Y wire, a blown 3-amp/5-amp fuse on the furnace or fan coil board, or a tripped float/condensate safety switch kills the cooling call. Some float switches cut both the outdoor unit and the blower; others cut only the outdoor unit.
8. **Heat pump stuck in heat, or reversing valve problem.** This applies only if your "AC" is actually a Carrier heat pump. See the heat pump pages.
9. **Compressor failure.** This includes internal overload, winding, or mechanical failure. It is the least common cause and the most expensive one.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Check the thermostat.** Set it to COOL, set the temperature at least 3–5°F below room temperature, and set the fan to AUTO. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank. Wait 5–10 minutes, because a built-in compressor delay is normal.
2. **Check the outdoor unit.** Go outside while cooling is called and see whether the outdoor fan is spinning and the unit is humming. If it is silent, power is the likely issue. If it hums loudly but nothing spins, turn cooling off and call a pro. Do not poke the fan blades.
3. **Check the breaker, once.** Find the AC/condenser breaker in your main panel. If it has tripped, reset it one time. If it trips again, stop and see Carrier AC Breaker Keeps Tripping.
4. **Check the disconnect.** The disconnect is the box on the wall near the condenser. Confirm the handle or pull-out block is fully seated in the ON position. Do not open it further or touch the fuses inside.
5. **Replace or check the air filter.** A clogged filter can freeze the coil, and you get warm or weak air once the coil ices over.
6. **Open vents and registers.** Make sure supply vents and return grilles are open and unblocked.
7. **Check the condensate line and drain pan, where visible.** A full pan or a clogged line can trip the float switch and stop cooling. Clear the outside end of the drain line or the visible PVC cleanout with a wet/dry vac, then wait for the system to restart.
8. **Seat the panels.** Make sure the furnace or air handler access door is firmly closed so its door switch is engaged.
9. **Clear around the condenser.** Remove leaves, grass, and debris from around the outside of the unit, keeping about 2 feet of space. A gentle garden-hose rinse from outside, with the power off, is fine. Do not remove the top or the panels.

If none of these steps restores cool air within about 15 minutes, set the thermostat to OFF and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should follow roughly this order:

1. **Verify the call for cooling.** They check for 24 VAC on the Y and C terminals at the condenser and confirm the thermostat setup. On a communicating system, they read the fault history on the wall control or outdoor board.
2. **Check line voltage** at the disconnect and contactor, including the fuses or breaker inside the disconnect.
3. **Test the contactor.** They check coil voltage and look for pitted or welded contacts.
4. **Test the capacitor** with a meter, measuring microfarads against the label rating. A weak capacitor (for example, 5% or more below the rating) is replaced.
5. **Check the compressor and fan amperage.** They also check the compressor windings and whether the internal overload has tripped. A hot overload can take 30–60 minutes or more to reset.
6. **Check refrigerant charge** with gauges or probes. They take superheat and subcooling readings based on the metering device and compare them to Carrier's charging chart. If the charge is low, they should recommend leak detection, not just a "top-off."
7. **Inspect the condenser coil, fan motor, and pressure switches**, plus the indoor coil for icing and the blower settings.

A quote that skips straight to "you need refrigerant" or "you need a new compressor" without meter readings deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air is warm, thermostat shows HEAT or is blank | Wrong mode or dead batteries | Set to COOL, lower the setpoint, replace batteries | None, unless the thermostat itself has failed |
| Outdoor unit completely silent | Tripped breaker, disconnect off, or no 24V signal | Reset the breaker once, check the disconnect handle | Check disconnect fuses, low-voltage fuse, and wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan/compressor won't start | Failed capacitor | Turn cooling off, don't keep restarting | Test and replace the capacitor |
| Outdoor unit silent, power confirmed | Failed contactor or broken Y wire | Turn cooling off and call | Test and replace the contactor or repair wiring |
| Air gradually got warmer over days or weeks, coil iced | Low refrigerant (leak) | Change the filter, run fan-only to thaw | Leak search, repair, evacuate, recharge |
| Fan runs, compressor cycles off when hot outside | Dirty condenser coil or failed fan motor | Clear debris, gently hose off the coil | Clean the coil, replace the fan motor or capacitor |
| Cooling stops, water in pan or near furnace | Float switch tripped by a clogged drain | Clear the condensate line | Clear the trap, check the float switch |
| Outdoor unit runs, still warm, loud or tripping | Compressor failure | Turn cooling off | Diagnose the compressor, quote replacement |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges for parts plus labor, as of 2026. Expect prices at the high end for after-hours or emergency calls.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $80–$200 |
| Thermostat batteries / filter (DIY) | $5–$40 |
| New thermostat (DIY standard) | $30–$250 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Low-voltage fuse / wiring repair | $100–$300 |
| Condensate line clearing / float switch | $100–$300 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $300–$800 |
| Leak search plus repair plus recharge (R-410A or R-454B) | $400–$1,500+ |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$3,000 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800–$4,000+ |

If the system is older than 12–15 years and needs a compressor or coil, get a replacement quote too. Check Carrier's registered-warranty status first, because many units carry 10-year parts coverage when registered.

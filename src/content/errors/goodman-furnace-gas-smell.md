## What this code means

**If you smell gas right now, do this first, before you read anything else:**

1. **Do not touch electrical switches, the thermostat, appliances, phones, or anything that could spark** inside the home. Don't light anything.
2. **Leave immediately** with everyone, including pets. Open doors on your way out only if doing so doesn't slow you down.
3. **Call your gas utility's emergency number or 911 from outside**, well away from the building.
4. **Do not go back in** until the utility or fire department says it is safe.
5. Before anyone restores gas service or relights the furnace, have a **licensed HVAC technician** find and repair the source.

"Gas smell" is not a flash code. Goodman furnaces have no sensor that detects leaking fuel. Natural gas and propane are odorless, so suppliers add mercaptan, which smells like rotten eggs or sulfur. That odor is your only built-in warning. When you smell it near the furnace, unburned gas is escaping somewhere: at a pipe fitting, at the gas valve, at the burners, or from a nearby gas line that happens to run past the furnace.

The fault does not reset itself, and power-cycling the furnace does nothing for it. A leak stays dangerous until it is physically repaired. Propane is heavier than air and collects low, in basements and crawlspaces. Natural gas rises. Either one can ignite.

If what you smell is burning dust, scorched wiring, or something "hot," see the burning smell and rollout pages listed under Related codes instead. If you are unsure which it is, treat it as gas.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

Field experience suggests this order. Goodman does not publish a ranked list for gas odor.

1. **Leaking pipe joint or flare fitting near the furnace.** Threaded black-iron joints, the union, or the flexible appliance connector can loosen or corrode, especially after other work has been done nearby.
2. **Gas valve leak.** The valve body or its inlet/outlet connections on the furnace's gas valve (White-Rodgers or Honeywell, depending on the model) can leak externally. A valve that fails to close fully can also let gas pass to the burners when it shouldn't.
3. **Delayed or failed ignition releasing unburned gas.** Gas flows during trial-for-ignition but doesn't light right away. Dirty burners, a weak hot-surface igniter, or a misaligned burner can cause this. You may notice a brief odor, sometimes with a startup bang. These cases usually also produce ignition codes (1 flash or lockout).
4. **Manual shutoff or sediment trap (drip leg) leaking.** Packing or threads at the shutoff cock or drip-leg cap.
5. **A source that isn't the furnace.** A water heater, a range, a dryer, a nearby gas line, or the meter can all leak, and the smell may simply be noticed near the furnace. Sewer gas and a dry floor drain can mimic the odor too.
6. **Propane-specific issues.** These include low tank level (odor fade or air in the line after a refill), regulator venting, or a furnace that is incorrectly converted (LP kit not installed or set wrong).

A cracked heat exchanger or blocked flue mainly produces **carbon monoxide**, which is odorless. It is not a gas smell. If your CO alarm is going off, follow the same evacuate-and-call steps.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

When you smell gas, the only safe action is to **get out and call the utility or 911**. Do not troubleshoot with gas odor present.

Once the utility has cleared the home (or after the repair), these steps are reasonable:

- **Leave the furnace off** at the thermostat until a technician has repaired it and signed off. Don't attempt even one reset or relight on your own.
- **Install a combustible gas detector and a CO alarm** near the furnace and sleeping areas if you don't already have them. Replace batteries in existing alarms.
- **Check that visible vent terminations are clear** outside (intake and exhaust pipes on high-efficiency models, or the flue cap), so the technician has an accurate picture.
- **Make sure the furnace door or panel is seated** after any service. The blower door switch keeps the furnace off if the door is left loose, so the furnace won't run without it.
- **Note when you smelled it.** Record whether it was only at startup, constantly, or near other appliances. Tell the technician. It speeds diagnosis.

Do not spray soapy water on fittings, sniff around the valve, or operate any gas shutoff yourself. Leave those to the utility or the technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A typical diagnosis goes like this:

- **Gas utility first response:** checks with a combustible gas indicator, may shut off and lock the meter or appliance, and tags the furnace if it finds a leak. Many utilities don't repair house piping. They hand off to a licensed contractor.
- **Leak check of the furnace gas train:** an electronic leak detector and/or leak-detection solution on every joint from the shutoff to the valve outlet, including the union, drip leg, and flex connector.
- **Gas valve test:** a check for external leaks and for whether the valve closes fully (no gas to the manifold with the call for heat off). A leaking valve is replaced, not repaired.
- **Pressure test of the piping**, if the leak isn't obvious. They isolate and test the house line with a gauge to find a pressure drop.
- **Ignition sequence observation:** they watch trial-for-ignition for delayed lighting, then check the igniter, burner alignment, inlet and manifold pressure against the rating plate, and the flame sensor. They review flash-code history.
- **Combustion and CO test** before returning the system to service, plus a check of the venting.
- **Propane systems:** a check of the regulator and tank, and confirmation that the furnace has the correct LP conversion kit and orifices.

Red flags in a quote: a technician who wants to replace the whole furnace without first showing you where the leak is, or who relights the unit without leak-testing.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong rotten-egg smell near furnace, constant | Pipe joint, flex connector, or valve leak | Leave now. Call utility/911 from outside | Leak test, tighten or replace fitting or connector, replace leaking valve |
| Smell only for a moment at startup, maybe a bang | Delayed ignition | Turn furnace off at thermostat. Call a technician (call utility if the odor lingers) | Clean or align burners, test igniter, check gas pressure |
| Smell near furnace and water heater area | Shared line or other appliance leaking | Leave. Call utility | Pressure test piping, locate and repair leak |
| Smell plus furnace locked out (1 flash) | Gas flowing but not lighting | Leave if odor present. No resets | Diagnose ignition, check valve and gas supply |
| Gas smell after propane refill or tank ran low | Air in line or odor fade, regulator issue | Leave. Call propane supplier's emergency line | Purge and leak-check, test regulator |
| CO alarm sounding, no odor | Combustion or venting problem (not a gas leak) | Leave. Call 911/utility | Combustion analysis, inspect heat exchanger and vent |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges and vary by region and access.

- **Gas utility odor investigation:** usually free
- **Diagnostic or emergency service call:** $100–$300 (after-hours often $150–$400)
- **Tighten or reseal a leaking fitting or union:** $100–$250
- **Replace flexible appliance connector:** $150–$300
- **Replace manual shutoff valve or drip leg:** $150–$350
- **Replace furnace gas valve:** $250–$650 (part $100–$250)
- **Burner cleaning or alignment for delayed ignition:** $100–$250. Igniter replacement adds $150–$300
- **House gas line leak repair or pressure test:** $150–$800+. Repiping can run $1,000+
- **Propane regulator replacement:** $150–$400
- **Cracked heat exchanger (CO issue):** $1,000–$2,500, often a reason to replace the furnace ($3,500–$7,500 installed)

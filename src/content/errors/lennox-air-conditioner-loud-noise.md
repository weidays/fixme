## What this code means

"Loud noise" is not a fault code that a Lennox air conditioner displays. It is a symptom. On iComfort-enabled systems, a noise problem may appear alongside a stored alarm, such as a high-pressure or compressor fault, so check the thermostat's alerts screen if you have one.

The type of sound matters more than the volume. Each sound points to a different part of the system:

- **Rattling:** usually something loose.
- **Screeching or squealing:** usually a motor bearing.
- **Buzzing:** usually electrical.
- **Banging or clanking:** often internal compressor trouble or a broken fan blade.
- **Hissing:** may mean a refrigerant leak.

This page covers the outdoor condensing unit and the cooling cycle. Heat-pump-specific noises are covered elsewhere, such as the defrost "whoosh" and reversing-valve shifts.

There is nothing to "reset" for noise. If the system has also locked out on a fault, cycle power once at the thermostat or breaker. If the lockout or noise returns, call a technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Debris or loose exterior panels.** Sticks, leaves or toys in the condenser fan, or a loose access panel, louver or screw, cause rattling and ticking.
2. **Failing outdoor fan motor bearings.** These cause squealing, screeching or grinding that often gets worse as the motor heats up.
3. **Bent, loose or out-of-balance fan blade.** This causes rhythmic thumping, scraping or vibration.
4. **Weak or failed run capacitor.** This causes a loud buzz or hum while a motor struggles to start, and it often leads to overheating.
5. **Pitted or chattering contactor.** This causes buzzing or rapid clicking from the electrical compartment.
6. **Compressor problems.** These include loose or broken internal mounts, failing valves, or hard starting. Symptoms are banging, clanking, rattling at shutdown, or a loud growl. A compressor sound blanket may also have shifted.
7. **Refrigerant line vibration or leak.** Lines touching the cabinet or a wall cause rattling. A hissing or bubbling sound can indicate a leak or a restriction.
8. **Indoor airflow problems.** A clogged filter, closed registers or an undersized return cause whistling and roaring at vents. A failing blower motor or blower wheel causes indoor rumbling or squealing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Identify the sound and where it comes from.** Is it indoor or outdoor? Does it happen at startup, while running, or at shutdown? Note it, or record a short video for the technician.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm the mode and setpoint, and replace the batteries if it uses them. Look for alert messages on iComfort or other smart thermostats.
- **Replace the air filter** if it is dirty. This often cures indoor whistling.
- **Open all supply registers** and make sure return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or curtains.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from the outside.** Turn the system off at the thermostat first. Clear leaves, grass clippings and debris from around the unit, keeping about 2 feet of clearance. Do not reach through the fan grille.
- **Confirm the exterior access panels are seated and their screws are snug.** Do not open the electrical panel.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Make sure it is flowing and not gurgling or backing up.
- **If the AC breaker has tripped, reset it once.** If it trips again or the noise returns, leave the system off and call a pro.

Stop and call a technician right away for any of these:

- grinding or screeching
- metal-on-metal banging
- a loud buzz with no fan movement
- hissing

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician should do the following:

1. **Listen with the unit running** to pinpoint whether the sound comes from the fan, compressor, electrical section, lines or indoor blower.
2. **Disconnect power** and check the fan blade for damage, tightness on the shaft and clearance. They should also spin the motor by hand to feel for bearing drag or wobble.
3. **Test the run capacitor's microfarads** against its rating with a meter. They should also inspect the contactor for pitting and measure voltage at the contactor.
4. **Measure the amp draw** of the compressor and fan motor and compare it to the nameplate values.
5. **Connect gauges** to check refrigerant pressures, superheat and subcooling. They should leak-check if the charge is low or there was hissing.
6. **Inspect the compressor.** This includes the mounts, the sound blanket and the startup behavior. A hard-start kit may be suggested for older single-stage compressors.
7. **Check the refrigerant line set** for rubbing or loose straps.
8. **Indoors, check static pressure** and inspect the blower wheel and motor.

Be cautious about a quote for a new compressor or a whole new system that isn't backed by amp-draw readings and electrical test results.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rattling or ticking outdoors | Debris, loose panel or screws | Clear debris around the unit (power off) and seat exterior panels | Tighten internal hardware and remove debris from the fan area |
| Squealing or screeching outdoors | Fan motor bearings failing | Turn the system off | Replace the condenser fan motor (and usually the capacitor) |
| Rhythmic thump or scraping | Bent or loose fan blade | Turn the system off | Replace or re-secure the fan blade and check the motor shaft |
| Loud buzz, fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or seized motor | Turn the system off and do not keep resetting | Test and replace the capacitor or motor |
| Buzzing or chattering clicks | Contactor or low voltage | Check the breaker once | Replace the contactor and check the wiring and voltage |
| Banging or clanking from the cabinet | Compressor mounts or internal failure | Turn the system off | Run electrical and pressure tests, then repair or replace the compressor |
| Hissing or bubbling | Refrigerant leak or restriction | Turn the system off | Find and repair the leak, then recharge |
| Line rattling against the wall | Loose line set | None beyond noting the location | Re-strap the lines and add isolation |
| Whistling at vents or roaring indoors | Dirty filter or restricted airflow | Replace the filter and open registers | Measure static pressure and fix the ductwork or return |
| Indoor rumbling or squeal | Blower wheel or motor issue | Replace the filter | Clean or replace the blower wheel or motor |

## Repair costs

Typical US installed prices are below. They vary by region, warranty status and whether the visit is after hours.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $80–$200 |
| Tightening panels, clearing the fan, re-strapping lines | $100–$250 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120–$350 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Condenser fan blade | $150–$350 |
| Condenser fan motor (PSC) | $300–$650 |
| Condenser fan motor (ECM, high-efficiency models) | $500–$1,200 |
| Hard-start kit | $150–$400 |
| Refrigerant leak repair and recharge | $300–$1,500+ |
| Indoor blower motor | $400–$1,500 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,500+ |

Many Lennox compressors carry a 10-year limited parts warranty when the unit is registered. If your compressor is still under warranty, the cost is mostly labor and refrigerant.

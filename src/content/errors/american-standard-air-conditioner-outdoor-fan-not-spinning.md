## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a flashing code. On most American Standard conventional air conditioners, the thermostat is calling for cooling, but the fan on top of the condenser is not turning. Sometimes the compressor is still humming or running, and sometimes the whole outdoor unit is silent.

The outdoor fan pulls air through the condenser coil to get rid of the heat removed from your home. If it stops, pressure and temperature inside the system rise fast. This page is rated **pro** for two reasons:

- Nearly every real cause is inside the electrical compartment, which carries line voltage. Capacitors can hold a charge even after the power is off.
- Running the system without the fan can damage the compressor, which is the most expensive part in the unit.

How the fault behaves depends on the model:

- **Most single- and two-stage units:** The fan has no dedicated error code, and there is no lockout to reset. The compressor may cycle off on its internal overload and restart once it cools. Fan motor type varies by model, so some two-stage units may behave differently.
- **Variable-speed (AccuComfort) and communicating (AccuLink) models:** The outdoor control may log a fan-motor or high-pressure fault. That fault can appear on the communicating thermostat or on the outdoor unit's control/drive diagnostic display. Whether it clears on its own or holds until power is cycled varies by model, so check the unit's service facts sheet.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** This is by far the most common cause on PSC-motor condensers. Most units use a dual capacitor that serves both the fan and the compressor. A weak fan section can leave the blade still, or turning only after a push.
2. **Failed condenser fan motor.** The bearings can seize, or the windings can open after years of heat and weather. Overheating often shows up as an intermittent stop followed by a restart.
3. **Contactor problem.** Pitted or burned contacts, or a failed coil, can cut power to the fan. Depending on the wiring, the compressor may be affected too, or the whole unit may stay off.
4. **No power or no control signal to the condenser.** Possible causes include:
   - a tripped breaker
   - a pulled disconnect
   - a blown fuse in the disconnect
   - a broken low-voltage thermostat wire
   - a tripped float or condensate safety switch indoors, which breaks the cooling call
5. **Physical obstruction.** Debris, ice from earlier freezing, or a bent blade can rub against the shroud and jam the fan.
6. **Loose or burned wiring.** Rodent damage or corroded terminals in the control compartment can interrupt power to the fan.
7. **Variable-speed and ECM-fan models: ECM fan motor or outdoor control/drive fault.** The control may stop driving the motor, or the motor module may fail. The outdoor unit's control/drive diagnostic display guides this diagnosis.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it is set to Cool, with a setpoint a few degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Breakers:** Check the AC breaker at the main panel. If it tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Look at the disconnect box beside the condenser. Make sure the handle or pull-out block is fully seated in the On position. Do not open the box beyond the pull-out, and do not touch the fuses.
- **Air filter:** Replace a dirty filter at the furnace or air handler. A clogged filter doesn't stop the outdoor fan directly, but it stresses the system.
- **Condensate line and safety switch:** If the indoor drain pan is full, a float switch may be cutting the cooling call. Clear the visible drain line opening or pan if you can reach it safely. Leave the switch itself alone.
- **Look, don't touch:** From outside the unit, note whether the compressor hums, whether the blade is visibly blocked by debris, and whether you smell burning.
- **Shut it down:** If the fan still won't spin, set the thermostat to Off. This protects the compressor until a technician arrives.

Never push the blade with a stick, reach through the fan guard, or open the control panel.

## How a technician will diagnose it

1. **Verify the call and power.** The tech confirms 24 V at the contactor coil and line voltage at the disconnect and on the load side of the contactor.
2. **Make the system safe.** They kill power and safely discharge the capacitor.
3. **Test the capacitor.** They measure microfarads with a meter. A reading more than about 6–10% below the label rating, or a capacitor with a bulged top, means replacement.
4. **Inspect the contactor.** They look for pitted contacts and a failed coil, and check voltage drop across the contacts.
5. **Test the fan motor.** They check winding resistance, check for a short to ground, and spin the shaft by hand with power off to check the bearings. They also check whether the motor's internal overload has tripped from heat.
6. **Variable-speed units:** They read the fault codes on the outdoor unit's control/drive diagnostic display or the history on a communicating thermostat. Then they check the control's output and the ECM motor according to American Standard's service literature.
7. **Check for collateral damage.** They check compressor amp draw and pressures to make sure the compressor wasn't hurt while the fan was down.

A credible quote should name the specific failed part and the test reading that proves it, such as "fan section measured 3.1 µF on a 5 µF rating."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan still | Weak capacitor or seized motor | Turn thermostat Off | Test and replace capacitor or motor |
| Fan starts only after a push | Failing capacitor | Do not push; shut down | Replace dual run capacitor |
| Fan runs a while, then stops | Overheating motor or bad bearings | Turn system Off; note timing | Test motor and its overload, replace motor |
| Whole outdoor unit silent | Breaker, disconnect, contactor, or no call | Check thermostat, breaker (one reset), disconnect | Test 24 V and line voltage; replace contactor or repair wiring |
| Unit off and indoor drain pan full | Condensate safety switch tripped | Clear visible drain line or pan | Clear the line fully and verify the switch |
| Grinding or scraping noise | Bent blade or debris | Shut off; clear loose debris around the outside of the unit | Replace blade, realign motor |
| Fault shown on communicating thermostat | ECM motor or outdoor control/drive fault | Record the code; cycle power once. If the fault returns, leave the unit off and call a technician | Diagnose the control/drive and ECM; replace as needed |

## Repair costs

These are typical US installed prices, including the service call. Brand-specific (OEM) parts and the cost of living in your area can raise them.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $80–$200 |
| Run capacitor (single or dual) | $120–$400 |
| Contactor | $150–$400 |
| PSC condenser fan motor (plus capacitor) | $400–$900 |
| Fan blade | $150–$350 |
| Wiring repair | $100–$350 |
| Variable-speed ECM fan motor | $600–$1,200 |
| Outdoor control board (variable-speed) | $500–$1,200 |
| Compressor damage from running without the fan | $1,500–$3,500+, and may justify replacing the unit |

If your unit is still under American Standard's registered parts warranty (often 10 years), the part may be covered. You would still pay for labor.

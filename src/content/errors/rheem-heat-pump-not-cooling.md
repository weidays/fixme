## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a single fault code. Your Rheem heat pump is either not running in cooling mode, or it runs but does not remove enough heat from the house. Rheem heat pumps cool with the same refrigerant circuit they use for heating. A reversing valve redirects the refrigerant flow when the thermostat calls for Cool. Problems specific to cooling usually involve:

- the outdoor unit not starting
- the reversing valve not shifting
- refrigerant charge
- airflow across the indoor coil

Some Rheem/Ruud control boards and EcoNet systems log a diagnostic code alongside this symptom. If your outdoor unit's LED is flashing, decode it first. Whether a code auto-resets or holds until power is cycled depends on the board, so check the label on the control panel cover. Low-pressure and high-pressure lockouts on many models clear after a timed delay. Repeated trips can hold the unit off until power is cycled.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat setting or communication problem.** The thermostat is set to Heat, Auto or Fan, has dead batteries, or (on EcoNet systems) has lost communication with the unit.
2. **Dirty air filter or restricted airflow.** This starves the indoor coil and can freeze it, so the system runs without cooling.
3. **Failed run capacitor.** The compressor hums or the outdoor fan won't spin. This is one of the most common outdoor-unit failures in hot weather.
4. **Failed or pitted contactor.** The outdoor unit gets no power even though the indoor blower runs.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** The unit runs constantly with weak cooling. You may see ice on the lines or a low-pressure lockout.
6. **Dirty outdoor coil or failed outdoor fan motor.** This causes high head pressure, high-pressure switch trips and short cycling.
7. **Reversing valve or solenoid coil fault.** The unit blows warm air in Cool because it is stuck in heating. Rheem heat pumps energize the O terminal for cooling, so a wiring or solenoid fault leaves the valve in its de-energized heating position.
8. **Defrost/control board fault, or a tripped pressure switch lockout.** Behavior and codes vary by model.
9. **Compressor failure.** This is the least likely cause and the most expensive repair.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Cool** and lower the setpoint at least 3°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank. Wait 5 minutes, because most systems have a compressor protection delay.
- **Air filter:** Pull it out. If it is gray or clogged, replace it with the correct size. If the indoor coil or refrigerant lines are iced, set the system to **Off** with the fan **On** for a few hours to thaw before trying Cool again.
- **Breakers and disconnect:** Check the breakers for both the indoor air handler and the outdoor unit. Also check the outdoor disconnect switch near the condenser. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **One power reset:** Turn the system off at the thermostat. Switch off both breakers for 5 minutes, then restore power. Do this only once. Repeated resets can mask a fault and stress the compressor.
- **Vents and outdoor clearance:** Open supply registers and make sure return grilles aren't blocked. Clear leaves, grass clippings and debris from around the outdoor unit, keeping about 2 feet of clearance. Do not open the outdoor unit.
- **Condensate:** Many air handlers have a float switch that shuts off cooling when the drain pan backs up. Check whether the drain line is clogged or the pan is full. Clear the line's accessible outdoor end, or use a wet/dry vac at the drain outlet.
- **Panels:** Make sure the air handler's access panels are fully seated.

If the outdoor unit hums but the fan won't spin, turn the system off. Do not push the fan blade with a stick. That is a common internet tip, but it is a shock and injury hazard.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will typically work through these steps:

1. Verify the thermostat call: 24V on Y (compressor) and O (reversing valve) at the outdoor unit.
2. Read any board LED or EcoNet fault history.
3. Test the run capacitor's microfarad rating against its label.
4. Inspect the contactor for pitting and confirm line voltage passes through.
5. Measure compressor and fan motor amperage against nameplate ratings.
6. Connect gauges or probes to check suction and liquid pressures, superheat and subcooling for the charge type (R-410A or R-454B on newer units).
7. Measure the temperature split across the indoor coil. Roughly 16–22°F is typical.
8. Confirm the reversing valve shifts by checking line temperatures and the solenoid coil.
9. Check the pressure switches and the condition of the outdoor coil.

If refrigerant is low, expect leak detection before recharge. A quote that just "adds Freon" every year with no leak search is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nothing runs, indoor or outdoor | Thermostat, breaker, condensate float switch | Check thermostat mode and batteries, breakers, drain pan | Test transformer, fuses and low-voltage wiring |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Contactor, disconnect, capacitor, board | Check outdoor breaker and disconnect, one reset | Test and replace the contactor or capacitor, diagnose the board |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or fan motor | Turn the system off | Replace the capacitor or fan motor |
| Runs constantly, air barely cool | Low refrigerant, dirty filter or coil | Replace the filter, clear debris around the outdoor unit | Leak search, repair, recharge, coil cleaning |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Restricted airflow or low charge | Replace the filter, run Fan only to thaw | Check charge and blower, find the leak |
| Blows warm air in Cool | Reversing valve or O-wire fault | Confirm the thermostat is set to Cool | Test the solenoid, wiring and valve; replace the valve if needed |
| Starts, then shuts off within minutes | High- or low-pressure lockout | Clean around the outdoor unit, check the filter | Diagnose the pressure fault, fan or charge |
| Breaker trips when the compressor starts | Shorted or grounded compressor, bad capacitor | Do not reset repeatedly | Megohm and amperage tests, compressor replacement |

## Repair costs

Ranges below are typical US prices, parts and labor.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic or service call | $90 – $200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Condensate drain clearing or float switch | $100 – $250 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350 – $800 |
| Coil cleaning (outdoor or indoor) | $100 – $400 |
| Leak search plus repair plus recharge | $400 – $1,500+ (R-454B and evaporator leaks run higher) |
| Reversing valve solenoid coil | $150 – $400 |
| Reversing valve replacement | $1,000 – $2,500 |
| Control or defrost board | $300 – $800 |
| Thermostat replacement (DIY) | $30 – $250 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $3,500+ |

On an older unit that needs a compressor or reversing valve, compare the quote against a full system replacement. That typically costs $6,000–$12,000 installed.

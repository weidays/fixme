## What this code means

"Compressor not running" is a symptom rather than a numbered fault code on most Lennox air conditioners. The thermostat is calling for cooling, but the compressor in the outdoor unit is not pumping refrigerant. Depending on the fault, you may notice:

- The outdoor fan spins, but you don't hear the compressor's hum.
- The unit hums or clicks without starting.
- The outdoor unit is completely silent.

Indoors, the blower usually keeps moving room-temperature air.

How this shows up depends on the model:

- **Standard single- and two-stage Lennox units** (Merit, most Elite) have no diagnostic display at the outdoor unit. Diagnosis is electrical: the technician checks the contactor, capacitor, overload and windings.
- **Some Elite and Signature units** have an outdoor control board with LED flash codes. This board can lock out the compressor after repeated high- or low-pressure switch trips.
- **Inverter models with iComfort/S30** (XC25, SL28XCV) show specific alert codes on the thermostat. Those codes and their manuals take priority over this general guide.

**Reset behavior varies:**

- The compressor's internal overload resets on its own once the motor cools.
- The board's anti-short-cycle timer adds about 5 minutes after power interruptions.
- Pressure-switch lockouts on boards that have them may need a power cycle to clear.

This page covers the compressor specifically. If the outdoor fan is also dead, or the breaker trips, see the related pages listed below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** This is the most common cause. Without capacitor assist, the compressor hums, trips its internal overload, and stops. On many units a dual capacitor also serves the outdoor fan, but often only the compressor side fails.
2. **Worn or pitted contactor.** The contactor's points may be burned, or its 24 V coil may have failed. The compressor never receives 240 V, or receives it on only one leg.
3. **Compressor internal thermal overload open.** The compressor overheated and its overload opened. Common triggers are low refrigerant charge, a dirty condenser coil, an outdoor fan that isn't running, or a weak capacitor. The overload resets on its own, but the underlying cause remains.
4. **High- or low-pressure switch open or board lockout** (models with pressure switches). Low refrigerant from a leak opens the low-pressure switch. A blocked condenser coil or failed fan opens the high-pressure switch. On units with an outdoor control board, repeated trips cause a lockout.
5. **Low-voltage control problem.** A thermostat Y-signal isn't reaching the outdoor unit. Possible causes are a damaged thermostat wire, a blown 3 A fuse on the furnace or air handler board, or a tripped float switch on the condensate drain cutting the 24 V circuit.
6. **Wiring faults.** Burned or loose terminals at the compressor, contactor or capacitor.
7. **Compressor mechanical or electrical failure.** Windings may be open, shorted or grounded, or the compressor may be locked mechanically. This is less common, but it is the most expensive outcome.
8. **Inverter drive or board fault** (variable-capacity models only). Diagnosed from the iComfort/S30 alert codes.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it is set to COOL, with the setpoint at least 3°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it has them. Wait 5–10 minutes, because the built-in compressor delay is normal.
- **Breakers and disconnect:** Check the AC breaker in the main panel and the outdoor disconnect switch. If a breaker is tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it is dirty. A clogged filter can freeze the coil and cause low-pressure trips.
- **Condensate line and float switch:** Check whether the drain pan or float switch near the indoor coil is holding water. A tripped float switch cuts cooling power. Clearing a clogged drain line at its outside end, or with the access tee using a wet/dry vac, is a reasonable homeowner task.
- **Outdoor unit surroundings:** Clear leaves, grass clippings and debris from around the unit, keeping about 2 feet of clearance. Make sure the exterior panels are seated. Don't open the service panel.
- **One reset:** If the unit seems locked out, switch the thermostat off and cut power at the breaker for 5 minutes. Then restore power and call for cooling once. If the compressor still doesn't start, or starts and then quits, stop. Running a struggling compressor over and over can destroy it.

If you hear humming and clicking from the outdoor unit, turn cooling off and call for service.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should roughly follow this order:

1. **Confirm the 24 V call.** Measure 24 V across the contactor coil while the thermostat calls for cooling. If it's missing, trace back through the low-voltage circuit: the thermostat, the indoor board fuse, the float switch, any pressure switches, and the outdoor board's lockout or flash code.
2. **Check line voltage.** Measure 240 V at the contactor line side and load side. Inspect the contactor points for pitting or burning.
3. **Test the capacitor.** Discharge it safely, then measure its µF against the rating printed on it. A reading more than about 6% below rating, or a bulging case, means replacement.
4. **Check the compressor windings.** Measure resistance across C-R, C-S and R-S. An open reading usually means the internal overload is open. Let the motor cool and retest before condemning the compressor.
5. **Megohm or ground test.** Check the windings to ground. A grounded compressor has to be replaced.
6. **Measure amp draw on startup.** A locked-rotor reading that never drops points to a locked compressor. A hard-start kit is sometimes tried first.
7. **Check refrigerant pressures and the condenser.** Check charge, coil condition and fan operation to find why the overload or pressure switch opened. A leak search is required before any refrigerant is added.

**Questions to raise about a quote:**

- If a tech recommends a new compressor without showing winding or ground readings, ask for them.
- If a tech adds refrigerant without mentioning a leak, ask where the refrigerant went.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor fan runs; compressor hums, then clicks off | Failed run capacitor, or compressor tripping on overload | Turn cooling off; don't keep retrying | Test and replace the capacitor; check amp draw; consider a hard-start kit |
| Outdoor unit completely silent | No 24 V signal, tripped breaker or disconnect, failed contactor coil | Check thermostat, breaker, disconnect and float switch; one reset | Trace the 24 V circuit and indoor fuse; replace contactor |
| Fan runs, compressor silent, no hum | Contactor passing only one leg, open overload, wiring fault | Turn off cooling and let the unit cool | Inspect contactor and wiring; check windings after cool-down |
| Compressor runs a few minutes, then stops repeatedly | Overheating from low charge or a dirty coil, pressure-switch trips | Clear debris around unit; replace filter | Check pressures; leak search; clean condenser coil; check fan |
| Water in drain pan, no cooling | Condensate float switch cutting power | Clear the drain line at its outside end | Repair drain or float switch if clearing fails |
| Breaker trips when compressor tries to start | Grounded or locked compressor, shorted wiring | Reset once; don't reset again if it trips | Megohm and winding tests; possible compressor replacement |
| Thermostat shows an alert code (iComfort/S30) | Inverter drive, sensor or pressure fault | Note the code; one power cycle | Diagnose per the model's alert code table |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call or diagnostic | $90 – $200 |
| Run capacitor (single or dual) | $120 – $350 |
| Contactor | $150 – $350 |
| Hard-start kit | $150 – $350 |
| Wiring or terminal repair | $100 – $300 |
| Indoor board fuse, or float switch reset/repair | $90 – $250 |
| Pressure switch | $200 – $450 |
| Leak search and repair, plus refrigerant | $300 – $1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type; R-22 systems cost more) |
| Outdoor control board (two-stage/communicating models) | $400 – $900 |
| Inverter drive or board (variable-capacity models) | $800 – $1,800+ |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $3,500+ |

**Warranty notes:**

- Lennox compressors often carry a 5–10 year parts warranty, depending on the series and whether the unit was registered. The warranty can cut the compressor cost substantially, but labor is usually extra.
- On a unit older than about 12–15 years, a failed compressor often makes replacing the whole system the better value.

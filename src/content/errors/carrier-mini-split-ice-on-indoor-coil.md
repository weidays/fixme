## What this code means

Frost or ice on the indoor coil of a Carrier ductless unit means the coil is running below 32°F. You will usually see it behind the front grille or louvers when the unit is in Cool or Dry mode. It is a symptom, not a single fault. It happens when too little warm air moves across the coil or too little refrigerant feeds it.

Most Carrier mini-split indoor boards use a coil temperature sensor (often labeled T2) to watch for freezing. When the coil gets too cold, anti-freeze protection stops the compressor and keeps the indoor fan running until the coil warms up. Cooling then resumes automatically. This protection resets on its own and does not hold until power is cycled. Whether a fault code or blinking light appears varies by model and board.

If the cause is not fixed, the unit keeps cycling through freeze-ups. You may notice:

- weak cooling
- water dripping from the unit
- eventually, a protection lockout

This page covers the ductless indoor unit specifically. A general frozen coil on a central system is covered under "Frozen evaporator coil."

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty indoor filters.** This is by far the most common cause on wall-mount units. Clogged mesh filters starve the coil of airflow.
2. **Dirty coil or blower wheel.** Dust and biofilm build up on the coil fins or the barrel-style blower wheel. This chokes airflow even when the filters are clean.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Flare connections at the line set are a well-known leak point on mini-splits. Low charge drops coil pressure and temperature.
4. **Indoor fan motor or fan speed problem.** A weak or failing DC fan motor, or a board fault that keeps the fan too slow, reduces airflow.
5. **Running in very low outdoor temperatures in Cool mode.** Cooling below the rated outdoor range (common in server rooms or on mild spring nights) can frost the coil.
6. **Faulty coil temperature sensor or EEV.** A drifted indoor coil sensor can defeat anti-freeze protection. A sticking electronic expansion valve in the outdoor unit can underfeed the coil. How these are tested varies by model.
7. **Very low set point combined with Low fan or Quiet/Sleep mode for long periods.** This can contribute, especially in humid rooms, but is rarely the only cause.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Stop the freeze.** Set the remote to Fan Only, or turn the unit off. Let the ice melt completely. Place towels underneath and never chip at the ice.
- **Clean the filters.** Lift the front panel (this is the user-access panel described in your owner's manual) and slide out the mesh filters. Rinse them in lukewarm water, let them dry fully, and reinstall. Plan to do this every 2–4 weeks in heavy use.
- **Check the remote.** Replace the remote batteries. Make sure the mode is Cool, not a very low set point on Low fan for hours. Try Auto or Medium fan speed.
- **Clear the airflow path.** Move curtains, furniture or shelves away from the unit's intake and louvers.
- **Watch the condensate.** During thawing, make sure water drains outside and isn't backing up. If you can see the drain line's outdoor end, clear any visible blockage or kinks.
- **Do one power reset.** After the coil is fully thawed, reset the breaker or disconnect once, then run Cool and watch it. If ice returns, stop and call a technician.

Do not remove the cabinet cover or the louver assembly. Do not spray coil cleaners into the unit. Do not touch the refrigerant connections.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician should:

1. **Confirm airflow.** Inspect the coil face and the blower wheel, checking for buildup with a light. Measure the temperature drop across the indoor unit. If the coil or wheel is dirty, they will quote a deep clean, which may include removing the blower wheel or bagging the unit for a wash.
2. **Check the indoor fan.** Verify the fan reaches its commanded speeds. On DC-motor units, check the motor's feedback to the board.
3. **Evaluate the charge.** On mini-splits this is typically judged by superheat, subcooling or the manufacturer's charging chart, since most units have only one service port. A low charge should prompt a leak search (electronic detector, bubbles at the flares) **before** any recharge. Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.
4. **Read the sensors.** Measure the indoor coil sensor's resistance against the model's temperature/resistance chart and check its clip placement on the coil.
5. **Check the EEV.** Verify EEV operation at the outdoor unit if pressures suggest a restriction.
6. **Pull stored fault history.** Use the remote's check or diagnostic function or the board's display, where the model supports it.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frost across the coil, filters gray with dust | Dirty filters | Thaw, then wash and dry the filters | Usually not needed |
| Ice returns with clean filters, musty smell, black specks from louvers | Dirty coil or blower wheel | Keep filters clean and run Fan Only to thaw | Deep clean of the coil and blower wheel |
| Ice at one corner or near the line connection, weak cooling | Low refrigerant / leak | Turn off and call | Leak search, flare repair, evacuate and weigh in the charge |
| Airflow weak even on High fan | Indoor fan motor or board | Check for blockages in front of the unit | Test the fan motor and board, replace the failed part |
| Ices up only on cool nights or in mild weather | Operating below the cooling range | Raise the set point or use Dry/Auto | Confirm settings; consider low-ambient options if appropriate |
| Freezes repeatedly with no protection shutdown | Faulty coil sensor or EEV | Thaw and call | Test the sensor against its chart, check the EEV coil and valve |
| Water dripping during and after the freeze | Melt overflowing the pan | Towels; check the drain outlet | Clear the drain and correct the root cause |

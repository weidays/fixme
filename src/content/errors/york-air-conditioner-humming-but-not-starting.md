## What this code means

"Humming but not starting" is a symptom, not a stored fault code. The outdoor condenser on your York AC gets power, but the motors don't turn.

When the thermostat calls for cooling, the contactor closes and sends 240V to the compressor and the outdoor fan motor. If one of those motors gets power but can't turn over, you hear a steady hum or buzz instead of the normal startup. Sometimes you also hear a click a few seconds later as the compressor's internal overload trips.

Usually the hum comes from one of three things:
- A motor that can't get its starting boost from the capacitor
- A contactor that isn't closing fully
- A compressor that is mechanically stuck

York's standard condensers have no self-diagnostic display for this. Variable-speed inverter models, such as the Affinity YXV, behave differently: the inverter board usually logs a fault code rather than letting the compressor hum.

This page covers the humming condition specifically. If the outdoor unit is completely silent, see "Compressor not running" or "Won't turn on" instead.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** This is by far the most common cause on York condensers. Most models use a dual capacitor that serves both the compressor and the fan. When it weakens, a motor gets voltage but no starting torque, so it hums. Heat speeds up capacitor failure, so this shows up most often in summer. A bulging or leaking top is a classic sign, but a capacitor can fail without any visible change.
2. **Pitted or sticking contactor.** Burned contacts can pass enough current to make a motor hum but not enough to start it. The coil can also buzz loudly while the contacts fail to close.
3. **Outdoor fan motor seized or failing.** The fan hums but the blade doesn't spin, while the compressor may or may not run. Dry or worn bearings are typical on older units.
4. **Compressor hard to start or locked rotor.** Aging compressors, especially those that have been off for a while or are running on low voltage, may need a hard-start kit. A mechanically seized compressor will hum, trip its overload, and repeat that cycle.
5. **Low supply voltage or loose high-voltage connection.** A failing breaker, corroded disconnect, or loose lug can deliver too little voltage to start a motor under load.
6. **Compressor winding fault.** An internal short or open winding also produces humming and an overload trip. This is less common than the causes above, but it is the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

First, set the thermostat to OFF so the unit stops trying to start. Then work through these checks:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it is set to COOL with a setpoint well below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter doesn't cause humming by itself, but it adds strain and can confuse the diagnosis.
- **Breaker and disconnect:** Check the AC breaker in the main panel and the disconnect box by the outdoor unit.
  - If the breaker has tripped, reset it once.
  - If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Outdoor unit surroundings:** Look from outside the cabinet only. Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the unit and off the top grille. Keep about 2 feet of clearance. Do not reach into the grille.
- **Exterior panels:** Make sure the access panels are seated and secured.
- **One restart:** If the overload has tripped, leave the system off for 30-60 minutes so the compressor can cool. Then set the thermostat to COOL once.
  - If it hums again, turn it off and call a technician.
  - Do not keep retrying. Repeated attempts can burn out the compressor windings.

Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser. A capacitor can hold a dangerous charge even with the power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should do the following before quoting anything major:

- **Confirm the hum's source:** compressor, fan motor, or contactor coil.
- **Measure line voltage** at the disconnect and at the contactor, both at rest and during the start attempt, to rule out low voltage.
- **Inspect the contactor** for pitted contacts and check voltage drop across it.
- **Discharge and test the capacitor** with a meter that reads microfarads, comparing it to the rated value. Readings more than about 6-10% low mean replacement.
- **Test the fan motor** by checking its windings and seeing whether the shaft turns freely by hand with the power off.
- **Check the compressor:**
  - Measure winding resistance across common, start, and run.
  - Check for a short to ground with a megohmmeter or meter.
  - Measure amp draw. Locked-rotor amps (LRA) on startup point to a stuck compressor.
- **If the windings test good but the compressor won't start,** try a hard-start kit before condemning the compressor.

Be wary of a compressor replacement quote that wasn't preceded by a capacitor test and winding checks. Ask to see the readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hum from outdoor unit, fan not spinning, no cooling | Failed dual run capacitor | Turn thermostat off; check breaker | Test and replace capacitor |
| Loud buzz, nothing starts | Pitted or sticking contactor | Turn thermostat off | Replace contactor |
| Compressor runs, fan hums but won't turn | Fan capacitor or seized fan motor | Clear debris outside the grille | Replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Hum, then click after a few seconds, repeating | Compressor locked rotor or overload tripping | Shut off; wait 30-60 min; try once | Test windings, install hard-start kit, or replace compressor |
| Hum plus dimming lights or breaker trip | Low voltage, grounded compressor, or loose connection | Reset breaker once only | Check voltage and connections; test compressor for ground |
| Bulging capacitor seen during a service visit | Capacitor failure from heat or age | None | Replace capacitor; check contactor |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic or service call | $75-$200 |
| Run capacitor (single or dual) | $150-$450 |
| Contactor | $150-$400 |
| Hard-start kit | $200-$450 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $400-$900 |
| Wiring or disconnect repair | $150-$500 |
| Compressor replacement (in warranty, labor and refrigerant only) | $1,200-$2,500 |
| Compressor replacement (out of warranty) | $1,800-$3,500+ |
| Full condenser replacement | $3,500-$7,500+ |

**Warranty:** York typically offers a 10-year limited parts warranty on registered units. It often covers the compressor, but labor is usually extra.

**Repair or replace:** On a system more than 12-15 years old, or one that uses R-22 refrigerant, a failed compressor is often the point where replacing the whole unit makes more sense.

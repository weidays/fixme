## What this code means

"Compressor not running" is a symptom, not a numbered York fault code. You'll usually notice it because:

- The indoor blower runs.
- The outdoor fan may spin.
- The outdoor unit doesn't make the steady hum of the compressor.
- Supply air comes out at room temperature.

On single-stage York units there is usually no fault display. On two-stage and variable-speed models, the outdoor control board or inverter drive may flash a status LED that points to the cause. Those flash meanings differ by board, so check the label inside your unit's service panel or your manual rather than a generic chart.

**How resets work:**

- **Internal overload:** The compressor has a built-in overload that opens when the compressor overheats. It resets automatically once the compressor cools, which can take 30 to 60 minutes or more.
- **Anti-short-cycle delay:** York boards typically wait about 5 minutes after a shutdown before restarting the compressor.
- **Lockouts:** Some boards lock out after repeated high- or low-pressure trips. These lockouts hold until the power is cycled. Behavior varies by model.

**How this page differs from related pages:**

- If the outdoor fan is also stopped, see "Outdoor fan not spinning."
- If the whole system feels warm and you aren't sure which part has failed, start with "Not cooling."

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** This is the most common cause. A weak or failed dual capacitor can stop the compressor from starting while the fan still runs, or the fan may stall too. You may hear a hum followed by a click as the overload trips.
2. **Compressor thermal overload tripped.** Overheating causes this, often from:
   - a dirty outdoor coil
   - a weak capacitor
   - low refrigerant charge
   - high outdoor temperatures

   It clears by itself once the compressor cools.
3. **Faulty or pitted contactor.** Burned contacts may close for the fan circuit but not pass enough voltage to the compressor. Insects or debris in the contactor are also common.
4. **Open high- or low-pressure switch.** Many York units have these, depending on the model. A low-pressure trip points to low charge or a refrigerant leak. A high-pressure trip points to a dirty condenser coil or a fan problem.
5. **No 24V call reaching the outdoor unit.** This can be caused by:
   - thermostat setup
   - a broken low-voltage wire
   - a blown 3A or 5A fuse on the furnace or air handler board
   - a tripped float or condensate safety switch on the indoor unit
6. **Control board or inverter drive fault.** This applies to two-stage and variable-speed models only. The board may hold the compressor off because of a sensor fault, a communication error or a lockout.
7. **Loose or burned high-voltage wiring.** Check compressor terminals and contactor lugs.
8. **Failed compressor.** The causes are open or shorted windings, a grounded motor or a seized compressor. This is the least common cause, and a technician should confirm it with measurements before you accept a replacement quote.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat.** Make sure it is set to COOL and set several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank. If you have a smart thermostat, check that it isn't in a compressor-delay or eco mode.
- **Wait out the delay.** After any change, wait at least 5 to 10 minutes. The anti-short-cycle timer may be holding the compressor off.
- **Breakers and disconnect.** Check the outdoor AC breaker at the main panel and the outdoor disconnect switch. If a breaker has tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and see "Breaker keeps tripping."
- **Air filter.** Replace the filter if it's dirty. A clogged filter can cause low-pressure trips.
- **Condensate line.** Look at the indoor drain pan and line. If the pan is full or the float switch is tripped, the system will stop calling for cooling. Clearing a visible clog at the line's outlet or cleanout is fine.
- **Outdoor unit exterior.** Remove leaves, grass clippings or covers that block airflow around the coil. Keep at least 2 feet clear. Don't open the cabinet.
- **Vents.** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked.
- **One reset.** Turn the thermostat off, switch off the outdoor disconnect, and wait 5 minutes. Restore power, then set the thermostat back to COOL. Do this only once. If the compressor is hot, give it an hour to cool first.

If the compressor still doesn't run, call a licensed HVAC technician. Everything inside the outdoor cabinet carries 240V, and capacitors stay charged even after the power is off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the usual steps helps you judge whether a quote is reasonable. A technician will typically:

1. **Confirm the 24V call** at the outdoor contactor coil. If it's missing, they'll trace it back through the thermostat, low-voltage wiring, indoor board fuse and safety switches.
2. **Check line voltage** across the contactor, before and after it. Burned or pitted contacts are visible.
3. **Discharge and test the capacitor** with a microfarad meter. They'll compare the reading to the rating on the label, which usually allows ±6%.
4. **Check whether the compressor is hot.** If it is, they'll let it cool, or cool it with water, before condemning it, because the overload may simply be open.
5. **Measure winding resistance** across the C, S and R terminals. They'll also check for a short to ground with a megohmmeter.
6. **Measure amp draw on startup.** If the compressor hums and draws locked-rotor amps, the technician may try a hard-start kit.
7. **Check refrigerant pressures** once the compressor runs, to identify low charge or a restriction. A refrigerant leak needs leak searching and repair, not just a top-off.
8. **Read board or inverter LED codes** on two-stage and variable-speed models. They'll check sensor inputs and communication wiring.

**Warning sign on a quote:** a compressor replacement quoted without capacitor, voltage and winding test results.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor fan runs, compressor silent, top of unit hot | Thermal overload tripped | Turn off for 1 hour, clear debris around unit, reset once | Find the cause of overheating: capacitor, coil, charge |
| Hum then click every few minutes | Failed capacitor or stuck compressor | Turn system off to prevent damage | Test and replace capacitor; consider hard-start kit; check windings |
| Nothing runs outdoors, indoor blower runs | No 24V call, tripped breaker, float switch, or blown control fuse | Check thermostat, breaker, disconnect, condensate drain | Trace low voltage, replace fuse, repair wiring |
| Fan runs but compressor never starts, no hum | Pitted contactor, open pressure switch, loose wire | Check filter and outdoor airflow | Replace contactor, diagnose pressure switch, tighten or replace wiring |
| Compressor starts then stops after minutes | Low charge (low-pressure switch) or dirty coil (high-pressure) | Replace filter, rinse outdoor debris from exterior only | Leak search, repair and recharge; clean coil |
| Board LED flashing (two-stage or variable-speed models) | Board, sensor, or inverter fault or lockout | Note the flash pattern, cycle power once | Interpret code, test sensors and board, replace if needed |
| Breaker trips when compressor tries to start | Shorted or grounded compressor or wiring | Do not reset repeatedly | Megohm test, wiring repair or compressor replacement |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices, including parts and labor:

| Item | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| DIY checks (thermostat batteries, filter, breaker, drain) | $0–$40 |
| Service call or diagnostic | $90–$200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Hard-start kit | $200–$450 |
| Control fuse or low-voltage wiring repair | $100–$300 |
| Pressure switch replacement (refrigerant recovery may be needed) | $250–$600 |
| Refrigerant leak repair and recharge | $400–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type: R-410A or R-454B) |
| Outdoor control board or inverter drive (two-stage or variable-speed) | $500–$1,500 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800–$3,500+ (more for variable-speed models; less if covered by York's parts warranty, which is often 10 years when registered, though labor is usually not covered) |

If your system is older than 12 to 15 years and needs a new compressor, get a quote for a full condenser replacement as well. It often makes better financial sense.

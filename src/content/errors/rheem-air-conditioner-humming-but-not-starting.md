## What this code means

"Humming but not starting" is not a displayed error code on most Rheem air conditioners. It is a symptom. The outdoor condenser is getting the call for cooling, and the contactor has pulled in to send 240 volts to the motors. Then the compressor, the condenser fan motor, or both try to start and cannot. You hear a steady buzz or hum, but the fan doesn't spin, or the compressor never kicks on.

This page is different from "Compressor not running" and "Outdoor fan not spinning." Those cover units that are silent or partially working. A hum means power is reaching a motor that is stalled. A stalled motor draws several times its normal current. The longer it sits like that, the more likely a cheap repair turns into an expensive one.

On single-stage and two-stage Rheem units, the compressor's internal overload usually trips after a short time. The hum then stops, and the overload resets automatically once the compressor cools, which can take 30 to 90 minutes. The unit then tries again. That repeating pattern can look like intermittent operation.

Variable-speed inverter models, such as EcoNet-enabled units, behave differently. They typically log a fault on the outdoor control board, so a technician should read the board's diagnostic codes first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** This is by far the most common cause. Many Rheem condensers use a dual capacitor that serves both the compressor and the fan motor. If one side fails, that motor hums but cannot start. A bulging or leaking top is a telltale sign, but a capacitor can test bad and still look normal.
2. **Burned or pitted contactor contacts.** Worn contacts can pass enough voltage to make a motor hum, but not enough current to start it. A contactor that has lost one leg of power does the same thing.
3. **Seized or failing condenser fan motor.** Worn bearings can stop the fan from turning even with a good capacitor. The compressor then overheats and trips on its overload.
4. **Hard-starting compressor.** An aging compressor may struggle to start against system pressure, especially after a short off-cycle. A technician may fit a start capacitor or hard-start kit.
5. **Low supply voltage.** A loose connection at the disconnect, a failing breaker, or utility voltage sag on hot afternoons can all leave the motors unable to start.
6. **Locked or grounded compressor.** If the compressor is mechanically seized, or a winding is shorted or open, it will hum and trip without ever starting. This is the least common cause, but the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Turn the system off now.** Set the thermostat to OFF so the stalled motor stops overheating. Wait at least 30 minutes before trying again.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it is set to COOL, and set it a few degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Check the breaker and outdoor disconnect.** Look for a tripped or half-tripped double-pole breaker labeled AC or condenser. Reset it once.
  - If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
  - The outdoor disconnect switch or pull-out block should be fully seated.
- **Replace a dirty air filter.** A restricted filter raises system pressure, which makes the compressor harder to start.
- **Clear around the outdoor unit.** Remove leaves, grass clippings and debris you can reach from outside the cabinet. Keep about 2 feet clear on all sides.
  - Do not open the panels.
  - Do not reach through the fan grille.
- **Try one restart.** After the 30-minute rest, turn cooling back on and listen.
  - If it hums again without the fan spinning or the compressor starting, shut it off and call a pro.
  - Do not keep cycling it.
- **Check that the panels are seated.** Make sure the service panels on the outdoor unit are fully closed. Do not open them.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically work through these steps:

1. **Verify supply voltage** at the disconnect and contactor, with the unit off and then under load. Both legs should be present, near 240V, and stay within the nameplate range when the motors try to start.
2. **Inspect and test the contactor.** This includes looking for pitted contacts and measuring voltage drop across each pole.
3. **Discharge and test the capacitor** with a capacitance meter. Each side should read within the rated tolerance printed on the label, often ±5–6%.
4. **Check the condenser fan motor.** The technician will spin the fan by hand with the power off to check for free bearings, then check the windings.
5. **Test the compressor windings** (common, start and run) for resistance and for a short to ground with a megohmmeter or meter. They will also check whether the internal overload is open, which means waiting for the compressor to cool.
6. **Measure amperage on startup.** Locked-rotor amps that never fall back to running amps confirm a stalled compressor. They may then try a start capacitor temporarily as a diagnostic.
7. **Read the control board fault codes** on inverter models.

**How to sanity-check a quote:**

- If you are told the compressor is bad, ask for the winding and ground test readings.
- Ask whether a capacitor or hard-start kit was tried first.
- A capacitor diagnosis should come with a measured microfarad reading that is out of spec.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor unit hums, fan doesn't spin, no cooling | Failed fan side of dual capacitor or seized fan motor | Turn system off, check breaker, clear debris outside the unit | Test and replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Fan runs, compressor hums then clicks off | Weak compressor side of capacitor or hard-starting compressor | Turn off, wait 30 min, try one restart | Replace capacitor, possibly add hard-start kit |
| Hum repeats every hour or so | Compressor overload tripping and resetting | Leave system off and call for service | Test windings, capacitor and amperage |
| Hum with dimming lights or breaker trips | Locked or grounded compressor, or a supply problem | Reset breaker once only; if it trips again, leave it off | Megohm test compressor, check wiring and breaker |
| Buzzing at the unit with nothing moving | Pitted contactor passing partial power | Turn system off | Replace contactor |
| Hums only on hot afternoons | Low voltage or marginal capacitor | Note the times and tell the technician | Load-test voltage, check capacitor |
| Inverter model shows fault, unit won't start | Drive or board fault, or compressor fault | Cycle power once at the breaker | Read board codes and diagnose per Rheem service data |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

- **Run capacitor (single or dual):** $120–$350
- **Start capacitor or hard-start kit:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$750 (more for ECM motors)
- **Electrical repair at disconnect or breaker:** $150–$500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800–$4,500+
  - Warranty coverage may reduce the parts cost if the unit is registered. Labor and refrigerant are usually not covered.
  - On older systems, replacing the whole condenser is often the better value.
- **Diagnostic or service call:** $80–$200, often credited toward the repair

Catching it early matters. If you shut the unit off quickly when it starts humming, the fix is often a capacitor or contactor rather than a compressor.

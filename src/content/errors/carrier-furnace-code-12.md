## What this code means

Carrier Furnace **Code 12** means **"Blower On After Power Up"** — the control board detected that either its **115 VAC** (line voltage) or **24 VAC** (control/thermostat voltage) power was interrupted *during* a call for heat.

When power returns, the board plays it safe: it runs the **blower for about 90 seconds** to clear any residual heat from the heat exchanger, then returns to normal operation. That's the whole story — this is a **status/history code, not a lockout**.

Because it's a self-clearing code, **you do not need to reset the furnace**. Once power is stable and a normal cycle completes, Code 12 stops flashing. It may still sit in the board's fault-history memory as a record that a power interruption happened.

The value of Code 12 is what it *tells* you: if it shows up repeatedly, your furnace is losing power mid-cycle, and something is causing that — anything from a tripped breaker to a loose wire or a failing transformer. Those are worth chasing down.

> Note: LED flash legends can vary slightly between board revisions. Always confirm the flash count against the diagnostic label printed inside the blower compartment door of your specific unit.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **A normal utility power blip or outage** — a brief grid interruption during a heat call. Extremely common after storms or momentary sags. Nothing is wrong with the furnace.
2. **A tripped breaker or a bumped furnace disconnect switch** — someone switched off the service switch, or the breaker tripped and reset.
3. **Loose or corroded 115 VAC line-voltage connections** — at the disconnect, junction box, or board line terminals, causing intermittent dropout.
4. **Loose or damaged 24 VAC control wiring** — thermostat wire, board terminals, or a low-voltage splice interrupting control power momentarily.
5. **A weak or failing low-voltage transformer** — dropping out under load and briefly killing the 24 VAC control circuit.
6. **A control board issue** — an aging board that momentarily loses its own power or misreads voltage. Least common; diagnosed by elimination.

If Code 12 appears **once** after a known storm or outage, it usually needs no action. If it appears **repeatedly**, causes 3–6 deserve a technician's attention.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no cabinet, no wiring, no tools inside the unit.

- **Check your thermostat.** Confirm it's set to *Heat* and the setpoint is above room temperature. If it's a battery model, **replace the batteries** — a dying thermostat can drop the call for heat.
- **Check the breaker.** Find the furnace circuit in your electrical panel. If it's tripped, switch it fully off, then back on **once**.
- **Check the furnace switch.** The wall switch near the furnace (looks like a light switch) must be **ON**. Make sure it wasn't bumped off.
- **Was there a recent power outage or storm?** If so, Code 12 is almost certainly just a record of that event — no further action needed.
- **Check that the blower door is fully seated.** A panel that isn't closed all the way can interrupt power via the door interlock. Reseat it firmly.
- **Look at supply and return vents.** Make sure registers are open and unobstructed so airflow is normal once the furnace runs.

If the code keeps returning after these checks, stop here — the remaining causes are inside the cabinet and are technician work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech treats Code 12 as a *clue*, then hunts for the intermittent power loss. Expect them to:

- **Read the full fault history** from the control board to see how often Code 12 has logged and what other codes accompany it.
- **Measure 115 VAC** at the disconnect and at the board's line terminals, checking for voltage sags or dropout.
- **Inspect and tighten line-voltage connections** at the disconnect, junction box, and board.
- **Measure 24 VAC** at the board and test the **low-voltage transformer** under load for dropout.
- **Check the door interlock switch and its wiring** for intermittent contact.
- **Inspect thermostat wiring** end-to-end for loose terminals, corrosion, or damaged splices.
- **Evaluate the control board** last, replacing it only if power delivery elsewhere checks out clean.

A good tech will not simply swap the board without first confirming the power supply is stable — that's the sanity check on any quote you get.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Code 12 once after a storm/outage | Utility power blip | Verify thermostat & breaker; no further action | None needed unless recurring |
| Blower runs ~90s then furnace works normally | Normal post-power-up blower purge | Confirm normal heating resumes | None — expected behavior |
| Breaker tripped, Code 12 logged | Tripped breaker / off switch | Reset breaker once; check furnace switch | Investigate why breaker tripped if repeated |
| Code 12 recurs with no outages | Loose 115 VAC connection | Reseat blower door | Test & tighten line-voltage connections |
| Intermittent heating, Code 12 repeating | Loose 24 VAC / thermostat wiring | Replace thermostat batteries | Inspect & repair low-voltage wiring |
| Code 12 with random dropouts under load | Failing transformer | None | Test & replace 24 VAC transformer |
| Persistent Code 12, wiring checks clean | Control board fault | None | Replace control board |

## Repair costs

Code 12 itself is free to "fix" when it's just a power blip. Costs only appear when a real intermittent fault is found:

- **No repair needed (power blip / outage):** **$0**
- **Diagnostic / service call:** **$90 – $180** (often waived if repair is done)
- **Tighten or repair line- or low-voltage connections:** **$100 – $250**
- **Low-voltage transformer replacement:** **$150 – $300**
- **Thermostat replacement (if it's dropping the call):** **$120 – $350** installed; **$20 – $250** DIY for the stat itself
- **Control board replacement:** **$350 – $700+** depending on model

Get connection and transformer checks done before authorizing a control board — the board is the most expensive and least likely cause.

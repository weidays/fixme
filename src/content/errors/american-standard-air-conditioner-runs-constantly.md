## What this code means

"Runs constantly" is a symptom, not a fault code. American Standard condensers don't display a code for it. On communicating AccuLink systems, the thermostat or outdoor control may log related alerts, such as low-pressure or high-pressure trips, which a technician can read.

It shows up in two different ways, and the difference matters:

- **The system runs but can't reach the setpoint.** This is a capacity problem. Airflow, refrigerant charge, a dirty coil or an undersized system is limiting how much heat the system can remove.
- **The system keeps running after the thermostat is satisfied or turned off.** This is a control problem. The usual culprits are a stuck contactor, a thermostat fault or a wiring short holding the call for cooling on.

There is no lockout to reset for this symptom. It continues until the underlying cause is fixed.

Variable-speed and two-stage models behave differently. Long, low-speed run cycles are normal for them by design.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return/supply vents.** Restricted airflow cuts capacity, so the system runs longer and longer.
2. **Thermostat setting or fault.** Common examples:
   - The fan is set to ON instead of AUTO, so the blower runs constantly even when cooling is off.
   - The setpoint is set too low.
   - Batteries are weak.
   - The thermostat is mislocated, such as in sun or near a heat source.
   - The thermostat itself has failed.
3. **Extreme heat, or a system undersized for the load.** Residential systems are typically sized to hold about 75°F indoors at your area's outdoor design temperature, which varies by climate. When it's hotter outside than that design temperature, continuous running can be normal.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil.** Cottonwood, grass clippings or dirt reduce heat rejection.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** You may see long runs, weak cooling and sometimes icing.
6. **Stuck or welded contactor in the outdoor unit.** The compressor and fan run even with the thermostat off.
7. **Low-voltage wiring short.** For example, a nicked thermostat wire or a wire damaged by a string trimmer can energize the cooling call (Y) continuously.
8. **Duct leaks or poor insulation.** Cooled air is lost in the attic or crawlspace.
9. **Failing compressor or two-stage/variable-speed control problem.** The system runs but delivers reduced capacity.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:**
  - Confirm the mode is COOL and the fan is set to AUTO, not ON.
  - Set a realistic temperature, such as 74–78°F.
  - Replace the batteries if the thermostat uses them.
  - Make sure sunlight or lamps aren't heating the thermostat.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it's dirty, and use the correct size. Check it monthly in peak season.
- **Vents and registers:** Open all supply registers and clear furniture or rugs away from return grilles.
- **Outdoor unit (from outside only):**
  - Keep plants and debris at least 2 feet away.
  - Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose with the power OFF at the disconnect. Don't open the cabinet.
- **Condensate line:** Check that the drain is flowing and the drain pan isn't full.
- **If the outdoor unit runs with the thermostat OFF:** Switch the system off at the outdoor disconnect or breaker. Leave it off and call a technician to avoid compressor damage.
- **Check for ice:** Look for ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil. If you see any, turn cooling off, run the fan only to thaw, and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A technician will typically work through these checks:

- **Thermostat call:** Verify the thermostat call (Y/G voltage), and check whether the outdoor unit drops out when the thermostat is satisfied.
- **Contactor:** Inspect the contactor for pitted or welded contacts and a coil that won't release.
- **Low-voltage wiring:** Check the thermostat cable for shorts between R and Y.
- **Refrigerant charge:** Measure superheat and subcooling against American Standard's charging chart. If the charge is low, they'll leak-search with an electronic detector or nitrogen.
- **Temperature split:** Measure the split across the indoor coil. 16–22°F is typical.
- **Airflow:** Measure static pressure, then inspect the blower and indoor coil.
- **Condenser coil:** Inspect and clean it.
- **Compressor:** Check amperage against the rated load amps.
- **Communicating systems:** Pull stored alerts from the AccuLink thermostat or outdoor control.
- **Ductwork:** Inspect for leaks and assess whether the system is sized correctly for the home's load.

A good quote names the measured problem, such as "subcooling 3°F, target 10°F." It should not just say "needs freon."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower never stops | Fan set to ON | Set fan to AUTO | Check fan relay/control board if it persists |
| Runs, never reaches setpoint, weak airflow | Dirty filter/blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents | Static pressure test, coil/blower cleaning |
| Runs all afternoon on very hot days | Load exceeds design capacity | Raise setpoint, close blinds | Load calculation, duct/insulation review |
| Outdoor unit runs with thermostat off | Stuck contactor or shorted wire | Shut off at disconnect | Replace contactor, repair wiring |
| Long runs, lines icing, warm-ish air | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off cooling, run fan to thaw | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor coil matted with debris | Dirty condenser | Hose off fins, power off | Chemical coil cleaning |
| Hot, stuffy rooms near attic | Duct leakage | Check visible registers | Duct sealing/insulation |
| Runs but high amps, poor cooling | Weak compressor | None | Compressor diagnosis/replacement |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges, including labor:

| Repair | Cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter (DIY) | $10–$40 |
| Thermostat replacement | $150–$450 installed ($50–$250 DIY for standard 24V; AccuLink communicating thermostats cost more) |
| Service call/diagnostic | $90–$200 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Low-voltage wiring repair | $100–$350 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$300 |
| Leak search | $200–$600 |
| Refrigerant recharge (R-410A or R-454B) | $250–$900, depending on pounds |
| Leak repair (brazing/fitting) | $200–$1,000 |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$2,800 |
| Duct sealing | $300–$2,000 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,000+ |

Warranty coverage on parts may apply to registered units. Labor is usually not covered unless you bought an extended plan.

## What this code means

"Blowing warm air" is a symptom, not a York fault code. The indoor blower is moving air, but the air coming out of the vents is at or above room temperature. It is not just cool air that feels weak.

On most York split systems, this means the outdoor condenser is not removing heat. The usual reasons are:

- The compressor is not running.
- The unit is not getting cooling power at all.
- The system has lost most of its refrigerant.

Standard single-stage York condensers have no diagnostic display. Some newer inverter and communicating models (for example Affinity with the Hx thermostat) show a fault code on the thermostat or an outdoor-board LED. If you see one, write it down, because it narrows the diagnosis.

This page covers air that is clearly warm. Related problems are covered elsewhere:

- Air that is only slightly cool, or a house that won't reach the setpoint: see "Not cooling."
- Ice on the indoor coil: see "Frozen evaporator coil."
- Outdoor unit completely silent: see "Compressor not running" and "Outdoor fan not spinning."

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat set to Heat, Fan "On," or the wrong setpoint.** With the fan set to On, the blower keeps circulating air even when cooling has stopped. That air can feel warm.
2. **Tripped outdoor breaker or pulled disconnect.** The indoor air handler runs on a separate circuit. The blower keeps running while the condenser has no power, so you get room-temperature air.
3. **Failed run capacitor.** This is the most common failed part in York condensers. The compressor hums or won't start, and the outdoor fan may or may not spin.
4. **Stuck or pitted contactor.** If it won't pull in, the condenser never starts. If it is welded shut, the system can run constantly, sometimes with a frozen or failing system.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** The system runs but delivers little cooling, and the air feels warm. Leaks are commonly found in evaporator coils.
6. **Dirty condenser coil or outdoor fan failure.** The compressor overheats and trips its internal overload. You get cycles of cool air, then warm air.
7. **Low-voltage control problem.** Examples include a blown control fuse, a cut thermostat wire, or a tripped safety switch (high or low pressure, or the condensate float switch on some installs). Any of these stops the condenser from receiving its call to cool.
8. **Compressor failure.** This is usually the last thing diagnosed, after the capacitor, contactor and charge have been ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Set it to Cool and the fan to Auto. Lower the setpoint at least 3°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Breakers and disconnect:** Check the breaker panel for a tripped AC/condenser breaker. You may reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop; see "Breaker keeps tripping." Also check that the outdoor disconnect switch or pull-out block is fully seated. Do not open the disconnect box beyond reseating the handle.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it is dirty. A clogged filter cuts airflow and can lead to coil freezing. You will then get weak airflow and, later, warm air.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply vents are open and return grilles are not blocked by furniture.
- **Outdoor unit, from outside only:** Clear leaves, grass and debris to about 2 feet around the unit. Do not open the panels.
- **Condensate:** If your system has a float switch on the drain pan or line, a full pan can shut off cooling. Check whether the pan or line is overflowing. Clear a clogged drain line at its cleanout using a wet/dry vacuum at the outside end.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn cooling off. Run the fan only so the ice melts, which takes several hours. Then call for service.
- **Wait out an overload:** If the outdoor unit is hot and silent, give it 30–60 minutes off. Then try once. If you get warm air again, call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent visit usually follows this order:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling.** The tech checks for 24 VAC at the condenser contactor coil (Y and C terminals) and inspects the thermostat wiring. On communicating York models, they read the fault history from the thermostat or outdoor board.
2. **Check line voltage** at the disconnect and contactor, and inspect the contactor points.
3. **Test the run capacitor** with a meter and compare the result to its µF rating. The usual tolerance is ±6%.
4. **Measure compressor amps and windings** if the compressor won't start, and check whether the internal overload is open.
5. **Measure the refrigerant system** with gauges or probes: suction and liquid pressures, superheat and subcooling, compared to York's charging chart. They also check the temperature split across the indoor coil. A normal split is about 15–22°F.
6. **Leak search** if the charge is low, using an electronic detector, bubble solution or nitrogen pressure test. The evaporator coil and service valves are common leak spots.
7. **Inspect the condenser coil and fan motor** for overheating causes.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "new compressor" without capacitor, amp and superheat/subcooling readings is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, open disconnect, no 24 V call | Check thermostat mode; reset breaker once; reseat disconnect | Test control voltage, fuse, contactor coil, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan off or compressor not starting | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor fan spins, compressor silent | Capacitor (compressor side), compressor overload, failed compressor | Turn off and let it cool 30–60 min, try once | Test capacitor, windings and amps |
| Runs constantly, air barely cool or warm | Low refrigerant from a leak | Replace filter; check for ice | Measure charge, find and repair leak, recharge |
| Cools for a while, then warm air | Dirty condenser coil, outdoor fan problem, compressor overheating | Clear debris around unit | Clean coil, test fan motor and capacitor |
| Ice on lines or coil, then warm air | Restricted airflow or low charge | Replace filter; run fan only to thaw | Check charge, blower and coil |
| Thermostat code on Affinity or communicating model | Board-reported fault (varies by model) | Note the code; power-cycle once | Read fault history, follow York service manual |
| Water near air handler and no cooling | Float switch tripped by clogged drain | Clear condensate line | Clear trap or pan, check switch wiring |

## Repair costs

Typical US costs, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Thermostat, filter, breaker reset | $0–$50 (DIY) |
| Service call and diagnosis | $90–$200 |
| Run capacitor | $150–$400 |
| Contactor | $150–$400 |
| Low-voltage fuse or wiring repair | $100–$300 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350–$800 |
| Leak search | $200–$500 |
| Leak repair plus recharge | $400–$1,500, depending on refrigerant (R-410A, R-454B, or legacy R-22 at much higher cost) and leak location |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$3,000 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,500+ |

Parts may be covered if your unit is still within York's limited warranty. The compressor is usually covered for 10 years with registration, but labor often is not. On units more than 12–15 years old, or ones using R-22, a compressor or coil failure often makes replacing the system the better value.

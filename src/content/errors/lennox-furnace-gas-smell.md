## What this code means

**If you smell gas right now, stop reading. Get everyone out of the house. Do not touch light switches, the thermostat, phones or anything that could spark while you are inside. Leave the door open as you go. Once you are outside and away from the building, call your gas utility's emergency number or 911.** Do not go back in until responders say it is safe.

"Gas smell" is not a code that a Lennox control board displays. It is a symptom, and it overrides every other troubleshooting step. Natural gas and propane carry an added odorant (mercaptan) that smells like rotten eggs or sulfur. If you notice it near your Lennox furnace, gas may be escaping from:

- the house supply line or flex connector,
- the furnace's internal gas train, or
- the burner area during a faulty ignition.

Lennox boards (SLP, EL, ML, Merit) monitor flame, pressure switches and limits. None of them can sense raw gas in the room. The furnace may keep running, and no code will appear or reset. A gas smell can also point to a venting or combustion problem, which raises carbon-monoxide risk. Treat any persistent gas odor as an emergency until a utility or licensed technician has cleared it.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

These follow the order that utility responders and HVAC technicians typically find gas odors at a furnace.

1. **Loose or leaking fitting at the furnace gas connection.** This includes the union, the sediment trap (drip leg), the manual shutoff valve, or the black-iron joints near the furnace. It is the most common source, especially after installation or other work.
2. **Corroded, kinked or old flexible gas connector.** Older uncoated brass connectors are a known hazard and should be replaced, not repaired.
3. **Leak at the gas valve body or its inlet and outlet fittings.** This covers the White-Rodgers or Honeywell valve on the burner manifold. Valve make varies by model and production year.
4. **Delayed or rough ignition.** A brief whiff at startup with a "whoosh" can mean gas built up before lighting. Possible reasons include a dirty burner, a weak igniter, or a manifold pressure problem. See Loud bang on startup.
5. **Leak elsewhere on the house gas line or another appliance.** The water heater, range or dryer can be the real source, and the smell just collects in the furnace room.
6. **Propane tank running low (propane homes).** Odor can be stronger as the tank nears empty. Have the supplier check the system.
7. **Combustion or venting fault.** A cracked heat exchanger, blocked flue or disconnected vent pipe usually produces exhaust or "burning" odors rather than rotten egg. It is still a serious CO hazard that needs a technician right away.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

With a gas emergency, **calling comes first**. Only do the following once the utility or fire department has cleared the home, or if the odor is very faint and you are already safely outside:

- **Evacuate and call.** Use your utility's 24/7 gas emergency line or 911, from outside the house. Utility checks are usually free.
- **Do not operate anything inside.** That means no thermostat changes, no breaker resets, and no furnace restarts while gas may be present.
- **Check your detectors after clearance.** Make sure CO alarms on every level work and have fresh batteries. Replace any alarm past its expiration date, usually 7 to 10 years.
- **Look at outdoor vents (from outside only).** For high-efficiency models (SLP98, EL296, ML195 and similar), check the PVC intake and exhaust pipe terminations. If snow, leaves or nests block them, tell the technician. Do not clear them until the gas issue is resolved.
- **Do not relight or reset the furnace yourself.** After a gas leak shutoff, the utility or a licensed technician must restore gas and relight appliances.

There are no filter, thermostat or condensate fixes for a gas smell. Do not open the furnace cabinet.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing these steps helps you judge whether a quote is reasonable.

1. **Ambient gas reading.** The tech walks the area with an electronic combustible-gas detector to confirm gas is present and locate where it is strongest.
2. **Leak pinpointing.** They apply bubble solution to every joint from the shutoff valve to the gas valve, including the union, drip leg, flex connector and valve fittings.
3. **Pressure test.** They may test the house piping (pressure or "drop" test) with appliances isolated. Many jurisdictions require this before gas can be restored.
4. **Gas valve inspection.** They check the valve body for external leakage. They also measure inlet and manifold pressure against the furnace rating plate and the Lennox installation manual.
5. **Ignition sequence observation.** They watch the igniter, flame sensing and flame carryover across the burners to rule out delayed ignition.
6. **Combustion and venting analysis.** They test flue gas for CO, check for draft or vent leaks, and inspect the heat exchanger. A camera may be used if a crack is suspected.
7. **Clearance and documentation.** They repair or replace the faulty part, re-test for leaks, relight, and document the result. A utility red tag may need formal clearance.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong rotten-egg smell near furnace | Leaking fitting, union or flex connector | Evacuate, then call the utility or 911 from outside | Leak-test joints, re-make fittings or replace connector, pressure-test |
| Smell only near gas valve area | Gas valve body or fitting leak | Evacuate, then call the utility | Confirm leak, replace gas valve, check pressures |
| Brief whiff plus whoosh or bang at startup | Delayed ignition from dirty burners, weak igniter or pressure problem | Stop using the furnace and call a technician | Clean burners, test igniter and flame sensor, set manifold pressure |
| Smell throughout house, not just furnace room | House piping or another appliance leaking | Evacuate, then call the utility | Utility locates the leak, plumber or HVAC tech repairs, pressure test |
| Faint odor on a propane system | Low tank or a leak | Leave the house and call your propane supplier | Supplier leak-checks and refills, technician checks appliances |
| Exhaust or burning smell, headache, CO alarm | Vent blockage, disconnected flue or cracked heat exchanger | Get out, call 911, and keep the furnace off | Combustion test, vent repair, heat exchanger inspection or replacement |
| Utility tagged the furnace off | Leak confirmed at the appliance | Do not relight; schedule a licensed technician | Repair, re-test and clear the tag per local code |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Utility emergency leak check | $0 (most utilities) |
| HVAC emergency diagnostic or after-hours call | $100–$350 |
| Tighten or re-make leaking fitting or union | $100–$250 |
| Replace flexible gas connector | $150–$350 |
| Replace manual gas shutoff valve | $150–$400 |
| Replace furnace gas valve | $400–$900 (part may be covered under Lennox warranty) |
| Burner cleaning and ignition tune-up (delayed ignition) | $150–$400 |
| House gas line leak repair (plumber) | $200–$1,000+ depending on access |
| Pressure test for code clearance | $75–$300 |
| Vent or flue repair | $150–$800 |
| Heat exchanger replacement | $1,500–$3,500+, often leading to furnace replacement ($4,000–$9,000 installed) |

## What this symptom means

"Loud noise" is not a numbered Carrier fault code. It is a symptom, so most Carrier air conditioners will show no code or flashing light for it. On Infinity systems, a related fault may appear on the wall control if the noise comes with a motor or compressor problem.

The noise comes from one of three places:

- **The outdoor condenser:** compressor, condenser fan motor and blade, contactor, capacitor, or loose panels.
- **The indoor blower section:** furnace or fan coil.
- **The ductwork.**

The type of sound is the best clue:

- **Grinding or screeching:** usually motor bearings.
- **Loud humming with nothing turning:** usually a capacitor or a seized motor.
- **Rattling or buzzing:** usually loose parts or a contactor.
- **Banging or clanking:** usually a loose or broken internal part.
- **Hissing or screaming:** can mean a refrigerant leak or very high pressure.

The noise does not "reset." Restarting the system will not fix the underlying cause.

This page covers the AC's outdoor unit and the AC-related indoor noises. For a reversing-valve whoosh or defrost noises, see the heat pump page instead.

**If you hear grinding, screaming, banging, or a loud hum with the fan stalled:**

1. Turn the system off at the thermostat.
2. Leave it off until a technician inspects it.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failing condenser fan motor bearings.** These cause squealing, grinding, or a growl that changes as the fan spins. They are common on units older than about 8–10 years.
2. **Weak or failed run capacitor (fan or compressor).** This causes a loud hum or buzz. The fan may struggle to start or not start, and the compressor may hum without starting.
3. **Pitted or chattering contactor.** This causes a loud buzz or rapid chattering from the outdoor unit's electrical compartment. It can also be caused by low control voltage.
4. **Loose, bent, or debris-struck fan blade.** This causes rattling, ticking, or a rhythmic thump. Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade striking the fan guard are typical.
5. **Loose cabinet panels, fasteners, or vibrating refrigerant lines.** This causes rattling or buzzing. It is often worse at startup and shutdown.
6. **Indoor blower problems.** These include a loose or unbalanced blower wheel, worn blower motor bearings, or debris in the wheel. They cause noise at the indoor unit when the AC runs.
7. **Compressor problems.** These cause loud clanking, knocking, or rattling inside the compressor shell, or a hard-start "thunk." Possible causes include broken internal mounts, worn components, or liquid slugging. Scroll compressors in many Carrier units can also briefly run backward and make a rattle on shutdown. That is normal if it is brief. Persistent noise is not.
8. **Refrigerant-side issues.** These cause hissing, gurgling, or a high-pitched scream. A loud scream from a scroll compressor can indicate very high pressure or a fault, and it needs immediate shutdown.
9. **Duct noise.** This includes booming, popping, or whistling from ducts or registers. Common causes are restricted airflow, a dirty filter, closed registers, or undersized returns.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Note the sound and where it comes from.** Record whether it is indoors or outdoors, and whether it happens at startup, while running, or at shutdown. A short phone video helps the technician a lot.
- **Shut it off if the noise is severe.** For grinding, screaming, banging, or a hum with the fan not spinning, set the thermostat to OFF and leave it off.
- **Check the air filter.** A clogged filter can cause whistling, duct booming, and blower strain. Replace it if it is dirty.
- **Open supply and return registers.** Make sure they are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs. This reduces whistling and duct noise.
- **Clear around the outdoor unit from the outside only.** Remove leaves, sticks, and debris around the base and top grille with the system OFF. Keep about 2 feet of clearance. Do not reach into the fan or remove the top.
- **Check the exterior panels.** If an outer access panel is visibly loose, make sure it is seated and its exterior screws are snug. Do not open the electrical compartment.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it is not rapidly cycling the system. Replace the batteries if the display is weak.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A gurgling sound near the indoor unit can come from a slow condensate drain. Make sure the line is flowing and the drain pan is not overflowing.
- **Check the breaker if the unit is off.** If the unit tripped its breaker, you may reset it ONCE. If it trips again or the noise returns, stop and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A Carrier-trained technician will usually take these steps:

1. **Listen and isolate.** They run the system and identify whether the noise is from the compressor, the fan, the contactor, the indoor blower, or the ductwork. They may run components separately.
2. **Shut off power at the disconnect and inspect the condenser fan.** They check for blade damage, wobble, and loose set screws. They spin the motor by hand to check for bearing drag.
3. **Test the run capacitor(s).** They measure microfarads against the rating printed on the capacitor. A dual capacitor serves both the fan and the compressor on many models.
4. **Inspect the contactor.** They look for pitting and chatter, and check the 24V control voltage.
5. **Measure compressor amperage.** They compare it to the rated load amps (RLA) on the data plate. On a hard start, they check locked rotor amps (LRA). They check winding resistance if the compressor fails to start.
6. **Check refrigerant pressures, superheat, and subcooling.** This rules out overcharge, restriction, or non-condensables causing compressor strain or noise.
7. **Inspect the indoor blower wheel, motor mounts, and bearings.** On ECM motors, they check for fault indications.
8. **Check fault history on Infinity or variable-speed models.** The control may log compressor or motor faults that help confirm the cause.
9. **Check airflow and static pressure** if the noise comes from the ducts.

A good quote should name the specific failed part and explain how it was confirmed. Examples are a capacitor reading below its rating, or a motor that drags when turned by hand. A vague "compressor is going" with no amp or pressure readings is a red flag. Ask for the readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squeal or grinding from outdoor unit | Worn condenser fan motor bearings | Turn system off and schedule service | Replace condenser fan motor (and capacitor if weak) |
| Loud hum, fan not spinning | Failed run capacitor or seized fan motor | Shut off immediately; do not let it hum | Test and replace capacitor or motor |
| Buzzing or chattering at outdoor unit | Pitted contactor or low control voltage | Note when it happens; call pro | Replace contactor and diagnose the control circuit |
| Rattling or ticking | Debris, bent blade, loose panel or screws | Clear debris from outside with power off; seat exterior panels | Replace or balance blade; secure internal parts and lines |
| Metallic clanking or knocking inside unit | Compressor internal damage or broken mounts | Shut off and call pro | Diagnose compressor; repair mounts or replace compressor |
| High-pitched scream or hiss | Very high pressure, refrigerant leak, or compressor fault | Shut off immediately | Pressure test, leak search, repair |
| Brief rattle right at shutdown | Scroll compressor briefly reversing (often normal) | Monitor; call if it is prolonged | Check for a faulty check valve or compressor issue if persistent |
| Whistling or booming from ducts or registers | Dirty filter, closed registers, restricted returns | Replace filter; open registers | Static pressure test; duct or return modifications |
| Thumping or scraping from indoor unit | Loose or unbalanced blower wheel, worn blower motor | Turn off and call pro | Tighten or replace blower wheel or motor |
| Gurgling near indoor unit | Slow condensate drain | Clear and check the condensate line | Clean drain and trap; check pan |

## Repair costs

Typical US price ranges, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service or diagnostic call | $80–$200 |
| Tightening panels, lines, or fan blade; clearing debris (tech visit) | $100–$250 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $120–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Condenser fan blade replacement | $150–$350 |
| Condenser fan motor replacement | $350–$800 (variable-speed motors on some Infinity units cost more) |
| Indoor blower wheel repair or replacement | $200–$600 |
| Indoor blower motor replacement | $400–$1,200 (ECM or variable-speed models are at the high end) |
| Compressor sound blanket | $100–$300 |
| Refrigerant leak search and repair | $250–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type) |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800–$4,000+ |

**Compressor replacement:** Warranty parts can lower this cost. Labor and refrigerant are usually extra. On older R-22 units, replacing the whole system is often the better value.

**Full condenser replacement:** $3,500–$8,000+, depending on size and series.

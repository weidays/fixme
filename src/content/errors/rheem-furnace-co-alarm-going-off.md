## What this code means

> **Act first: this is an emergency.**
> 1. Get everyone, including pets, outside into fresh air right away. Leave doors open on your way out if it takes no extra time.
> 2. Call **911** or your local fire department from outside. Many gas utilities also send a crew for CO calls, so you can call your gas utility's emergency line too.
> 3. Get medical help for anyone with headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion or drowsiness. Tell responders a CO alarm went off.
> 4. Do not go back inside until responders say the air is safe. Do not run the furnace again until a licensed HVAC technician has found and fixed the cause.

"CO alarm going off" is not a Rheem diagnostic code. It means a carbon monoxide alarm in your home detected a dangerous level of CO.

A Rheem gas furnace can produce CO in two situations:
- It burns fuel incompletely.
- Its exhaust escapes into the living space instead of going out through the vent.

The furnace control board has **no CO sensor**. Its status LED may look completely normal, so you cannot clear or confirm this problem at the furnace.

Some of the same faults can also trip safety devices:
- A blocked flue can open the pressure switch.
- Exhaust spilling back can trip the flame rollout switch.

Those switches are not CO detectors. A furnace can make CO without tripping either one.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

Rheem does not publish a manufacturer troubleshooting order for CO alarms. The ranking below reflects how often each cause turns up in the field for Rheem and Ruud gas furnaces.

1. **A non-furnace source, or a false or faulty alarm.** A water heater, range, fireplace, attached garage, or generator is often the real source. An alarm at the end of its life can also misbehave. Responders confirm this with their own meter.
2. **Blocked, disconnected or damaged venting.**
   - On 90%+ condensing models, snow, ice, leaves or nests can block the outdoor PVC intake or exhaust terminations, and pipe joints can separate.
   - On 80% models, the metal flue can come loose, corrode, or be blocked at the chimney. A shared chimney liner can also be undersized, especially after the water heater was replaced.
3. **Backdrafting or spillage on 80% atmospheric-vent installs.** Strong exhaust fans, a tight house, or a blocked chimney can pull flue gas back into the room.
4. **Poor combustion.** This includes dirty or misaligned burners, wrong gas pressure (over-firing or under-firing), or a wrong orifice or missing propane conversion. These faults raise CO in the flue gas.
5. **Inducer motor or combustion-air problems.** A weak or failing inducer can fail to fully clear the exhaust.
6. **Cracked or failed heat exchanger.** This is less common than venting faults, but it is serious. It is more likely on older units and on furnaces that have overheated repeatedly from restricted airflow.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these **only after** responders have cleared the house and the furnace is off. None of them replace a technician visit.

- **Leave the furnace off.** Set the thermostat to OFF. Do not do a "test reset" to see whether the alarm comes back.
- **Read the alarm.** Note the beep pattern and any display reading (ppm or peak level) for the technician. If the date label shows the alarm is past end of life, replace it. Do this in addition to the service call, not instead of it.
- **Check the alarm's batteries.** Battery problems explain chirping, but not a full alarm.
- **Look at the outdoor vent terminations from outside, without opening anything.** On 90%+ models, gently clear snow, ice or leaves from around the PVC intake and exhaust pipes where they exit the wall or roof, if you can reach them safely from the ground. Report what you found. Do not restart the furnace yourself even if you found a blockage.
- **Look at the air filter.** Replace it if it is clogged. A clogged filter is not a direct CO cause, but it contributes to heat exchanger overheating over time.

Do not open the furnace cabinet. Do not disconnect vent pipes, and do not try to inspect the heat exchanger or burners yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A proper CO call should include the following steps. Use them to sanity-check what you are told.

- **Ambient CO readings** throughout the house with a calibrated meter, near every combustion appliance.
- **Combustion analysis** with a flue-gas analyzer at the furnace test port. This measures CO (air-free), O2 and flue temperature and compares them with Rheem's specifications.
- **Full vent inspection.** For 80% models this covers connector joints, slope, and the chimney or liner, including sizing with the water heater. For 90%+ models it covers PVC joints, terminations and clearances.
- **Draft and spillage test** on atmospheric-vent installs, run with exhaust fans and dryers on to simulate the worst case.
- **Gas pressure check**, measuring inlet and manifold pressure against the rating plate, plus a check of orifices and fuel type.
- **Burner and inducer inspection**, including inducer operation and pressure switch readings.
- **Heat exchanger evaluation**, using visual or camera inspection, and possibly flame-disturbance and rising-CO tests when the blower starts.

Be cautious about a "cracked heat exchanger, you need a new furnace" verdict that comes with no photos, no combustion readings and no vent inspection. Ask for evidence, and get a second opinion if needed.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full alarm, people feel unwell | Active CO leak from any source | Evacuate, call 911, seek medical care | Locate source, repair, verify with combustion analysis |
| Alarm during or after snowstorm, 90%+ furnace | Blocked intake or exhaust termination | From outside, clear snow around pipes; keep furnace off | Inspect venting, confirm proper draft, restart and test |
| Alarm near furnace/water heater, 80% furnace | Disconnected flue, blocked chimney, backdrafting | Keep furnace off; report what you saw | Repair connector or liner, run spillage and draft tests |
| Alarm when blower starts | Possible cracked heat exchanger | Keep furnace off | Heat exchanger inspection, CO rise test, warranty claim |
| Sooty marks or scorching near burners | Poor combustion or rollout | Keep furnace off | Clean and adjust burners, check gas pressure and orifices |
| Single chirp every minute | Low battery or alarm at end of life | Replace batteries or the alarm | None, unless alarm readings showed CO |
| Alarm only with car or generator running | Non-furnace source | Stop the source, ventilate | Optional whole-home CO check |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges. Prices vary by region, and emergency or after-hours visits add cost.

| Item | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Fire department response | Usually free |
| CO diagnostic or combustion analysis visit | $100 – $300 |
| New CO alarm (DIY) | $25 – $60 each |
| Reconnect or reseal vent pipe | $150 – $400 |
| Replace venting run or termination kit | $300 – $1,000 |
| Chimney liner (often driven by water heater sizing) | $1,000 – $3,000 |
| Burner cleaning and gas pressure adjustment | $150 – $350 |
| Inducer motor replacement | $400 – $900 |
| Heat exchanger replacement (parts often under warranty) | $1,500 – $3,500, mostly labor |
| Full furnace replacement | $3,500 – $7,500+ |

On older units, replacing the whole furnace is often more sensible than replacing the heat exchanger. This is especially true where labor is not covered by warranty.

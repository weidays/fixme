## What this code means

"Leaking water" is not a flash-code fault—your York furnace does not have an LED code for a water leak. Instead, you're seeing physical water pooling under or around the cabinet. On high-efficiency **condensing** York furnaces (90%+ AFUE, with white PVC intake/exhaust pipes), this is almost always a **condensate drainage problem**.

Condensing furnaces extract extra heat from exhaust gases, which cools them enough to form liquid water (condensate). A healthy unit typically produces a gallon or more a day in cold weather—more during heavy runtime, less in mild weather—and drains it through a trap and PVC piping to a floor drain, pump, or exterior. When that path is blocked, cracked, or disconnected, the water backs up and overflows.

Water is dangerous around a furnace: it can short the control board, corrode the inducer motor, and trigger pressure-switch lockouts. Because confirming the source and repairing internal components is technician work, this is rated **pro severity**.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged condensate drain line or trap** — the most common cause. Algae, dust, and sediment block the trap or PVC tubing, so water backs up and overflows the inducer, pressure switch, or collector box.
2. **Full or stuck condensate pump** — if your furnace drains to a pump (no gravity floor drain), a failed float switch or clogged pump lets water overflow.
3. **Cracked or disconnected condensate trap/tubing** — the plastic trap can crack with age or freeze; rubber hoses pop off their barbs.
4. **Blocked or improperly sloped PVC flue/exhaust** — a sagging or blocked exhaust pipe traps condensate that then leaks back into the cabinet.
5. **Clogged air-conditioning coil drain (cooling season)** — if an A-coil sits on top of the furnace, its drain pan can overflow and masquerade as a furnace leak.
6. **Cracked secondary heat exchanger** — the worst case. The condensing heat exchanger corrodes and leaks internally. This is rare, expensive, and may warrant furnace replacement.

Note: 80% (non-condensing) York furnaces rarely leak water on their own—if one is leaking, suspect the attached AC coil or a flue/chimney condensation issue.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do:

- **Set the thermostat to OFF** and cut power at the furnace switch to stop more water from accumulating near electrical parts.
- **Clean up standing water** with a wet/dry vacuum or towels so it doesn't reach the control board or wiring.
- **Check (and replace) the air filter.** A clogged filter can cause AC coil freeze-ups that later drip as water—a dirty filter is cheap to rule out.
- **Look at the condensate drain line's exit point** (floor drain or outdoor termination). If you can see an obvious external blockage (debris, ice at the outlet), note it for the technician.
- **Check the condensate pump reservoir** if visible—if it's full to the brim, the pump has failed.
- **If the furnace switch or breaker has already tripped, leave it off.** Water that reaches furnace wiring, the control board, or the blower can trip the breaker, and re-energizing wet electrical components risks shock, board damage, and fire. Do not reset it—call a technician and let them confirm the equipment is dry and safe first.

Do **not** open the furnace cabinet, remove the trap, probe internal tubing, or run the unit while water is pooling.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. Confirm whether water originates from the furnace or the AC coil above it (season and drip pattern are big clues).
2. Inspect and clear the condensate trap and PVC drain line, often flushing with water or vacuuming the blockage.
3. Test the condensate pump and float switch.
4. Check the trap and all rubber tubing for cracks, splits, or loose connections.
5. Verify the PVC exhaust/intake slope and look for sags or blockages trapping condensate.
6. Inspect the collector box and inducer housing for leaks.
7. If all drainage is clear but water persists, perform a combustion/heat-exchanger inspection for a cracked secondary heat exchanger—this requires disassembly and is the final, most serious check.

Expect a diagnostic fee of roughly $100–$200, often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water pooling under cabinet in heating season | Clogged condensate drain or trap | Shut off power, vacuum water, replace filter | Clear/flush trap and PVC drain line |
| Reservoir full, water overflowing near pump | Failed condensate pump | Check pump isn't unplugged | Test float switch, replace pump |
| Water only when AC runs | AC coil drain pan overflow | Replace filter, check drain exit | Clear coil drain, flush pan |
| Drip near collector box / inducer | Cracked trap or loose tubing | Cut power, clean up water | Replace trap/hoses, reseal |
| Water plus pressure-switch lockout codes | Blocked/sagging PVC exhaust | Note external vent blockage | Re-slope or clear flue piping |
| Breaker or furnace switch tripped with water present | Water reaching wiring or control board | Leave power off—do not reset | Dry out, test and repair electrical components |
| Persistent leak, drainage all clear | Cracked secondary heat exchanger | None—stop using furnace | Inspect/replace exchanger or furnace |

## Repair costs

- **Clear clogged drain/trap:** $0 DIY (if external) – $200 professional
- **Replace condensate trap or tubing:** $150 – $300
- **Replace condensate pump:** $200 – $450 installed
- **Clear/repair AC coil drain pan:** $150 – $400
- **Re-slope or repair PVC flue venting:** $200 – $500
- **Cracked secondary heat exchanger:** $1,200 – $3,000+. The part itself is often covered by warranty, but labor is the cost driver—the furnace has to be largely torn down to reach the condensing heat exchanger. On an older furnace, many homeowners are quoted full replacement instead, which is frequently the better value.

Air filters cost $10–$30; condensate drain tablets run $10–$20 and help prevent future clogs.

## What this code means

"Leaking water" is a symptom, not a fault code. Carrier air conditioners do not show a dedicated error code for a water leak. When the system cools, the indoor evaporator coil pulls moisture out of the air. That water collects in a drain pan under the coil and flows out through a condensate line, sometimes with help from a condensate pump.

A leak means water is escaping that path. Some installations have a float or overflow safety switch in the pan, drain line or pump. When it trips, it shuts the cooling off, usually by cutting the thermostat's control circuit. The system may then look dead, or the thermostat may show cooling while nothing runs.

These switches typically reset on their own once the water level drops. Some pumps and float switches have a manual reset, so check the device label.

This page covers the indoor unit in cooling mode. If the water appears in heating season from a high-efficiency furnace, see Carrier Furnace Leaking Water.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged condensate drain line.** Algae, sludge, dust or insects block the line, so the pan overflows. This is by far the most common cause, especially early in cooling season.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil melting.** Low airflow or low refrigerant causes ice to form on the coil. When the ice melts, it releases more water than the pan can handle, or it drips outside the pan.
   - Low airflow usually comes from a dirty filter or blocked returns.
   - Signs include ice on the copper line or reduced cooling.
3. **Failed or unplugged condensate pump.** This applies to attic, closet or basement installs without gravity drainage. The pump motor fails, its float sticks, or the pump is unplugged.
4. **Disconnected, sagging or improperly pitched drain line.** Fittings separate at the pan or trap. A line without enough slope, or a missing or dry trap on a draw-through air handler, can hold water or pull it back into the cabinet.
5. **Cracked or corroded drain pan.** Older steel pans rust through, and plastic pans can crack. Water then drips into the furnace or onto the floor. This is common in units more than 10 to 15 years old.
6. **Clogged secondary or overflow drain, or an unprotected attic pan.** The backup line or auxiliary pan is blocked, so the overflow goes into the ceiling.
7. **Sweating ducts, plenum or refrigerant suction line.** Missing or damaged insulation lets condensation drip in humid spaces. This can look like a unit leak.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Turn the system off at the thermostat** to stop making more water. Protect floors and ceilings with towels and buckets.
2. **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it is dirty. A clogged filter is a leading cause of coil freezing.
   - If you see ice on the coil or the refrigerant line, leave the system off.
   - You can run the fan only (Fan ON, cooling off) for a few hours to melt the ice.
   - Collect the melt water while it drains.
3. **Look at the visible drain line and its outlet.** Find where the line ends, which may be outside near the foundation, at a floor drain or utility sink, or at a pump.
   - If the outlet is dry while the unit was running, the line is likely clogged.
   - From the outdoor end, you can use a wet/dry vacuum to pull out the clog. Seal the hose around the pipe and run it for one to two minutes.
4. **Use the condensate access port, if one is installed.** Many installers fit a capped tee or vent on the line near the indoor unit.
   - You can remove the cap and slowly pour about a cup of plain water, or a cup of distilled white vinegar, to flush the line.
   - Do not use bleach if your drain has PVC cement joints you are unsure of. Never mix cleaners.
5. **Check the condensate pump, if you have one.** Make sure it is plugged in and that its outlet is powered.
   - Look for water standing in the pump reservoir. A full reservoir with a silent pump means the pump needs service.
   - Do not open the pump's wiring.
6. **Check the attic or auxiliary pan.** If the pan under an attic unit has water in it, the primary drain has failed. Do not run cooling until it is fixed.
7. **Look at visible vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return grilles are open and unblocked. This prevents low airflow and coil freezing.
8. **Turn cooling back on and watch.** If the system won't start, a float switch may still be tripped. Give it time to drain.
   - If it still won't start, or the leak returns within a day, call a technician.
   - Do not remove panels from the indoor unit or bypass any float switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- **Check the drain system.** The tech traces it from the pan to the outlet, then clears the line with nitrogen, a pump or a drain auger. They should also verify the trap and the slope of the line, which should be about 1/4 inch per foot.
- **Inspect the pan and coil.** With the cabinet opened, they look for cracks, rust-through and debris, and check for water inside the furnace or air handler.
- **Test the float and overflow switches.** They confirm the switches are wired to interrupt cooling and that they reset properly.
- **Test the condensate pump.** This covers pump operation, the float, the check valve and the discharge tubing.
- **Diagnose coil freezing, if ice was present.** They measure airflow and static pressure, check blower speed settings, and measure refrigerant pressures, superheat and subcooling.
  - Low refrigerant means a leak that must be found and repaired, not just topped off.
- **Check insulation.** They look at the suction line and duct insulation to rule out sweating.

A fair quote names the specific cause. Be cautious if you are told to replace the whole system for a clogged drain. Also be cautious of a refrigerant top-off with no leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water pooling at the indoor unit, drain outlet dry | Clogged condensate line | Vacuum the line from the outlet; flush through the access port | Clear the line, clean the trap and pan, add a cleanout or float switch |
| AC won't start, water in the pan or pump | Float or overflow switch tripped | Clear the drain, wait for the water level to drop | Clear the drain, test and reposition the switch |
| Ice on the coil or line, heavy dripping as it melts | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace the filter, open the vents, thaw with the fan only | Check airflow and blower; find and repair any refrigerant leak |
| Pump reservoir full, pump silent | Failed or unpowered condensate pump | Confirm it is plugged in and the outlet works | Replace the pump or its float and check valve |
| Water leaking inside the furnace below the coil | Cracked or rusted drain pan, or a disconnected fitting | Turn the system off, protect the area | Reconnect the fitting, or replace the pan or coil |
| Ceiling stain under an attic unit | Primary drain failed, overflow pan full or clogged | Turn the system off, empty or vacuum the pan | Clear the drains, install an overflow pan switch |
| Drips along the ducts or refrigerant line | Condensation on uninsulated surfaces | Note where the drips are | Repair or replace the insulation |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, 2026:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| DIY drain clearing (shop vac, vinegar) | $0–$30 |
| Professional drain line clearing or service call | $100–$250 |
| Float or overflow safety switch installed | $100–$250 |
| Drain line repair or re-pitch, trap correction | $150–$400 |
| Condensate pump replacement | $150–$400 |
| Auxiliary or secondary drain pan replacement | $200–$500 |
| Insulation repair on the suction line or ducts | $100–$400 |
| Refrigerant leak search and repair plus recharge (frozen coil cause) | $300–$1,500+ |
| Evaporator coil replacement (cracked integral pan) | $1,000–$2,500+ |

Your coil may still be under Carrier's parts warranty, typically 5 to 10 years with registration. Labor is usually not covered.

## What this code means

"Breaker keeps tripping" is a symptom, not a stored fault code. Your electrical panel is shutting off power because the heat pump, or part of it, is drawing more current than the circuit allows. The cause is either a short to ground or an overload.

A heat pump differs from a cooling-only AC in an important way. A Bryant heat pump system usually has two or more separate circuits:

- **Outdoor unit circuit.** This feeds the compressor, outdoor fan and reversing valve. It is usually a 2-pole breaker sized to the outdoor unit's nameplate MOCP.
- **Indoor air handler / fan coil circuit(s).** This feeds the blower and the electric heat strips (auxiliary and emergency heat). Larger heater kits often use two breakers.

The first thing to note is **which breaker trips and when**. That single detail points a technician to the right component.

A tripped breaker does not reset itself. It stays off until someone resets it by hand. Reset it **once only**. If it trips again, the fault is still there.

The cooling-season version of this problem is covered on our Bryant AC Breaker Keeps Tripping page. This page focuses on heat-pump-specific causes: heat strips, defrost and the year-round load.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

The ranking below applies to conventional single-stage and two-stage outdoor units, which use a run capacitor and a contactor. Variable-speed (inverter) units are different; see the next section.

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor (outdoor unit).** The compressor or fan motor struggles to start and pulls locked-rotor current. This is one of the most common failures on any split system. Note that a failing capacitor more often trips the compressor's internal overload, so the unit shuts down and restarts later, than the panel breaker. When it does trip the breaker, the trip often comes a few seconds after the outdoor unit tries to start, usually with a hum.
2. **Electric heat strip problems (indoor unit).** These include a shorted or grounded heater element, a failed sequencer or relay that holds strips on, or overheated or loose lugs on the heater kit. The trip happens only when aux, emergency or defrost heat is called. This cause is heat-pump-specific.
3. **Compressor electrical fault.** A grounded or shorted winding, or a mechanically locked compressor, often trips the breaker instantly at startup. A grounded compressor is a classic cause of an instant breaker trip. This is the most expensive outcome.
4. **Loose, corroded or damaged wiring.** Common spots are the disconnect, contactor, heater kit terminals, or wiring chewed by rodents. Corroded disconnects are common on outdoor units exposed to winter moisture and salt. Like a grounded compressor, a wiring short to ground is a classic cause of an instant trip.
5. **Pitted or failed contactor.** Chattering or welded contacts cause arcing and current spikes.
6. **Outdoor fan motor shorted or seized.** It can trip the breaker alone, or it can overload the compressor by raising head pressure.
7. **Severe airflow or coil problems causing overload.** A badly clogged filter, a blocked or iced outdoor coil, or a defrost malfunction raise pressures and amp draw. These usually trip the compressor's internal overload first, but they can push a marginal breaker over the edge.
8. **Breaker or circuit problem.** This includes a worn breaker, an undersized breaker, or a loose breaker connection at the panel bus, especially after a new heater kit or unit was installed.

Bryant does not publish a single numbered troubleshooting sequence for this symptom. The order above reflects general field experience. Many technicians check the capacitor and contactor early because they fail often and are quick to test, then move on to motors and wiring. If the breaker trips instantly with no hum, a grounded compressor or a wiring fault moves to the top of the list.

## If you have a variable-speed (inverter) Evolution heat pump

Bryant's variable-speed Evolution heat pumps generally have no conventional run capacitor or line-voltage contactor driving the compressor. The capacitor and contactor causes above, and the matching rows in the tables below, usually do not apply to these units.

On inverter units, breaker-trip causes center on:

- **The inverter drive and control.** This includes shorted drive components, a failed input filter, or a failed drive board.
- **The inverter compressor itself.**

Heat strip problems, wiring and disconnect faults, and worn or undersized breakers can still cause trips on these systems, because the indoor heater kit and the supply wiring work the same way.

Diagnosis on an inverter unit centers on the drive board and the compressor and follows Bryant's service literature for that model. It is technician-only work. The drive can hold stored electrical energy even after the power is off, so never open the cabinet. Repairs are typically more expensive than the capacitor and contactor jobs listed in the cost table below, which reflects conventional units. Ask your technician for a written diagnosis and check your warranty registration before approving a drive or compressor repair.

The safe homeowner checks in the next section are the same for every Bryant heat pump.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Note which breaker trips.** Is it the outdoor unit breaker, or the air handler or heat strip breaker? Labels in the panel help. Write this down for the technician.
- **Note when it trips.** It could be at startup, after minutes of running, only in heat mode, only during defrost, or only when "Aux" or "Em Heat" shows on the thermostat.
- **Reset the breaker once.** Turn it fully OFF, then ON. If it trips again immediately or within minutes, leave it off. Do not reset it repeatedly.
- **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it is dirty. A clogged filter raises amp draw and heat strip temperatures.
- **Check thermostat settings.** Turn emergency heat off unless you need it. This keeps the heat strips from running continuously while you wait for a technician.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from outside the cabinet.** Clear snow, ice buildup, leaves or debris from around the coil so air can flow. Do not chip ice off the coil fins.
- **Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked.**
- **Confirm the exterior access panels are seated and secured.** Do not open the electrical compartment.

Stop and call a technician if you smell burning, see scorch marks, or hear buzzing from the disconnect or panel.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech working on a conventional single-stage or two-stage unit will typically:

1. **Ask which breaker and when.** This separates outdoor unit faults from heat strip faults.
2. **Inspect the panel and disconnect.** They look for heat damage, loose lugs and corrosion, and verify the breaker size against the nameplate MOCP for both units.
3. **Megohm or ohm-test the compressor windings to ground** with power off, and check common-run-start resistances. A grounded compressor is a definitive finding.
4. **Test the run capacitor(s)** with a capacitance meter against the rated µF.
5. **Inspect the contactor** for pitting and welding, and check coil operation.
6. **Test the outdoor fan motor** for shorts and free rotation.
7. **Check the heater kit.** This includes element resistance, element-to-ground insulation, sequencer and relay operation, and limit switches. They measure strip amp draw during a heat call.
8. **Measure running amps** on the compressor, fan and strips against nameplate RLA/FLA.
9. **Verify defrost operation** and outdoor coil condition if trips occur in cold weather.

On a variable-speed unit, steps 4 and 5 are replaced by diagnosis of the inverter drive and its components, as described in the section above.

If someone quotes a new compressor or system without documenting a winding or ground test, ask for the readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

These rows assume a conventional single-stage or two-stage outdoor unit. Rows that mention a capacitor or contactor generally do not apply to variable-speed units.

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor breaker trips within seconds of startup, unit hums | Failed run capacitor or locked compressor | Reset once; if it repeats, leave it off | Test and replace the capacitor; test the compressor for locked rotor |
| Outdoor unit hums, shuts off, restarts later without tripping the breaker | Weak capacitor tripping the compressor's internal overload | Note the pattern; call a tech | Test the capacitor and compressor |
| Outdoor breaker trips instantly, no hum | Grounded compressor or shorted wiring | Leave the breaker off | Megohm-test the compressor and inspect wiring and the disconnect |
| Indoor or heat strip breaker trips only on aux, emergency or defrost heat | Shorted heater element, failed sequencer, loose lug | Turn off emergency heat, replace the filter, call a tech | Test the elements and sequencers; repair lugs or the heater kit |
| Trips after running a while in cold weather | Iced coil or defrost failure raising amp draw | Clear snow and debris from around the unit | Diagnose defrost board, sensor and reversing valve; check amps |
| Trips randomly, buzzing or burnt smell at disconnect | Loose or corroded connections | Leave it off and call promptly | Replace the disconnect, lugs or wire |
| Contactor chatters, then breaker trips | Pitted contactor or low-voltage issue | None | Replace the contactor and check control voltage |
| Trips with a dirty filter, normal after a change | Airflow overload | Keep filters changed on schedule | Verify blower and amp draw if it recurs |
| Amps normal but breaker still trips | Worn or undersized breaker | None | Replace the breaker or correct its size |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, parts and labor, for conventional single-stage and two-stage systems. Variable-speed drive and inverter compressor repairs typically cost more and are not reflected here.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic or service call | $90–$200 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Disconnect box or whip replacement | $150–$400 |
| Wiring or lug repair | $100–$350 |
| Breaker replacement (HVAC tech or electrician) | $150–$350 |
| Heat strip sequencer or relay | $150–$400 |
| Heater element or full heater kit | $300–$900 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350–$800 |
| Compressor replacement (in warranty, labor and refrigerant only) | $1,200–$2,500 |
| Compressor replacement (out of warranty) | $1,800–$3,500+ |

Bryant's limited parts warranty (commonly 10 years with timely registration) may cover the compressor or motor part cost. Labor is usually extra. Check your registration status before approving a major repair.

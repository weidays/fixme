## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is not a numeric fault code — it is a physical symptom on your Carrier condenser (the unit outside). The fan on top of the outdoor unit pulls air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system pulls out of the house.

When it stops spinning, the compressor may still be running (you'll hear a hum or buzz), refrigerant pressure and temperature climb quickly, and the unit will either stop cooling or trip out on a high-pressure or overload safety. **This is a pro-level repair** because the most common culprits — capacitors and motors — carry stored electrical charge and involve 240V wiring.

On variable-speed **Infinity** systems, the outdoor fan is driven by a control board and ECM motor, and a fan that won't spin may throw an actual fault code on the wall control. Diagnosis on those units differs from the standard PSC motor described here.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor (and often the compressor) the jolt of torque to start. A bulged, leaking, or weak capacitor leaves the motor humming but not turning.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Bearings fail or windings burn open. The motor won't turn even with a good capacitor, and may be hot to the touch or smell scorched.
3. **Bad contactor** — The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit. Pitted or stuck contacts can leave the fan (and sometimes the compressor) without power.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse** — No power reaching the outdoor unit at all — nothing runs.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, ice, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Failed control board (ECM/Infinity units)** — On variable-speed systems the board commands the fan; a board or ECM fault stops it.
7. **Motor overheat / thermal cutout** — A motor that ran hot may shut itself off until it cools, then fail again shortly.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — everything else involves live 240V and stored charge.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and lower the setpoint well below room temperature so the system is actually calling for cooling. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A badly clogged filter can cause airflow faults and freeze-ups that lock the system out. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker in your main panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Confirm the pull-out disconnect box next to the condenser is fully seated (it can get bumped or left out after service).
- **Look, don't touch:** With the system off at the thermostat, look for obvious debris — leaves, a stick, ice — resting on or around the fan blade. Do not reach in, and do not spin the blade to "help" it while power is on.
- **Condensate:** Check that the indoor drain pan isn't full; a tripped float switch can shut the whole system down.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, shut the system off at the thermostat and call a technician. Do not repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. Confirm the thermostat is calling and 24V is reaching the contactor.
2. Cut power at the disconnect and **safely discharge the capacitor** before touching anything.
3. Test the run capacitor with a meter for microfarad (µF) value against the rating printed on it — a reading well below spec means replacement.
4. Check the contactor for pitted contacts and verify it pulls in and passes voltage.
5. Test the fan motor windings for continuity and correct resistance, and check the motor for a locked rotor.
6. Verify line voltage at the disconnect and inspect wiring for burned or loose terminals.
7. On Infinity/ECM units, read fault codes at the wall control and test the board's command signal to the motor.

Expect a diagnostic fee of roughly **$90–$180**, usually credited toward the repair. Ask which specific component tested bad — a good tech will show you the capacitor µF reading or the motor resistance.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan sits still | Failed run capacitor | None — 240V/stored charge | Discharge and replace capacitor, test µF |
| Fan spins if nudged, not on its own | Weak run capacitor | None | Replace capacitor |
| Nothing runs outdoors, indoor blower works | Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse | Reset breaker once; reseat disconnect | Find root cause of trip, replace fuse |
| Fan motor hot, smells burnt, won't turn | Burned-out fan motor | Shut system off | Replace condenser fan motor and capacitor |
| Buzzing at contactor, intermittent operation | Pitted/stuck contactor | None | Replace contactor |
| Blade physically blocked by debris | Debris/bent blade | Remove visible debris (power off) | Clear jam, straighten or replace blade |
| Wall control shows fan fault (Infinity) | Board or ECM motor fault | None | Diagnose board/ECM per Carrier service data |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$300
- **Condenser fan motor (PSC):** $300–$650
- **Fan blade:** $100–$250
- **ECM motor (Infinity/variable-speed):** $500–$1,000+
- **Control board (Infinity):** $400–$900
- **Tripped breaker / debris / reseated disconnect:** $0–$180 (often just the diagnostic fee)

Prices vary by region, unit age, and part availability. If a motor and capacitor are both original and 10+ years old, many techs replace them together since the failed part often takes out the other.

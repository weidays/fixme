## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a Carrier fault code — most residential Carrier condensers don't display error codes at the outdoor unit. The outdoor fan (condenser fan) sits on top of the outdoor unit and blows air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system pulled from inside your home.

When that fan stops turning while the compressor is running, refrigerant pressure and temperature climb rapidly. Carrier units protect themselves with a high-pressure switch or internal compressor overload, so you'll often see the system trip off, short-cycle, or stop cooling entirely.

This is a **pro-level** problem. The most common causes involve testing live high-voltage components and capacitors that can hold a dangerous charge even after power is removed. Do the safe checks below, then call a licensed HVAC tech.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** By far the most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start spinning. When it weakens or fails, the fan hums but won't turn — or only spins if nudged.
2. **Burned-out condenser fan motor.** Bearings seize or windings fail. The motor may be hot, silent, or tripping on its internal overload.
3. **Faulty contactor.** The relay that sends 240V to the fan and compressor can have burnt or pitted contacts that fail to close properly.
4. **Debris or ice jamming the blade.** Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
5. **Tripped high-pressure switch or overload.** The unit shut the fan/compressor down to protect itself, often as a *result* of another problem like a dirty coil.
6. **Loose or corroded wiring / low-voltage control fault.** Broken connections at the motor, capacitor, or control board.
7. **Failed control board (Infinity/Performance boards).** Less common; varies by model — some units drive the fan through the board rather than a simple contactor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical access panel on the outdoor unit** — the capacitor can hold a lethal charge.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause freeze-ups and pressure trips that shut the outdoor unit down. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor disconnect breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Visible debris:** With the unit **off at the breaker**, look through the top fan grille for obvious leaves, sticks, or ice on the blade. Clear anything loose from outside the grille only.
- **Indoor coil / condensate:** Check that the indoor coil isn't iced over and the condensate drain isn't backed up — both can trip protective controls.
- **Airflow around the unit:** Make sure nothing is blocking airflow within a couple feet of the outdoor unit.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, turn the system off and call a technician. Running it will risk the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence. Use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** with the system running and verifies whether the compressor is trying to start.
2. **Kills power and discharges the capacitor** before touching anything inside the electrical panel.
3. **Tests the run capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads (µF). A reading well below spec confirms a bad cap.
4. **Checks the contactor** for pitted contacts and confirms it pulls in and passes voltage.
5. **Tests the fan motor** — resistance across windings, checks for a seized shaft, and looks for a tripped internal overload.
6. **Verifies incoming voltage** to the motor and inspects wiring for loose or burnt connections.
7. **Checks pressures and the high-pressure switch** if the unit is tripping on protection.
8. **On Infinity/Performance models,** reads any stored fault data and confirms whether the control board is commanding the fan.

Expect a diagnostic fee of **$80–$180**, often applied toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin; starts if nudged | Failed run capacitor | Shut off, don't nudge repeatedly | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan silent, motor hot to touch | Failed motor / tripped overload | Power off, let cool | Test windings, replace motor |
| Compressor runs, fan dead, then unit trips | Bad capacitor or contactor | Turn system off | Test cap & contactor |
| Blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Clear loose debris (power off) | Straighten/replace blade |
| Unit clicks but nothing energizes | Faulty contactor | Check breaker once | Replace contactor |
| Fan iced or coil frozen | Airflow/freeze issue | Replace filter, thaw | Diagnose root cause, see Related |
| Newer Infinity unit, no fan command | Control board fault | None | Read faults, test/replace board |

## Repair costs

Honest US installed ranges. Actual prices vary by region and labor rates.

| Repair | Typical installed cost |
|---|---|
| Run capacitor replacement | $130 – $300 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $350 |
| Condenser fan motor replacement | $300 – $650 |
| Fan blade replacement | $150 – $350 |
| High-pressure switch | $150 – $350 |
| Control board (Infinity/Performance) | $400 – $900+ |
| Diagnostic fee (often credited) | $80 – $180 |

A capacitor is a cheap part (~$12–$40) but the labor and the risk of discharging it safely are why it's rated pro. If a tech quotes a full motor replacement, ask whether the capacitor was tested first — a weak cap can *cause* motors to fail prematurely, and both are sometimes replaced together.

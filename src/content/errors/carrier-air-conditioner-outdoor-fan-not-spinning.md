## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered Carrier fault code — it's a physical symptom. On a running Carrier air conditioner, the outdoor unit (the condenser) has a large fan on top that pulls air across the coil to reject heat. When the compressor is running but that fan sits still, heat can't escape, pressure and temperature climb fast, and the system will eventually shut itself down on a safety.

You'll usually notice one of two things: the outdoor unit hums but the fan doesn't turn, or the unit is completely silent while the thermostat calls for cooling. Both point to the outdoor electrical or mechanical components rather than a control-board code.

Because this involves 240V line voltage, a stored-charge capacitor, and a running compressor, most of the actual repair is **pro-level work**. The safe checks below are still worth doing first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run/start capacitor (dual-run capacitor).** The single most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor the torque it needs to start spinning. When it weakens, the fan hums but won't turn, or won't start unless nudged.
2. **Burned or stuck contactor.** The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted, welded, or non-pulling contacts can leave the fan without power.
3. **Failed condenser fan motor.** Worn bearings, an open winding, or a shorted motor. Often the fan is seized or spins with heavy resistance by hand (power off).
4. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse.** If the whole outdoor unit is dead and silent, check the electrical supply first.
5. **Debris or ice jamming the fan blade.** Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade can physically block rotation. A frozen coil can also lock things up — see *Frozen evaporator coil*.
6. **Low-voltage/control fault.** A bad thermostat wire, tripped float switch, or control-board issue can stop the 24V "call" from reaching the contactor.
7. **Compressor overload / high-pressure lockout.** If the unit overheated from a prior fan failure, it may be shut down on a safety and needs diagnosis before re-running.

On **Infinity variable-speed ECM** condensers, the fan is controlled electronically rather than by a simple capacitor, and faults are reported through the Infinity control's diagnostics — the causes and repair steps differ, so have those units serviced by a Carrier dealer.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the outdoor electrical panel, touch the capacitor, or spin the fan by hand while power is on.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can freeze the indoor coil and cascade into odd outdoor behavior. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated. If it looks disturbed, leave it and have it inspected.
- **Visible obstructions:** With the unit fully OFF at the breaker, look for leaves, sticks, or debris resting on the fan blade or grille. Clear only what's clearly loose.
- **Ice:** If the indoor coil or refrigerant lines are frosted over, turn the system OFF (fan ON at thermostat) and let it thaw — see *Carrier AC Frozen Evaporator Coil*.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full condensate pan or tripped float switch, which can cut the cooling call.

If the fan still won't spin after these, shut the system down and call a technician. Do not repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping or locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the call and voltage.** Confirms 24V reaches the contactor and 240V reaches the unit.
2. **Test the capacitor.** With power off and the capacitor safely discharged, they'll measure microfarads (µF) against the rating printed on it. A reading well below spec confirms a failed capacitor.
3. **Inspect the contactor.** Look for pitting, welding, or a coil that won't pull in.
4. **Test the fan motor.** Check windings for open/short, spin the blade for bearing drag, and confirm the motor gets voltage but won't run.
5. **Check for a safety lockout.** High-pressure switch, compressor overload, or Infinity control faults.
6. **Confirm the fix under load.** After repair, they verify the fan runs and pressures/temperatures normalize.

A good tech won't just swap a motor without testing the capacitor first — the capacitor is cheaper and more commonly at fault.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but doesn't spin | Failed capacitor | Turn unit off; don't push the blade | Test & replace dual-run capacitor |
| Fan spins if nudged, then keeps going | Weak capacitor | Turn off; stop nudging it | Replace capacitor; check motor |
| Outdoor unit totally silent | Tripped breaker/fuse or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test disconnect, contactor, control voltage |
| Fan blade won't turn by hand (power off) | Seized fan motor | Check for obvious debris only | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Buzzing at contactor, no fan/compressor | Pitted/stuck contactor | None | Replace contactor |
| Fan off, indoor coil iced | Frozen system | Set to OFF, fan ON, thaw | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Ran hot, now shut down | Overload/high-pressure lockout | Leave off, call pro | Diagnose root cause before restart |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included (varies by region and unit access):

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $75–$150 (often credited toward the repair).
- **Dual-run capacitor replacement:** $150–$300.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $300–$650 (higher for OEM Carrier motors or larger tonnage; usually includes a new capacitor).
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Infinity ECM fan module / control diagnosis:** $400–$900+ — these are specialized and best handled by a Carrier dealer.

If a tech finds the compressor was damaged by running without airflow, costs rise sharply — another reason not to keep running a unit whose fan won't spin.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Carrier outdoor unit (the condenser). The fan on top of the condenser pulls air across the coil to reject the heat your system pulls out of the house. If it stops turning while the compressor runs, the system loses its ability to shed heat and pressures climb rapidly.

Carrier condensers use either a PSC (permanent split capacitor) fan motor or, on higher-end Infinity/Greenspeed equipment, an ECM motor. Both rely on a start/run capacitor, a 24V contactor to switch line power, and thermostat/board signals to command the fan on. A failure anywhere in that chain shows up as a dead fan.

**Important:** if the compressor is humming but the fan is dead, turn the system off at the thermostat now. Running like this can burn out the compressor — the single most expensive part in the whole system.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common. The capacitor gives the fan (and often the compressor) its starting torque. When it weakens, the fan won't start on its own — but may spin if nudged.
2. **Bad condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, an open winding, or a tripped internal overload. Often the motor is hot to the touch and hums without turning.
3. **Failed contactor** — Pitted, welded, or non-pulling contacts prevent line voltage from reaching the fan and compressor.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuses** — At the panel or the outdoor disconnect. Sometimes caused by a shorting motor or capacitor.
5. **Debris or ice jamming the blade** — Sticks, a bird's nest, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Control board or thermostat signal loss** — No 24V "Y" call reaching the outdoor unit, or a failed board on communicating Infinity systems.
7. **Motor overheat / high-pressure lockout** — On some models a safety opens and cuts the unit until it cools or is serviced.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser** — the capacitor holds a dangerous charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Set to COOL and the setpoint several degrees below room temperature. Confirm it's calling for cooling.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter can freeze the indoor coil and mask what's happening outside. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor air handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — that indicates a short.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated. Don't remove the internal fuses.
- **Visible debris:** With the system OFF at the breaker, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a nest against the blade. Clear only what you can reach without removing panels.
- **Indoor vents:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut down the system.

If the fan still won't turn after these checks, stop — the remaining work involves lethal voltage and stored capacitor charge.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the steps helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the call:** Confirms 24V is reaching the contactor coil when the thermostat calls for cooling.
2. **Check line voltage:** Measures 240V at the contactor's load side to confirm power is getting through.
3. **Safely discharge and test the capacitor:** Uses a meter with a capacitance function to compare the reading against the microfarad rating printed on the cap. A reading well below spec means replacement.
4. **Test the contactor:** Looks for pitted or welded contacts and confirms the coil pulls in.
5. **Test the motor:** Checks windings for open/short, verifies it's not seized, and confirms it isn't tripping on internal overload.
6. **Inspect the board (Infinity/communicating units):** Reads any stored fault data and confirms fan output.
7. **Check pressures and airflow:** Confirms nothing else caused a safety lockout.

A capacitor swap is quick. If they're recommending a full motor replacement, expect them to have shown a failed windings test or a seized motor.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums, won't start, spins if nudged | Failed run capacitor | Shut off; don't touch fan | Discharge & replace capacitor |
| Fan dead, compressor humming | Capacitor or contactor | Turn system off immediately | Test cap, contactor, motor |
| No sound at all from unit | Breaker, disconnect, or no 24V call | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Trace 24V, test board/contactor |
| Motor hot, hums, no spin | Failed motor or bearings | Shut off | Test windings, replace motor |
| Blade physically blocked | Debris or bent blade | Clear visible debris (power off) | Straighten/replace blade & balance |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Short in motor/capacitor/wiring | Stop resetting; call pro | Isolate and repair short |
| Unit runs then shuts down | High-pressure or overheat lockout | Turn off, let cool | Diagnose pressures & airflow |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor. Diagnostic/service call fees ($90–$200) often apply and may credit toward the repair.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor (PSC):** $300–$550
- **Condenser fan motor (ECM, Infinity):** $450–$650+
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150–$300
- **Control board (communicating systems):** $400–$900
- **Breaker/disconnect repair:** $150–$400

Prices vary by region, tonnage, and whether your equipment is under warranty. Carrier parts warranties (typically 5–10 years if registered) may cover the part but not labor.

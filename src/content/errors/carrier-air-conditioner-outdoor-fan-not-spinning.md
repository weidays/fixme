## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Carrier condenser (the outdoor unit). When your AC is calling for cooling, the compressor and the outdoor fan motor should both run. The fan pulls air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system pulled out of the house.

When the fan blade sits still while the unit is trying to cool, the heat has nowhere to go. Head pressure climbs fast, and the compressor's internal overload will eventually trip — or worse, the compressor can be damaged. This is why we rate it **pro**: it's usually a straightforward electrical repair, but running the unit in this state risks a very expensive compressor.

Most Carrier single-speed condensers use a permanent-split-capacitor (PSC) fan motor driven through a dual run capacitor and a 24V contactor. Infinity variable-speed units use an ECM (electronically commutated) motor with its own control module and behave differently — those faults are handled by the system's diagnostic display, not by the checks below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The capacitor gives the motor the jolt it needs to start. When it weakens or fails, the fan hums, sits still, and gets hot. On dual capacitors the fan side can fail while the compressor side still works.
2. **Seized or worn fan motor** — Bearings wear out. If the blade is stiff to turn by hand (power off), the motor is likely done.
3. **Contactor not pulling in or burnt contacts** — Pitted, corroded, or stuck contactor points can interrupt power to the fan. Sometimes the whole unit is dead; sometimes just intermittent.
4. **Compressor overload / high-pressure trip** — If the unit already overheated from running fanless, safeties may cut power until things cool down.
5. **Debris or ice jamming the blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a warped blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Motor wiring / loose connection** — A burnt spade terminal or chewed wire at the motor or capacitor.
7. **Control board or fan relay fault** (Infinity/variable-speed models) — On ECM-equipped units the outdoor board or module governs the fan; this needs the system's diagnostics.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks you should do yourself. **Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser** — it holds live 240V and a charged capacitor that can shock you even with the power off.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Try lowering it a few degrees to force a call.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can trigger a frozen coil and shutdowns. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the pull-out disconnect near the condenser is fully seated.
- **Visible debris:** With the system OFF at the breaker, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest jamming the blade. Do not reach into the electrical compartment.
- **Ice:** If the outdoor unit or the indoor coil is iced up, turn the system off (fan ON at thermostat) and let it thaw. See our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide.
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan/float switch can shut the whole system down. Check that the drain isn't clogged and standing water isn't present.

If none of this restores the fan, leave the system OFF at the thermostat and call a technician. Do not repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works electrically from the outside in. Here's the typical sequence so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Kill and lock out power**, then discharge the run capacitor before touching anything.
2. **Test the run capacitor** with a meter for microfarad (µF) rating against the label. A reading well below spec confirms it.
3. **Spin the blade by hand** — a smooth spin points to electrical; a stiff or grinding one points to a seized motor.
4. **Check the contactor** for pitted contacts and verify 24V at the coil when there's a cooling call, plus line voltage passing through.
5. **Measure voltage at the fan motor** — power present but no rotation (with a good capacitor) means the motor is bad.
6. **Check pressures/temperatures** to rule out a high-pressure lockout, and inspect for a tripped overload.
7. **On Infinity/variable-speed units**, they'll pull fault codes from the control and check the fan module and ECM instead of a capacitor.

Replacing the capacitor is a 15–30 minute job; a motor swap runs 1–2 hours.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but blade doesn't turn | Failed run capacitor | Shut unit off; don't run it | Test and replace capacitor |
| Blade stiff/hard to spin (power off) | Seized fan motor bearings | Note it; leave power off | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Whole outdoor unit dead, no hum | Contactor or no power | Reset breaker/disconnect once | Test/replace contactor, trace power |
| Fan runs then stops, unit cycles off | Overload / high-pressure trip | Turn off, let it cool | Diagnose pressures, root cause |
| Blade visibly jammed by debris | Debris in fan path | Power off, remove visible debris | Inspect blade/motor for damage |
| Fan won't start, capacitor tests good | Bad motor or wiring | Leave to pro | Check terminals, replace motor |
| Variable-speed unit, no fan + fault code | ECM/board fault | Note the displayed code | Diagnose fan module/control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor included unless noted. Your rates and unit size will move these.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $300 – $600 (higher for larger/premium motors)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75 – $200 (often credited toward the repair)
- **ECM fan module or outdoor control board (Infinity/variable-speed):** $400 – $900+

A DIY capacitor swap is cheap on parts alone, but capacitors store a dangerous charge and the panel is live — most homeowners should not open it. If you're not trained to discharge a capacitor and work around 240V, this is a pro repair.

If the fan trouble caused the compressor to overheat repeatedly, factor in the risk of a much larger compressor or condenser replacement — another reason not to keep running the unit.

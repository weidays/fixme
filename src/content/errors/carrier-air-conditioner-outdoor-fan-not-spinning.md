## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a lettered fault code — most Carrier air conditioners don't flash a dedicated code for it. The condenser (the outdoor unit) has a fan on top that pulls air across the coil to reject heat. When that fan stops, the system can't shed heat, pressures climb, and the compressor either overheats or trips on its internal overload.

You'll usually notice this alongside weak or no cooling, a compressor that hums or cycles on and off, or a warm blast of air that never seems to cool the house. On Infinity communicating systems, the wall control may display a related system fault or a compressor lockout after the pressure or temperature limits are exceeded — but the root cause is still the stalled fan.

**Do not keep running the system.** A compressor working with no outdoor airflow is on borrowed time.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The single most common cause. Carrier condensers use a run capacitor (often a dual capacitor serving both fan and compressor) to start and steady the fan motor. When it weakens, the fan hums but won't spin, or won't start at all.
2. **Seized or worn fan motor bearings** — Years of heat, dust, and moisture wear out the motor. A motor that's hard to spin by hand (with power off) or that runs hot and cuts out is likely failing.
3. **Failed fan motor windings** — The motor is electrically dead. Often shows as no hum, no movement, and no voltage draw.
4. **Contactor not pulling in** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. If it's pitted, stuck, or its coil is dead, the outdoor unit gets no power at all.
5. **Debris jamming the blade** — A stick, leaves, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Control board or relay fault** — On Infinity/Performance boards, a failed fan relay or damaged board can withhold the fan signal.
7. **Low-voltage / thermostat wiring fault** — A broken or shorted control wire can stop the outdoor unit from being called at all.
8. **Blown fuse or tripped breaker** on the outdoor circuit.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these only. Everything inside the condenser cabinet stores dangerous voltage — leave it to a pro.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and lower the setpoint several degrees below room temperature so the system actually calls for cooling. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter chokes airflow and can lead to freeze-ups and nuisance shutdowns. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check the indoor and outdoor breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips mean a fault.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Confirm the pull-out disconnect box next to the condenser is fully seated.
- **Visible obstructions:** With the system OFF at the disconnect, look for leaves, twigs, or debris resting on the fan blade. Clear anything visible from the top grille — do not reach into the cabinet.
- **Supply vents:** Make sure indoor registers are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or a tripped float switch can shut the whole system down; check for standing water near the indoor unit.

If the fan still won't spin, shut the system off and call a licensed technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from the outside in, and you can sanity-check the quote against this:

1. **Confirm the call and voltage.** They verify the thermostat is calling and that 24V reaches the outdoor contactor.
2. **Check the contactor.** They watch whether the contactor pulls in and measure line voltage across it — confirming the fan is actually getting power.
3. **Test the capacitor.** With power safely discharged, they measure microfarads (µF) against the rating printed on the cap. A weak or shorted capacitor is condemned. This is the most frequent finding.
4. **Test the motor.** They check winding resistance, spin the blade by hand for bearing drag, and — if the capacitor is good — give the blade a manual start test. A motor that starts only with a push has a bad cap or a tired motor.
5. **Check pressures and the board.** On communicating systems they read fault history; they may check refrigerant pressures to confirm the compressor wasn't damaged by running hot.

Expect a diagnostic/service-call fee, often $90–$180, usually credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but doesn't spin | Failed run capacitor | Shut off, don't run it | Discharge and replace capacitor |
| Fan starts when nudged, then runs | Weak capacitor or tired motor | None — call a pro | Replace cap; assess motor |
| No hum, no movement, no power | Contactor or fan relay fault | Check disconnect seated | Test/replace contactor or board relay |
| Whole outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker / blown fuse / disconnect | Reset breaker once | Trace fault, replace fuse, check wiring |
| Fan blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Clear visible debris (power off) | Straighten/replace blade or motor |
| Fan runs hot, cuts out, restarts | Failing motor bearings/windings | None — call a pro | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Compressor cycles, warm air | Fan out, compressor overheating | Turn system OFF now | Diagnose fan + verify compressor health |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Run capacitor (single or dual):** $150 – $350
- **Contactor:** $150 – $300
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350 – $650 (universal motor cheaper; genuine Carrier ECM more)
- **Fan blade:** $80 – $200
- **Control/fan relay board (Performance/Infinity):** $300 – $700+
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $90 – $180, often credited toward repair

**DIY note:** A confident, careful owner can replace a $20–$40 capacitor themselves — but only after safely discharging it, which is where people get hurt. Given the stored voltage and the risk of misdiagnosing a motor as a cap, most homeowners should treat this as a pro repair.

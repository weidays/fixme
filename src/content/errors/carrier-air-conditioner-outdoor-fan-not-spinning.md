## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered fault code — it's a symptom you can see and hear. When your Carrier air conditioner calls for cooling, the outdoor unit (the condenser) should hum to life with the compressor running and the top fan pulling air up through the coil. If the fan blade sits still while the unit hums, buzzes, or trips out, something in the fan circuit has failed.

The outdoor fan's job is to reject the heat your system pulls out of the house. Without it, refrigerant pressures spike within minutes. On many Carrier units a high-pressure switch will shut the compressor down to protect it — but not always fast enough. **Never let the compressor keep running with the fan stopped.**

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** The single most common cause. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it weakens, the fan won't start on its own (but may spin if nudged), or won't start at all.
2. **Burned-out condenser fan motor.** Bearings seize or windings fail, especially on older units. Often you'll smell hot insulation or see the motor tripping on its internal overload.
3. **Contactor not pulling in / stuck.** The contactor sends 240V to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts can cause partial or no power.
4. **Debris or physical obstruction.** A stick, ice, or a bent blade jamming against the shroud can stop the blade cold.
5. **Loose or corroded wiring** at the capacitor terminals or motor leads.
6. **Control board or relay fault** — more common on Infinity/communicating systems, where a board relay drives the fan.
7. **Locked-out compressor circuit** after a high-pressure trip, which can also cut the fan on some configurations.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do without tools or exposure to live power:

- **Set the thermostat** to Cool and a few degrees below room temperature so the system actually calls for cooling.
- **Check the indoor air filter.** A clogged filter can cause icing and pressure faults that shut the outdoor unit down.
- **Check the breaker** for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Look for obvious obstructions** — leaves, grass clippings, ice, or a fallen branch — in the top fan grille. Power the unit off at the disconnect before removing anything, and never reach in while it's energized.
- **Check the condensate drain** and switch (if equipped) inside; a full drain pan can shut the system down on some setups.
- **Listen:** if you hear a hum or buzz from the outdoor unit but no fan movement, that points to a capacitor or motor problem — turn the system off and call a technician.

Do **not** open the electrical access panel, touch the capacitor, or spin the blade with the power on. Capacitors store a lethal charge even after power is removed.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling** and verify 240V is reaching the disconnect and contactor.
2. **Safely discharge and test the run capacitor** with a meter, comparing measured microfarads to the rated value stamped on the can.
3. **Test the fan motor** — check windings for resistance, look for a tripped internal overload, and check that the motor isn't drawing excessive amps.
4. **Inspect the contactor** for pitted contacts and verify the 24V coil is being energized by the control board or thermostat.
5. **Check the fan blade** turns freely by hand (power off) — seized bearings feel gritty or won't move.
6. On **Infinity/communicating systems**, pull board fault history and confirm the fan relay is commanded on.
7. **Verify refrigerant pressures** after the fan is running to rule out a secondary high-pressure trip.

A good quote should name the specific failed part and its microfarad or horsepower rating — not just "the fan is bad."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit hums, fan won't start unless pushed | Weak/failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't keep nudging it | Discharge & test capacitor, replace |
| No hum, no fan, no compressor | Contactor not pulling in, breaker, or control fault | Reset breaker once; check thermostat | Test contactor coil, 24V signal, board |
| Fan tries to start, buzzes, then trips | Failing motor or seized bearing | Shut down to protect motor | Test motor windings/amps, replace motor |
| Blade physically jammed | Debris, ice, or bent blade | Power off, clear visible debris | Straighten/replace blade, check balance |
| Burning smell from outdoor unit | Overheating motor windings | Shut off immediately | Replace fan motor and capacitor |
| Fan cuts out after several minutes | High-pressure trip / dirty coil | Rinse coil gently, replace filter | Check pressures, coil, refrigerant charge |
| Communicating system, fan won't command on | Board relay fault (Infinity) | None — leave to pro | Read fault codes, test/replace board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (PSC):** $300 – $600
- **Condenser fan motor (ECM / Infinity):** $450 – $650+
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Control board (Infinity/communicating):** $400 – $900+
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair)

Prices rise with premium equipment, difficult access, and refrigerant work. If the compressor was damaged by running without the fan, costs can climb into the thousands — which is exactly why you should shut the unit down at the first sign of a stopped fan.

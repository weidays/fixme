## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered fault code on most Carrier air conditioners — it's a physical symptom. Your outdoor unit (the condenser) has a fan on top whose job is to pull air across the condenser coil so the system can dump heat outside. When the compressor runs but that fan sits still, heat has nowhere to go.

On Infinity and some Performance systems with a communicating control board, you may see a related fault logged at the thermostat, but the most common causes here are electrical and mechanical parts inside the condenser — not a software code. This is a **pro-level** repair because it involves high-voltage components, a charged capacitor, and wiring next to the sealed refrigerant system.

**Turn the system off at the thermostat now.** Running the compressor with a dead fan is the fastest way to turn a $200 repair into a $2,000 compressor replacement.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common. The capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start spinning. When it weakens or dies, the fan won't start (or only starts if you spin it by hand).
2. **Burned-out or seized fan motor** — Bearings fail, windings overheat, and the motor simply won't turn. Often accompanied by a humming noise or a hot/burnt smell.
3. **Failed contactor** — The relay that sends 240V to the condenser. Pitted or stuck contacts can prevent the fan (and compressor) from getting power.
4. **Debris jamming the blade** — Sticks, leaves, or ice can physically block the fan blade so the motor can't turn it.
5. **Tripped breaker or lost power to the condenser** — If the whole outdoor unit is dead (no fan, no compressor), suspect a breaker or disconnect issue.
6. **High-pressure or overheat safety trip** — On some models, a safety switch cuts the unit if pressures or temperatures spike (often tied to a dirty coil or blocked airflow).
7. **Control board or relay fault** — Less common, but on communicating Infinity/Performance boards a failed relay can stop the fan output.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — none require opening the electrical panel of the condenser:

- **Thermostat:** Make sure it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Indoor air filter:** A clogged filter chokes airflow and can trip safeties. Replace it if it's dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker labeled AC or condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** There's a pull-out disconnect box on the wall near the condenser. Confirm it's fully seated.
- **Visible debris:** With the unit **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for obvious sticks or leaves resting on the blade. Do not reach in or force the blade.
- **Vents and coil:** Make sure indoor supply/return vents aren't blocked, and that the outdoor unit isn't buried in mulch or fencing that starves airflow.
- **Condensate drain:** A full drain pan can trip a safety float on some setups — clear it if you can safely access it.

Do **not** open the condenser electrical cover, touch the capacitor, or spin the blade with power on. Capacitors hold a dangerous charge even after the breaker is off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a predictable order — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirm power** at the disconnect and contactor with a meter (checking for 240V in, 24V control signal).
2. **Test the run capacitor** with a capacitance meter, comparing the reading to the microfarad (MFD) rating printed on the label. A reading well below spec confirms failure.
3. **Check the contactor** for pitted contacts and proper pull-in when the thermostat calls for cooling.
4. **Test the fan motor** directly — measuring windings for resistance/continuity and spinning the blade by hand (power off) to feel for seized bearings.
5. **Inspect the blade** for damage or obstruction.
6. **Verify safeties and pressures** if the unit is short-cycling or tripping — a dirty condenser coil or low charge can cause overheat trips.
7. **Check the control board** (communicating systems) for relay output only after ruling out the cheaper mechanical parts.

If a tech wants to replace the motor without first testing the capacitor, ask why — the capacitor is far cheaper and the more common culprit.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan doesn't spin | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't run it | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan spins if nudged, then keeps going | Weak run capacitor | Turn system off | Replace capacitor |
| Humming/buzzing, no movement, burnt smell | Seized/burned fan motor | Shut off at breaker | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Whole condenser dead (no fan, no compressor) | Breaker, disconnect, or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor & power supply |
| Fan blade visibly blocked | Debris or ice jam | Kill breaker, note obstruction | Clear jam, inspect blade & motor |
| Unit starts then shuts off quickly | Safety trip / dirty coil / low charge | Check filter, clear coil area | Clean coil, check charge & safeties |
| Fault logged on Infinity thermostat | Board relay or motor fault | Note the exact message | Diagnose board vs. component |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Contactor replacement:** $200 – $450
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $400 – $700+ (higher for ECM or Infinity-specific motors)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Control board (communicating systems):** $400 – $900

Prices vary by region, motor type (PSC vs. ECM), and whether OEM Carrier parts are required. Communicating Infinity components tend to cost more than universal replacements.

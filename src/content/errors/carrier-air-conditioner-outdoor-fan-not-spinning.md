## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a stored fault code — most Carrier single-stage condensers don't have a display, so you're diagnosing by behavior. It means the fan motor on top of your outdoor condenser unit isn't turning while the system is calling for cooling.

The outdoor fan pulls air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system removed from inside. When it stops, the refrigerant can't shed heat, high-side pressure climbs fast, and the unit will either trip its internal high-pressure/overload protection or, if it keeps running, risk compressor damage. This is why we rate it **pro** severity even though one common cause (a capacitor) is inexpensive.

On Carrier **Infinity** and inverter-driven models, fan operation is managed by a control board and may log an actual fault; behavior there varies by model, so treat those as board-diagnosed rather than the classic capacitor scenario below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed dual run capacitor.** By far the most common cause. The capacitor provides starting torque; when it weakens or fails, the fan won't start (often you'll hear the compressor hum or see the fan try to twitch). A bad capacitor may look bulged or leaking on top.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor.** Worn bearings or a burned winding stop the motor entirely. If you can't spin the blade freely by hand (power off), the motor bearings may be seized.
3. **Failed contactor.** The 24V contactor is the relay that sends line voltage to the fan and compressor. Pitted, stuck, or non-energizing contacts can cut power to the fan.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuses at the disconnect.** No power to the whole outdoor unit; sometimes the compressor and fan are both dead.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade.** Sticks, ice, or a bird can physically block the blade.
6. **Low-voltage/control fault.** A failed thermostat call, blown low-voltage fuse on the furnace/air handler board, or broken thermostat wire means the contactor never energizes.
7. **Control board fault (Infinity/inverter models).** Board or module failure on higher-end units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or opening electrical panels:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers in your electrical panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again immediately, stop and call a pro.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter can cause the whole system to freeze up and shut down; replace it if it's dirty.
- **Indoor vents & registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked.
- **Visible debris:** With the outdoor breaker OFF, look through the fan grille for obvious sticks, leaves, or ice on the blade. Do not reach in.
- **Condensate:** Check the condensate drain/pan; a full float switch can shut cooling down.

Do **not** open the electrical access panel, discharge or replace the capacitor, or spin the blade with power on. The capacitor holds a dangerous charge even after power is off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will typically:

1. Confirm there's a cooling call and 24V reaching the contactor coil.
2. Kill power at the disconnect and safely **discharge the capacitor** before touching anything.
3. Test the capacitor's microfarad (µF) rating against the nameplate value — a weak reading confirms the most common failure.
4. Check the contactor for pitted or welded contacts and verify it pulls in.
5. Test the fan motor windings for continuity/resistance and check the bearings by spinning the blade by hand.
6. Verify line voltage at the motor with the contactor energized.
7. On Infinity/inverter units, read board fault codes and check the fan module.

A fair quote should name the failed part and the measured value (for example, "capacitor reads 12 µF, rated 45 µF"). Be wary of a blanket motor-replacement quote when only the cheap capacitor was tested bad.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan won't start | Failed run capacitor | Shut system off; don't force blade | Discharge & replace capacitor, verify µF |
| Fan and compressor both dead | Tripped breaker / blown fuse / contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace fuses/contactor, find root cause |
| Blade spins freely by hand but won't run | Bad capacitor or motor winding | Power off, check for debris | Test motor windings and capacitor |
| Blade won't turn by hand (power off) | Seized fan motor bearings | None — needs replacement | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Buzzing at contactor, no fan | Weak/stuck contactor coil | None | Replace contactor |
| No 24V, nothing runs | Low-voltage fuse / thermostat wire | Check thermostat & batteries | Trace low-voltage fault, replace fuse |
| Fault logged on Infinity display | Board / fan module fault | Note the code | Board-level diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area and unit age affect the total.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $10–$40 part DIY; **$150–$350** installed by a pro (includes diagnostic).
- **Contactor replacement:** $12–$40 part; **$150–$300** installed.
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** motor $80–$250 part; **$350–$650** installed (universal vs. OEM motor and access affect price).
- **Low-voltage fuse / thermostat wire repair:** **$100–$250**.
- **Infinity/inverter control board or fan module:** **$400–$900+** depending on model and parts availability.
- **Service/diagnostic call alone:** **$90–$180**, often credited toward the repair.

If a tech condemns the compressor after the unit ran hot with no fan, that's a much larger bill — which is exactly why you should shut the system off the moment you notice the fan isn't spinning.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a Carrier fault code — it's a symptom you can see. On most Carrier air conditioners, the outdoor (condenser) fan should spin whenever the compressor is running. Its job is to pull air across the condenser coil so the system can dump the heat it removed from your home.

When the fan stops but the compressor tries to run, the condenser overheats fast. High-end Carrier units (Infinity/Greenspeed and some Performance boards) may log a high-pressure or high-temperature fault and shut the compressor down to protect it. Basic single-stage units have no display and will simply run hot until something trips or fails.

Because the most common causes involve capacitors, motors and 240V wiring, this is rated **pro** — the safe checks below are limited, and the rest is a licensed technician's job.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run/dual-run capacitor** — The single most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. A bulged, leaking or weak capacitor leaves the fan dead or slow, often with a humming compressor.
2. **Failed condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, an open winding, or a burned-out motor. Sometimes the motor is hot to the touch and thermally shut off, then works again once cool.
3. **Bad contactor** — The relay that sends 240V to the unit can pit, weld, or fail to pull in, so power never reaches the fan (and compressor).
4. **Obstructed or seized fan blade** — Debris, a bent blade, or a rusted/seized motor shaft physically blocks rotation.
5. **Loose or burned wiring / connector** — Corroded terminals or a disconnected fan lead at the capacitor or motor.
6. **Control board or relay fault** — On Infinity/Performance boards, a failed fan relay or board output. Behavior varies by model, so a tech confirms with a meter.
7. **Tripped high-pressure or motor overload** — A protective trip that stops output; the underlying cause still needs to be found.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the electrical panel on the condenser, touch the capacitor, or spin a fan by hand on a live unit.

- **Thermostat:** Set to COOL and lower the setpoint several degrees below room temperature to confirm there's an active cooling call.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause a frozen coil and odd cycling. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Confirm the pull-out disconnect box by the condenser is fully seated (someone may have pulled it for service).
- **Clear obstructions:** With the system **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks or debris resting on the fan blades. Remove loose debris only — don't reach into wiring.
- **Condensate:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and stop cooling; empty an overflowing pan and clear the visible drain line opening.

If the fan still won't spin after these, turn the system off and book a technician — running it will risk the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech works from cheapest and most likely to most expensive:

1. **Confirms the call and power** — Verifies 240V at the disconnect and 24V control signal reaching the contactor.
2. **Tests the contactor** — Checks that the coil pulls in and that both line terminals pass voltage without pitting.
3. **Tests the capacitor** — Uses a meter to read microfarads against the rating printed on the cap. A reading well below spec means replacement.
4. **Tests the fan motor** — Checks windings for continuity/shorts and confirms it isn't seized. May bench-test with a known-good capacitor.
5. **Inspects wiring** — Looks for burned terminals, loose leads, and rodent damage.
6. **Reads any board faults** — On Infinity/Performance systems, pulls stored codes and checks the fan relay output.
7. **Checks pressures** — Confirms the unit didn't trip on high pressure and that the failure didn't damage the compressor.

Expect a diagnostic fee of roughly $80–$180, usually credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan sits still | Failed run/dual-run capacitor | Shut unit off at breaker | Test and replace capacitor |
| Fan spins slowly then stops, hot motor | Weak capacitor or failing motor | Turn off; don't force blade | Meter capacitor, test motor windings |
| Nothing at outdoor unit, no hum | Bad contactor, disconnect, or breaker | Reset breaker once; seat disconnect | Test contactor and 24V signal |
| Fan blade won't turn by hand (power off) | Seized motor or jammed blade | Remove loose debris only | Replace motor / clear obstruction |
| Fan worked, unit now short-cycles | High-pressure trip from prior overheat | Turn system off | Check pressures, verify compressor |
| Burning smell near condenser | Burned wiring or motor | Shut off at breaker immediately | Replace damaged wiring/motor |
| Higher-end unit shows a fault code | Board fan relay or protection trip | Note the code | Read code, test relay output |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your region, model, and part availability shift these.

- **Run/dual-run capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$300
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$650+ (universal vs. OEM matters)
- **Fan blade:** $80–$200
- **Wiring/terminal repair:** $120–$300
- **Control board (Infinity/Performance):** $400–$900
- **Diagnostic fee:** $80–$180 (often credited toward repair)

A capacitor is the best-case, most common fix. If the fan ran without its fan while the compressor labored, ask the tech to confirm the compressor wasn't damaged — that's the expensive failure this problem can cause.

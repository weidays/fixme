## What this code means

Most Carrier central AC condensers don't flash a dedicated "outdoor fan" fault code — this is a **symptom**, not a board code. The outdoor unit (condenser) hums or the compressor runs, but the large fan on top isn't turning. On Carrier Infinity systems with a communicating control, you may instead see a general outdoor unit fault or a "compressor lockout" once high pressure builds.

The outdoor fan's job is to pull air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system pulled out of the house. If it stops, refrigerant pressure and temperature spike quickly. **Running the system in this condition risks a compressor failure**, which is the most expensive repair on the unit. That's why this is rated a pro-level problem.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The single most common cause. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. A bulged, leaking, or out-of-spec capacitor leaves the motor humming but not spinning.
2. **Burned-out condenser fan motor** — Bearings seize or windings fail, especially on older units. The motor gets hot, hums, or does nothing.
3. **Failed contactor** — The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit can have pitted or stuck contacts, cutting power to the fan.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — The outdoor disconnect fuse or the breaker feeding the condenser has opened.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, a bird, or ice can physically block the blade.
6. **Wiring or low-voltage issue** — A loose wire, chewed conductor, or failed 24V signal from the thermostat/board.
7. **Bad fan motor capacitor on separate-cap units** — Some Carrier models use a separate fan capacitor rather than a dual capacitor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical access panel on the condenser** — capacitors store a dangerous charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Set to Cool and lower the setpoint several degrees to confirm the system is actually calling. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause freezing and shutdowns that mimic outdoor problems. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers in your main panel. If one is tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Confirm the pull-out disconnect box near the condenser is fully seated (it's a plug, not a wire connection — safe to check).
- **Visible debris:** With the unit fully off at the breaker, look through the top grille for obvious sticks, leaves, or ice on the blade. Clear only what you can reach without removing panels.
- **Supply vents:** Make sure indoor vents aren't closed or blocked, which contributes to freezing shutdowns.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or float switch can shut the system down; check for standing water at the indoor unit.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, call a licensed HVAC technician. Do **not** repeatedly nudge the blade or cycle the breaker to keep it running.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the call:** Verifies 24V is reaching the contactor coil when the thermostat calls for cooling.
2. **Check power at the contactor:** Measures line voltage in and out of the contactor to see if it's pulling in and passing power.
3. **Test the capacitor:** Uses a meter to read microfarads against the rating printed on the cap. A reading well below spec (typically more than ~10% low) means replacement.
4. **Test the motor:** Checks windings for resistance and shorts to ground, and spins the blade by hand to feel for seized bearings.
5. **Inspect wiring and blade:** Looks for burnt terminals, chewed insulation, and physical obstructions.
6. **Verify after repair:** Confirms the fan starts on its own and checks refrigerant pressures to make sure the compressor wasn't stressed.

A good tech checks the capacitor and contactor before condemning the motor — a $25 capacitor should never turn into a motor sale without proof.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan doesn't turn | Failed run capacitor | Shut off system; don't nudge blade | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan starts if pushed with a stick | Weak capacitor | Turn unit off, stop nudging | Replace capacitor |
| No sound at all from outdoor unit | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test contactor, low-voltage circuit |
| Fan hums but blade won't move | Seized fan motor bearings | Turn off at breaker | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Blade physically stuck | Debris or ice jam | Power off, clear visible debris | Clear jam, inspect blade balance |
| Fan runs then stops, breaker trips | Shorted motor or wiring | Do not keep resetting | Diagnose short, replace motor/wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor, for typical single-phase Carrier condensers. Prices vary by region and unit size.

- **Run/dual capacitor replacement:** $150 – $300 (part is $15–$45; the rest is diagnostic labor and service call)
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $300 – $650 (often includes a new capacitor)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Wiring repair:** $150 – $400 depending on extent
- **Service call / diagnostic:** $80 – $180, often credited toward the repair
- **Infinity/variable-speed ECM motor:** $500 – $1,000+, as these motors and controls cost more

If a tech quotes a full motor replacement, ask whether the capacitor was tested first — a bad cap is far cheaper and far more common.

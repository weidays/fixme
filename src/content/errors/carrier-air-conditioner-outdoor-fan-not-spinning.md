## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a Carrier fault code — most single-stage Carrier condensers have no display to flash a number. You notice it because the compressor hums or runs while the fan blade on top of the outdoor unit sits still, or the whole unit is silent and warm.

The outdoor (condenser) fan pulls air across the condenser coil to dump the heat your system pulled out of the house. If it stops while the compressor keeps running, pressure and temperature climb fast. That is why this is a **pro-level, shut-it-down issue**: continuing to run a unit with a dead fan is one of the most common ways to kill an otherwise good compressor.

On Infinity (variable-speed) systems, the control board may log a related fault and show it at the thermostat or on the board's LED. On Comfort and Performance single-stage units, there's usually no code at all — just a fan that won't turn.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** The single most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt of torque it needs to start spinning. When it weakens, the motor hums but won't start (and often starts if nudged).
2. **Burned-out condenser fan motor.** Bearings seize or the windings fail from age, heat, or running against a bad capacitor for too long.
3. **Bad contactor.** The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit can have pitted or welded contacts, so the fan (and sometimes compressor) never gets power.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown high-voltage fuse.** No power reaching the condenser at all.
5. **Debris or ice jamming the fan blade.** Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Failed capacitor start relay / control board (Infinity).** On variable-speed units, a board or motor module fault can disable the fan.
7. **Motor overheat / thermal cutout.** A dirty coil or hot day can trip the motor's internal overload; it may restart when cool, which points to an underlying problem.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Never open the outdoor unit's electrical panel** — the capacitor holds a dangerous charge even with power off.

- **Turn the system OFF.** Set the thermostat to off so the compressor stops running without its fan. This protects the compressor while you check things.
- **Check your breaker.** Look for a tripped breaker (outdoor AC and/or air handler). Reset it once. If it trips again immediately, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the outdoor disconnect.** Many units have a pull-out disconnect box on the wall near the condenser. Make sure it's fully seated.
- **Look for obvious blockage.** With power off, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a nest jamming the blade. Clear visible debris by hand.
- **Check the air filter and indoor vents.** A badly clogged filter or closed vents can freeze the system and mask the real issue — worth ruling out.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's calling for cooling and, if applicable, replace the batteries.

If the fan still won't spin, shut the unit off and call a licensed HVAC technician. Do not keep resetting or running it.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the steps lets you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the call and voltage.** They verify the thermostat is calling for cool and that 240V is reaching the condenser and 24V is reaching the contactor coil.
2. **Test the contactor.** They check whether the contactor is pulling in and passing power through its contacts.
3. **Discharge and test the capacitor.** They safely discharge it, then measure microfarads (µF) against the rating printed on it. A reading well below spec means replace.
4. **Test the fan motor.** With a known-good capacitor, they check motor windings for resistance and continuity, and spin the blade to feel for seized bearings.
5. **Check amp draw.** If the motor runs, they confirm it's not drawing high amps from a failing bearing or dirty coil.
6. **Board diagnostics (Infinity).** On variable-speed units, they read board fault codes and check the fan motor module rather than a simple capacitor.

A good tech tests the capacitor *and* motor together, because a failed capacitor often damages the motor and vice versa.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan still; spins if nudged | Failed run capacitor | Shut unit off, don't nudge repeatedly | Discharge & replace capacitor, test motor |
| Fan and compressor both dead, no sound | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once, check disconnect | Test contactor & incoming voltage |
| Fan tries to start, hums, then stops | Weak capacitor or failing motor | Turn off to protect compressor | Measure µF, test motor windings/amps |
| Blade physically blocked | Debris or bent blade | Power off, clear visible debris | Straighten/balance or replace blade |
| Fan runs a while then quits on hot days | Motor overheat / dirty coil | Turn off, note the pattern | Clean coil, check amp draw, replace motor if failing |
| No fan, thermostat shows a fault (Infinity) | Board or motor module fault | Note the code shown | Read board, test/replace module or board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Prices vary by region and whether you're in warranty.

- **Run capacitor:** $150 – $300 installed. The most common and cheapest fix.
- **Contactor:** $150 – $300 installed.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300 – $650 installed. Universal/aftermarket motors are cheaper than OEM.
- **Fan blade:** $80 – $200 installed.
- **Infinity fan motor module / control board:** $400 – $900+, depending on the part and availability.
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180, often credited toward the repair.

If the unit is 12+ years old and needs a motor plus other parts, ask whether repair still makes sense versus replacement — especially if the compressor was already stressed by running without the fan.

## What this code means

On most Carrier air conditioners, "outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom you observe, not a specific numbered fault code. The outdoor unit (condenser) has a large fan that pulls air across the coil to reject heat. When the compressor runs but that fan sits still, the system cannot dump heat, pressures climb, and the unit will overheat or trip out.

On Carrier **Infinity** systems, the wall control may log a related fault (for example, a low-voltage or high-pressure lockout) when the fan fails. On single-stage **Comfort** and **Performance** condensers, there is no display — you simply hear the compressor hum while the fan blade stays still. Because behavior varies by model, treat the code text here as the observed problem rather than a factory code.

**Do not keep running the system.** A compressor running without airflow can be permanently damaged in minutes.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common. The capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it weakens, the fan won't start on its own (but may spin if nudged) or won't start at all.
2. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, or an animal nest can physically block the blade.
3. **Failed condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, a burned winding, or an overheated motor that has tripped its internal thermal cutout.
4. **Failed or pitted contactor** — The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit may not be closing, so neither the fan nor compressor gets full power.
5. **Loss of 24V control signal** — A bad thermostat wire, low-voltage fuse, or control-board issue means the contactor never energizes.
6. **Tripped breaker or loose high-voltage connection** — Corroded or loose wire at the fan motor or capacitor.
7. **Seized motor from age or moisture** — Common on older units after wet or humid seasons.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do without training:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the target temp is below room temp. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the indoor coil to freeze and shut cooling down; swap it if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air handler breaker and the outdoor disconnect breaker. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Visible debris:** With the system **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for obvious sticks or leaves and clear them by hand. Do not stick tools into a powered unit.
- **Supply/return vents:** Make sure indoor registers aren't blocked or closed.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a float switch and stop cooling; check for standing water near the indoor unit.

Do **not** open the outdoor electrical panel, test or replace the capacitor, or spin the fan on a live unit. The capacitor stores a dangerous charge even with power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. Kill power at the disconnect and verify it with a meter.
2. **Discharge and test the run capacitor** with a multimeter against its labeled microfarad (µF) rating — a weak reading confirms the most common cause.
3. Inspect the **contactor** for pitting, buzzing, or failure to pull in.
4. Check for **24V control signal** reaching the contactor coil and inspect the low-voltage fuse on the control board.
5. Test the **fan motor windings** for continuity and shorts, and check whether an internal thermal overload has tripped.
6. Verify the blade spins freely by hand once power is confirmed off.
7. On **Infinity** systems, read stored faults at the wall control to cross-check pressure or communication errors.

If a tech quotes a full motor replacement without first testing the cheap capacitor, ask them to confirm the capacitor reading — that's the number-one gotcha.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan still; spins if nudged | Weak/dead run capacitor | Turn system off | Test & replace capacitor |
| No hum, fan and compressor both dead | Contactor or 24V signal | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, control voltage, low-voltage fuse |
| Fan blade physically blocked | Debris or nest | Power off, clear visible debris | Inspect blade, bearings, motor |
| Fan runs briefly then stops | Overheating motor / thermal trip | Turn off, don't reset repeatedly | Test motor windings, replace motor |
| Breaker trips when unit starts | Shorted motor or wiring | Reset once only | Find short, replace faulty component |
| Infinity shows high-pressure fault | Fan failure causing pressure spike | Turn off, note the fault | Diagnose fan + verify pressures |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area, unit age, and refrigerant type affect the final number.

- **Run/dual capacitor:** $15–$40 part; $150–$300 installed by a pro.
- **Contactor:** $20–$60 part; $150–$300 installed.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $150–$350 part; **$400–$650 installed** (includes new capacitor).
- **Low-voltage fuse / control wiring repair:** $75–$250.
- **Control board (Infinity/communicating units):** $300–$700+ installed.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180, often credited toward the repair.

If a compressor was damaged by running without the fan, repairs can climb into the $1,500–$3,000+ range — which is why you should shut the system down promptly.

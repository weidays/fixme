## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a formal Carrier fault code — it's a symptom. On most Carrier air conditioners, the outdoor unit (condenser) contains a fan that pulls air across the condenser coil to reject heat. When that fan stops, the compressor may still try to run, pressures climb fast, and the system either shuts down on a safety or risks compressor damage.

On **Infinity series** systems with communicating controls, you may see a related fault or status message at the thermostat or on the outdoor board. On **Comfort and Performance single-stage** units, there's no display — the fan simply doesn't turn, and you diagnose it by symptom.

Because this involves 240V power, a charged capacitor that can shock you even with power off, and sometimes sealed-system pressures, this is rated a **pro** repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it weakens, the fan won't start on its own (but may spin if nudged). A bulged or leaking capacitor is a dead giveaway.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Bearings fail, or the winding burns out. The fan won't turn even when nudged, or hums without spinning.
3. **Failed contactor** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts can interrupt fan power.
4. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, ice, or a bird can physically block the blade.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse** — No power reaching the outdoor unit at all (compressor also won't run).
6. **Bad control board (Infinity/communicating units)** — Less common; the outdoor board fails to command the fan.
7. **Overheated motor on thermal cutout** — A motor that runs hot may shut off, then restart when cool, giving intermittent behavior.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should perform:

- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. If it's a battery model, replace the batteries.
- **Indoor air filter** — A badly clogged filter can cause the system to freeze up or shut down. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker** — Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers in your electrical panel. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Visible debris (power off)** — Turn off the outdoor disconnect switch, then look through the top grille for sticks, leaves, or a bird's nest jamming the blade. Remove debris you can reach without tools. Do **not** stick your hand near the blade with power on.
- **Ice on the coil** — If the outdoor coil or lines are iced, shut the system off and let it thaw. See our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide.
- **Condensate** — Make sure the indoor drain isn't overflowing and tripping a float switch that stops the system.

Do **not** open the electrical panel on the outdoor unit, test or discharge the capacitor, or push the fan blade with power on. The capacitor holds a dangerous charge even after power is off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. **Kill power at the disconnect** and safely discharge the capacitor before touching anything.
2. **Test the run capacitor** with a meter — comparing the measured microfarads (µF) against the rating printed on the can. Anything more than ~6% off gets replaced.
3. **Check the contactor** for pitted contacts and verify 24V is pulling it in and 240V is passing through.
4. **Test the fan motor windings** for continuity and correct resistance, and check that it spins freely by hand.
5. **Verify incoming voltage** at the disconnect and unit.
6. On **Infinity/communicating systems**, pull fault codes from the outdoor board and thermostat and check board outputs.
7. If the fan runs but the system still shorts out, they'll check refrigerant pressures and the high-pressure switch.

A good quote should name the failed part and its measured value — not just "the fan's bad."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin; starts if nudged | Weak/dead run capacitor | Shut off, don't nudge repeatedly | Test and replace capacitor |
| Fan won't turn even when nudged | Seized fan motor | Power off, confirm blade is stuck | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Neither fan nor compressor runs | No power / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, disconnect, wiring |
| Fan blade physically blocked | Debris in unit | Power off, remove visible debris | Inspect blade/motor for damage |
| Fan starts then stops after a while | Motor thermal cutout / overheating | Note the pattern | Diagnose motor, airflow, capacitor |
| Fault message on Infinity thermostat | Board or motor fault | Note the code shown | Pull codes, test board outputs |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $300 – $700 (higher for OEM or ECM motors on Infinity units)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Outdoor control board (Infinity/communicating):** $400 – $900
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair)
- **DIY breaker reset or debris removal:** $0

Capacitors are cheap parts, so beware quotes that seem inflated for what is often a 20-minute fix. If a tech pushes a full motor replacement, ask whether the capacitor was tested first.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a **symptom**, not a stored fault code on most Carrier condensers. Standard single-stage and two-stage Carrier units (Comfort and Performance series) don't have a display that flashes a number for this — you diagnose it by observation. Higher-end **Infinity** systems with a communicating control can log faults and may report a fan or motor error at the indoor control or on the thermostat.

The outdoor fan sits on top of the condenser unit and pulls air across the coil to reject heat. When it stops, the compressor may still be trying to run — and that is the real danger. Without airflow, refrigerant pressure and temperature spike, which can trip the compressor on overload or, over time, destroy it.

**Key rule: if the fan isn't spinning but you hear the compressor humming or running, turn the system off at the thermostat and breaker until it's fixed.**

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual-run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it weakens, the fan won't start on its own but may spin if nudged.
2. **Failed condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings or a burned-out winding. The motor may hum, get hot, or do nothing at all.
3. **Bad contactor** — The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit can burn or pit its contacts, cutting power to the fan (and sometimes the compressor).
4. **Tripped or blown control/safety** — A tripped breaker, blown low-voltage fuse, or open high-pressure switch can shut the outdoor unit down.
5. **Seized fan or obstruction** — Debris, a bent blade, or ice against the blade can physically jam the fan.
6. **Wiring/connection failure** — A loose, corroded, or chewed wire at the motor, capacitor, or contactor.
7. **Control board or ECM fault (Infinity/variable-speed)** — On communicating units, the board or electronic motor module can fail and stop the fan.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser, discharge capacitors, or touch wiring** — capacitors hold a dangerous charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace thermostat batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the **outdoor disconnect/breaker**. Reset a tripped breaker once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause freezing and airflow issues that mimic outdoor problems — replace if dirty.
- **Visible obstruction:** With the system **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest touching the blade. Clear only what you can reach without removing panels.
- **Condensate/ice:** If the outdoor unit or lines are iced over, turn the system off and let it thaw — running it iced can jam the fan.
- **Vents:** Make sure indoor supply and return vents are open and unblocked.

If the fan still won't spin after these, it's an electrical or mechanical repair — call an HVAC tech.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verifies power** at the disconnect and confirms 24V is reaching the contactor.
2. **Tests the run capacitor** with a meter — checking the microfarad (µF) rating against the label. A weak reading confirms the most common failure.
3. **Checks the contactor** for pitted contacts and proper pull-in when energized.
4. **Powers the fan directly / spins the blade** — if it starts with a push, the capacitor is the culprit; if it hums without moving, the motor is likely seized.
5. **Tests the fan motor** windings for continuity and shorts to ground; checks bearings for drag.
6. **Inspects wiring** for loose, burned, or corroded connections.
7. **On Infinity units**, reads stored fault codes at the control and tests the ECM module.

Ask the tech to show you the capacitor reading or the motor test result — legitimate diagnoses are backed by measurements, not guesses.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan still; compressor humming | Failed run capacitor | Shut system off to protect compressor | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan spins when nudged with a stick | Weak capacitor | Turn off, don't run it | Confirm µF, replace capacitor |
| Fan hums but won't turn | Seized motor / bad bearings | Turn off | Replace fan motor |
| Nothing runs outdoors | Tripped breaker, bad contactor, blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, fuse, low-voltage |
| Blade physically jammed | Debris or ice | Clear visible debris (power off), thaw ice | Inspect blade, motor, coil |
| Fault logged on Infinity thermostat | ECM/control fault | Note the code | Diagnose board/motor module |
| Wire loose or chewed near unit | Rodent/vibration damage | Do not touch wiring | Repair/replace wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included (varies by region and model):

- **Run capacitor:** $150 – $300
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (PSC):** $300 – $650
- **Fan blade replacement:** $100 – $250
- **Low-voltage fuse / minor wiring:** $75 – $200
- **Infinity ECM fan module / control board:** $400 – $900+ (communicating parts are pricier)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75 – $200 (often credited toward the repair)

A capacitor is the cheapest and most common fix; a variable-speed ECM motor or Infinity board is the most expensive. Get the specific part reading before approving a motor or board replacement.

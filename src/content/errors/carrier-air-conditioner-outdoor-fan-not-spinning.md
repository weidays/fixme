## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a symptom you observe at the outdoor condenser unit. When your thermostat calls for cooling, the compressor and the outdoor (condenser) fan should both start. The fan pulls air across the condenser coil to dump the heat your system collected indoors.

If the compressor hums or runs but the fan blade sits still, the unit cannot reject heat. Refrigerant pressure and temperature climb quickly, and the compressor's internal overload will eventually trip — or, worse, the compressor overheats and fails. That's why this is a **pro-level** issue: the most common causes involve a capacitor that stores a dangerous electrical charge or live 240-volt wiring.

Higher-end Infinity systems may log a related communication or outdoor-unit fault at the thermostat, but base Comfort and Performance models give you no code at all — just a fan that won't turn.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** The single most common cause. A weak or dead capacitor robs the fan motor of starting torque. Classic tell: the fan spins if you nudge the blade, then keeps running.
2. **Failed condenser fan motor.** Worn bearings, an open winding, or a tripped thermal overload. The motor may hum, smell hot, or feel seized.
3. **Faulty contactor.** The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit can have pitted or stuck contacts, so voltage never reaches the fan and compressor.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuses / disconnect.** No power to the condenser at all — nothing runs.
5. **Obstructed or damaged fan blade.** Debris, ice, or a bent/loose blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Control-board or thermostat call failure.** Less common; the 24V signal never reaches the contactor. More likely on communicating Infinity systems.
7. **Loose or corroded wiring** at the capacitor, motor, or contactor terminals.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks you can safely do without opening the electrical compartment:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the indoor coil to freeze, which sometimes shows up as odd outdoor behavior. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again immediately, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated. If it's pulled, push it back in.
- **Visible obstructions:** Look through the top grille (do **not** remove panels). Clear leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest you can reach from outside.
- **Ice or condensate:** If the outdoor coil or lines are iced over, turn the system to FAN only and let it thaw. See our frozen coil guide below.

If the fan still won't spin after these, shut the system off and call a technician. **Do not open the electrical panel or touch the capacitor** — it can hold a lethal charge even with power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech follows a repeatable sequence so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** and confirm 24V is reaching the contactor coil.
2. **Cut power at the disconnect** and safely **discharge the capacitor** before touching anything.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads (µF). A reading well below spec confirms it's failed.
4. **Check the contactor** for pitted contacts and proper pull-in when energized.
5. **Test the fan motor** — resistance across windings, continuity to ground, and whether it spins freely by hand.
6. **Confirm incoming voltage** (typically ~240V) at the disconnect and fuses.
7. **Spin test:** with the correct new capacitor installed, verify the fan starts on its own.

A good tech tests the capacitor before condemning the more expensive motor — because a $20 part is a common fix mistakenly quoted as a full motor replacement.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan spins if nudged, then runs | Failed run capacitor | None — pro repair | Discharge & replace capacitor, verify µF |
| Compressor hums, fan dead | Bad capacitor or motor | Shut system off | Test capacitor, then motor windings |
| Nothing runs outdoors | Breaker, disconnect, or contactor | Reset breaker once, seat disconnect | Test incoming voltage & contactor |
| Fan motor hot / burnt smell | Failed motor bearings or winding | Shut off immediately | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Buzzing at contactor, no fan | Stuck/pitted contactor | None | Replace contactor |
| Blade blocked or wobbling | Debris or loose/bent blade | Clear visible debris only | Reseat/replace blade, check balance |
| Coil iced over | Airflow / refrigerant issue | Set to FAN, let thaw | Diagnose airflow or charge |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices, including parts and labor. Your Carrier model, tonnage, and region affect the final number.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $130 – $280
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $300 – $650 (often includes a new capacitor)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Control board (Infinity/communicating models):** $400 – $800+

If a tech quotes a full motor replacement, ask whether the **capacitor was tested first** — it's the cheapest and most common fix, and replacing it alone often solves the problem.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered fault code on most Carrier units — it's a physical symptom. Your outdoor condenser unit (the metal box outside) has a fan that pulls air across the coil to reject heat. When the compressor runs but that top-mounted fan sits still — or spins slowly, or only starts if you nudge it — the system can't shed heat.

On **Infinity series** units, related pressure or temperature faults may be logged at the wall control. On **Comfort and Performance series** and older condensers, there's no display outside, so you'll diagnose it by observation: listen for the compressor humming while the fan blade stays motionless.

This matters because running the compressor without condenser airflow drives head pressure and temperature up fast. That's why we flag this as a pro-level repair — left running, a fan problem can turn into a compressor replacement.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. When it weakens or fails, the fan hums but won't spin, or won't start at all. Cheap part, but stores a dangerous charge.
2. **Seized or burned-out fan motor** — Worn bearings seize the blade; overheating burns the windings. If the blade won't turn freely by hand (power off) or the motor is hot and smells burnt, it's likely the motor.
3. **Bad contactor** — The contactor is the relay that sends 240V to the compressor and fan. Pitted or stuck contacts can starve the fan of power. Sometimes you'll hear the compressor but nothing to the fan.
4. **Tripped internal overload / high-limit** — The motor's thermal overload cuts power when it overheats, then may reset when cool. Often a symptom of a failing motor or capacitor, not the root cause.
5. **Debris or ice jamming the blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a bird's nest can physically block the fan. In shoulder-season conditions, ice can also lock it.
6. **Control board or wiring fault** — Less common. Loose connections, chewed wires, or a failed board (more likely on Infinity units) can interrupt the fan signal.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser or touch the capacitor** — it holds a lethal charge even with power off.

- **Set the thermostat to Off**, then Cool with a low setpoint. Confirm the outdoor unit is actually being called to run.
- **Check the breakers** — both the indoor air handler/furnace breaker and the outdoor disconnect/breaker. Reset a tripped breaker once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Replace a dirty air filter** — a clogged filter can cause icing and pressure issues that cascade to the outdoor unit.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from the outside** for obvious debris on the fan blade — sticks, leaves, a nest. Clear only what you can reach from outside with the unit off at the disconnect.
- **Check the condensate drain** isn't backed up (some systems have a float switch that shuts cooling down).
- **Look for ice** on the outdoor coil. If present, turn the system off and let it thaw before restarting.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, turn the system off and call a technician. Do not repeatedly restart a unit whose fan won't run — you risk the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Visual and manual check** — With power off at the disconnect, they'll spin the blade by hand. Free spin points to electrical (capacitor/contactor); stiff or seized points to the motor.
2. **Capacitor test** — Safely discharge, then measure the capacitor's microfarad (µF) rating against the label. A reading well below spec confirms a bad cap. This is the #1 finding.
3. **Voltage checks** — Confirm 240V reaching the contactor and 24V at the contactor coil, then check for power leaving to the fan motor.
4. **Motor test** — Check windings for continuity/resistance and test the internal overload. A grounded or open winding means motor replacement.
5. **Contactor inspection** — Look for pitted, welded, or chattering contacts.
6. **Pressure check** — If the compressor was run without the fan, they may verify system pressures and check for compressor damage.

A good tech tests the capacitor first because it's the cheapest, most likely fix — be wary of anyone jumping straight to a motor or compressor replacement without showing you the cap reading.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't touch the cap | Discharge and test cap; replace |
| Fan won't turn by hand (power off) | Seized fan motor | Turn off; don't force it | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Compressor runs, no fan power at all | Bad contactor or wiring | Reset breaker once; then stop | Test/replace contactor, check wiring |
| Fan starts then stops after minutes | Motor overheating/overload | Turn off, let cool, call pro | Test motor, capacitor, and load |
| Blade physically blocked | Debris or ice | Clear visible debris (unit off) | Inspect for damage, restart safely |
| No outdoor activity, indoor runs | Disconnect, breaker, or board | Check outdoor disconnect/breaker | Trace control signal, test board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included unless noted. Prices vary by region and unit access.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350 (part is $15–$40; most of the cost is the service call and labor)
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $350 – $650 (universal motors are cheaper; OEM Carrier motors and Infinity-compatible motors run higher)
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $175, often credited toward the repair
- **Control board (Infinity units):** $400 – $700+
- **DIY debris removal:** $0

If a tech says the compressor was damaged by running without the fan, that's a $1,500–$2,800+ repair — another reason not to keep restarting the unit.

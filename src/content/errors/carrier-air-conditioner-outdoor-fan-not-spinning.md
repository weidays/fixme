## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a symptom you observe at the condenser (the outdoor unit). The compressor may be humming or running while the large fan on top of the unit sits still, or the entire unit may be silent.

The outdoor fan pulls air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system removes from the house. When it stops, head pressure and temperature spike almost immediately. That's why this is rated a **pro** problem: even if the cause is a cheap part, running the equipment in this condition risks a compressor failure that costs thousands.

Most Carrier split-system condensers use a single-phase PSC fan motor driven by a **dual run capacitor** and switched by a **contactor**. Higher-end Infinity/Greenspeed units may use an ECM fan motor and communicating control board, which behave differently and usually require dealer-level diagnostics.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. A weak or bulged capacitor can't provide starting torque, so the fan hums or won't move but spins freely by hand.
2. **Seized or failed condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, burned windings, or heat damage. The motor may buzz, trip the breaker, or do nothing.
3. **Bad contactor** — Pitted or stuck contacts, or a dead 24V coil, so line voltage never reaches the fan and compressor.
4. **Debris or ice jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse / disconnect pulled** — No power to the outdoor unit at all.
6. **Failed control board or low-voltage wiring fault** — More common on Infinity/communicating systems; also blown low-voltage transformer or a tripped float switch cutting the call for cooling.
7. **Loose or corroded wiring at the motor, capacitor, or contactor** — Vibration and weather over years can back out spade connectors.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Turn the thermostat to Off and the outdoor disconnect to Off before touching the unit.**

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to Cool and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A clogged indoor filter can cause icing and shutdowns that mimic outdoor problems. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor air-handler/furnace breaker and the outdoor AC breaker in the main panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser can be loose or unseated. Push it firmly back in.
- **Visible debris:** With power off at the disconnect, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or ice against the blade. Remove obvious debris. Do **not** reach in with power on.
- **Vents & condensate:** Make sure indoor supply/return vents are open. Check that the condensate drain pan isn't full — a tripped float switch will stop the whole system.

Do **not** open the electrical panel on the condenser, discharge or test the capacitor, or push the fan blade with the unit powered. Capacitors hold a lethal charge even after power is off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheapest and most likely toward expensive. Expect them to:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling** and verify 24V is present at the contactor coil.
2. **Check line voltage** at the disconnect and across the contactor.
3. **Discharge and test the run capacitor** with a meter, comparing the microfarad (µF) reading to the rating printed on it. A reading well below spec confirms a bad cap.
4. **Test the fan motor** — spin it by hand for bearing drag, check windings for continuity/short, and confirm voltage reaches the motor.
5. **Inspect the contactor** for pitted contacts and test the coil.
6. **Check wiring** at all spade connectors for corrosion or heat damage.
7. On **Infinity/communicating systems**, connect to the board or thermostat to read fault history and confirm ECM commands.

Sanity check: if a tech quotes a full motor or board replacement without first testing the capacitor, ask them to show you the capacitor reading. That's the number-one culprit and should be ruled out first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan still, spins freely by hand | Failed run capacitor | Shut unit off; don't run it | Discharge & test cap, replace |
| Fan and compressor both dead, unit silent | No power / contactor / breaker | Reset breaker once, reseat disconnect | Test line voltage, contactor, coil |
| Fan buzzes but won't turn, warm/burnt smell | Seized fan motor | Shut unit off immediately | Test & replace fan motor |
| Fan blade physically blocked | Debris or bent blade | Remove visible debris (power off) | Straighten/replace blade, check motor |
| Breaker trips when unit calls | Shorted motor or wiring | Do not keep resetting | Isolate short, replace component |
| Whole system off, drain pan full | Tripped float switch / condensate | Clear/empty pan, clear drain line | Clear drain, verify float switch |
| Infinity unit shows fault at thermostat | Board / ECM / comms fault | Note the code, don't reset repeatedly | Dealer diagnostics on control system |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area, unit access, and warranty status change them.

- **Run capacitor:** $12–$40 part DIY; **$150–$300** installed by a pro.
- **Contactor:** $15–$40 part; **$150–$300** installed.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $80–$300 part; **$350–$650+** installed (universal motors are cheaper than exact Carrier replacements).
- **Fan blade:** $20–$60 part; **$120–$250** installed.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180, often credited toward the repair.
- **Control board (Infinity/communicating):** $300–$700+ installed; usually dealer-only.
- **Compressor damage** from running without the fan: $1,500–$3,000+ — the reason to shut the unit off now.

If the unit is under Carrier's parts warranty (commonly 10 years with registration), the part itself may be free — you'll still pay labor and refrigerant/diagnostic charges.

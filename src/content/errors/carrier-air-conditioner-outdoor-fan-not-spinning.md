## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is not a stored fault code on most Carrier air conditioners — it's a symptom you observe at the outdoor condenser unit. The large fan on top of the condenser is supposed to pull air across the coil to reject the heat your system is removing from your home. When the compressor runs but that fan sits still, the unit cannot dump heat.

On Infinity communicating systems, a related fault may appear on the thermostat or control board, but on Comfort and Performance units there is usually no display — you simply notice the fan isn't moving while the compressor hums or the unit stops cooling.

This is a **pro-level** problem. Running the compressor with a dead fan builds dangerously high pressure and heat, and the most common repairs involve a capacitor that stores a lethal charge and line-voltage wiring. Turn the system **off** until it's diagnosed.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it weakens or fails, the motor hums but won't spin, or won't start at all.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Bearings wear out, or the windings fail from years of heat. A motor that's hot, smells burnt, or won't turn by hand (with power off) is likely done.
3. **Failed contactor** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts can leave the fan without power.
4. **Debris or ice jamming the blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a physically bent blade can block the fan. In cold-weather heat-pump operation, ice can lock it up.
5. **Tripped internal overload** — A motor that overheated may cut out on its own thermal protector and refuse to restart until cool (or permanently if failing).
6. **Wiring or connection fault** — A loose, corroded, or chewed wire between the contactor and motor.
7. **Board or relay fault (Infinity/communicating units)** — Less common, but the control can fail to command the fan on higher-tier systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — none involve opening the electrical panel of the condenser or touching the capacitor.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor condenser breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause system issues and freezing — replace it if dirty.
- **Visible debris:** With the system fully **off**, look down through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest resting on the blade. Remove obvious debris from outside the unit only.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the coil or fan, switch the system to OFF (or FAN-only if available) and let it thaw fully before running again.
- **Condensate drain:** A full drain pan can trip a safety float and shut the system down; clear a clogged drain line if accessible.

Do **not** spin the fan with a stick to "help it start," open the electrical box, or discharge the capacitor yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will work through this sequence — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the symptom** and shut off power at the disconnect for safety.
2. **Test the capacitor** with a meter. A reading well below its microfarad (µF) rating confirms a failed cap — the number-one finding.
3. **Check the contactor** for pitted contacts and verify it's pulling in and passing voltage to the fan.
4. **Test the motor** — check windings for continuity/shorts, spin the blade by hand for seized bearings, and check the thermal overload.
5. **Verify voltage** reaching the motor with power restored, isolating whether the problem is upstream (contactor/wiring) or the motor itself.
6. **Inspect the blade and wiring** for damage or loose connections.
7. On **Infinity/communicating systems**, check for control-board faults and confirm the fan command is being sent.

A good tech tests the cheap parts (capacitor, contactor) before condemning the expensive motor.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't force the blade | Test and replace capacitor |
| No hum, no movement, blade turns freely | Contactor or wiring fault | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test contactor, wiring, voltage |
| Blade won't turn by hand (power off) | Seized motor bearings | Stop running the unit | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Fan runs then stops when hot | Failing motor / thermal overload | Shut down, let cool | Replace motor |
| Blade blocked or bent | Debris or damaged blade | Remove visible debris (power off) | Straighten/replace blade |
| No fan on Infinity unit, thermostat fault | Control board / relay | Note the fault message | Diagnose board & fan command |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor. Prices vary by region and unit tier (Infinity parts run higher).

- **Run capacitor:** $150–$300 installed. The part itself is $15–$40; you're paying for diagnosis and labor.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$650 installed for a standard PSC motor; ECM motors on Infinity units can exceed $700.
- **Fan blade:** $75–$200 installed.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200, often credited toward the repair.
- **Compressor damage** (if the unit was run for a long time with no fan): $1,500–$2,800+ — the reason you should shut it off immediately.

DIY on the capacitor or motor is possible for experienced homeowners but carries real electrocution risk; a charged capacitor can injure or kill even with power off. Most homeowners should hire a pro for these.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is not a stored fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Carrier condenser (the outdoor unit). The large fan on top pulls air across the condenser coil to reject heat that your system removed from inside the house. When the compressor runs but that fan sits still, heat has nowhere to go.

On most Carrier single-stage condensers there is no diagnostic display, so you won't see a number — you'll just hear a humming unit with a motionless fan, or a unit that goes quiet after tripping its internal overload. On **variable-speed Infinity systems**, the outdoor board *can* log motor or communication faults, and behavior differs from single-stage units; a technician reads those with an Infinity controller or service tool.

The key point: **do not keep running the system.** A compressor running with no condenser airflow overheats fast and can be permanently damaged.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor** — By far the most common. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it degrades, the fan hums but won't start, or only spins after a manual push.
2. **Failed condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, an open winding, or a burned-out motor. Often accompanied by a burnt smell or a motor that's seized when spun by hand (power off).
3. **Contactor not pulling in** — The 24V contactor may have pitted/welded contacts or a bad coil, so the fan (and compressor) never get full power.
4. **Tripped internal thermal overload** — The motor shut itself off after overheating; it may restart when cool, then fail again — a sign the motor or capacitor is on its way out.
5. **Debris or obstruction jamming the fan blade** — Leaves, ice, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Loss of 24V control signal** — A blown low-voltage fuse on the furnace/air handler board, or a thermostat/wiring fault, so the outdoor unit never gets the call.
7. **Control board or ECM fault (Infinity/variable-speed)** — On communicating systems, a board or motor-module failure can stop the fan; these units report differently than single-stage models.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no panel removal, no touching capacitors or wiring:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause freezing and shutdowns that mimic a fan problem. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (furnace/air handler) and outdoor AC breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets are unsafe.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the pull-out disconnect box next to the unit is fully seated.
- **Visible obstruction:** With the **system off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or ice on the blade. Clear loose debris only — don't force the blade.
- **Supply/return vents:** Confirm indoor vents are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan/float switch can shut the system down; check for standing water at the indoor unit.

If the fan is warm, humming, smells burnt, or won't start after these checks, shut the system off and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. Kill power at the disconnect and **discharge the run capacitor** before touching anything (capacitors hold a lethal charge).
2. **Test the capacitor** with a meter against its rated µF — a common, quick failure to confirm.
3. **Check the contactor** for pitted contacts and verify the 24V coil pulls it in.
4. **Test the fan motor** windings for open/shorted or grounded conditions, and spin it by hand for bearing drag.
5. **Verify 24V control voltage** reaching the outdoor unit and check the low-voltage fuse.
6. **Measure amp draw** on the motor to catch a motor pulling high amps and overheating.
7. On **Infinity/variable-speed systems**, connect the Carrier controller or service tool to read logged faults and check the motor module and communication.

Sanity check for a quote: replacing a capacitor is a sub-$300 job in most markets. If you're quoted $600+, it should be for a fan motor, contactor plus motor, or a communicating-board repair — ask which part failed.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin; starts if nudged | Weak/failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test & replace dual run capacitor |
| Fan seized, won't turn by hand (power off) | Failed motor / bad bearings | None — call pro | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Compressor & fan both dead, faint hum | Contactor not pulling in | Check breaker/disconnect | Test/replace contactor |
| Fan runs then stops after minutes | Thermal overload / failing motor | Turn system off | Diagnose motor & capacitor, replace |
| Blade blocked by debris or ice | Obstruction | Clear loose debris, power off | Inspect blade, check for coil freeze |
| Whole outdoor unit dead | Blown 24V fuse / lost signal | Reset breaker once | Test control voltage, replace fuse |
| Fan fault on Infinity display | Board/motor module fault | None — call pro | Read faults, test module/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (universal replacement):** $400 – $650
- **OEM Carrier fan motor:** $500 – $850+
- **Low-voltage fuse / minor wiring:** $100 – $250
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Infinity/communicating board or motor module:** $500 – $1,200+ (varies widely by model and parts availability)

DIY reset (breaker, clearing debris) costs $0. Capacitor and motor work involves high voltage and a charged capacitor, so it's rated **pro** here for good reason.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is not a numeric fault code — it's a physical symptom of the condenser (outdoor unit). The large fan on top of your Carrier outdoor unit pulls air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system pumps out of the house. When that fan stops turning, the refrigerant can't shed heat, head pressure climbs fast, and the compressor is at serious risk.

On most Carrier split systems the fan motor is a single-speed PSC (permanent split capacitor) motor that shares a **dual run capacitor** with the compressor. Infinity variable-speed condensers instead use an ECM/electronically commutated motor controlled by a board, which fails and is diagnosed differently — say so to any tech who assumes it's "just a capacitor."

Because live 240V and capacitor testing are involved, this is rated **pro**. There are a few safe checks below, but the actual repair should go to a licensed HVAC tech.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor** — By far the most common. The fan hums but won't start, or starts only if you nudge it. A bulged, leaking, or out-of-spec capacitor can't deliver starting torque.
2. **Seized or worn fan motor bearings** — The motor tries to run but the shaft is bound. Often the motor is hot to the touch or smells burnt.
3. **Burnt-out condenser fan motor** — Open windings or a failed internal thermal cutout. No hum, no movement.
4. **Contactor not pulling in** — If the contactor doesn't close, neither the compressor nor the fan gets power. Usually the compressor is also silent.
5. **Debris or ice jamming the blade** — Twigs, a bird, or a physically bent blade blocking rotation.
6. **Loss of 24V control signal** — A tripped safety, bad thermostat wire, or low-voltage fuse means the outdoor unit never gets the call to run.
7. **Failed control board (Infinity/ECM units only)** — On variable-speed units, the board or ECM motor module can fail while the compressor still tries to run.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do with the panel closed and power respected:

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and lower the setpoint 3–4°F below room temperature so the system actually gets a cooling call. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A badly clogged filter can cause airflow faults and freezing that shut things down. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the **outdoor disconnect/breaker**. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — that signals an electrical fault.
- **Visible debris:** With the unit fully off, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bent blade you can see. Do not reach in while power is on.
- **Ice on the coil:** If the indoor coil is iced, turn the system to FAN ONLY to thaw it (see our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide).
- **Turn it off:** If the compressor is running but the fan isn't, **shut the system off at the thermostat** to protect the compressor, then call a pro.

Do **not** open the electrical panel, spin the fan by hand while powered, discharge or replace a capacitor, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Carrier tech will work in this order — use it to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the call:** Verify 24V is reaching the contactor coil when there's a cooling call.
2. **Cut power and discharge the capacitor:** Safety first — the capacitor holds a dangerous charge.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter, comparing the FAN terminal reading to its rated microfarads (µF). A reading well below rating confirms it.
4. **Check the contactor:** Look for pitted/welded contacts and confirm it pulls in.
5. **Test the fan motor:** Check windings for continuity, resistance, and grounded windings; spin the shaft for bearing drag.
6. **Verify voltage** at the motor once the contactor closes.
7. **Infinity/ECM units:** Read board fault history and test the ECM motor module rather than a capacitor.

A thorough diagnosis takes 30–60 minutes. Be wary of a tech who replaces the motor without first ruling out the (far cheaper) capacitor.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin; starts if nudged | Weak/failed run capacitor | Shut system off; don't nudge repeatedly | Test and replace dual run capacitor |
| No hum, no movement; compressor also silent | Contactor not closing or no 24V | Reset breaker once; check thermostat | Test contactor, 24V circuit, low-voltage fuse |
| Motor hums, hot, smells burnt | Seized bearings / failed motor | Turn off immediately | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Power off, remove visible debris | Straighten/replace blade, inspect motor |
| Fan and compressor both dead, breaker trips again | Electrical fault or shorted component | Do not keep resetting | Isolate short, replace faulty part |
| Variable-speed unit, fan dead, board shows fault | ECM motor or control board (Infinity) | Note the fault code shown | Read board history, test ECM module/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$180 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$300
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$300
- **Condenser fan motor (PSC):** $350–$650
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150–$350
- **ECM motor / module (Infinity variable-speed):** $500–$1,100+
- **Control board (Infinity):** $400–$900

Prices vary by region, unit age, and parts availability. If your Carrier unit is under the standard 10-year parts warranty (with registration), you may only owe labor — ask the tech to check the serial number.

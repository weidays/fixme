## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered fault on most Carrier air conditioners — it's a symptom you observe at the condenser (the outdoor unit). The large fan on top of the outdoor cabinet is supposed to pull air across the condenser coil to reject heat. When it stops, the compressor may still run, but heat has nowhere to go.

On **Infinity-series** systems, a related fault may appear on the wall control (for example, a communication or motor fault). On **Comfort and Performance base models**, there is no display — you'll only notice warm air indoors, a humming outdoor unit, or the outdoor unit shutting down on its internal safeties.

**This is a "stop and shut off" situation.** Continuing to run the compressor with a dead fan is one of the fastest ways to destroy a compressor, the most expensive part in the system.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common. The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor (and compressor) the torque to start. When the fan side dies, the motor hums but won't spin, or spins only if nudged.
2. **Burned-out condenser fan motor** — Bearings seize or windings fail. The motor may be hot, silent, or tripping the breaker.
3. **Failed or pitted contactor** — The relay that sends 240V to the outdoor unit. Burned contacts can starve the fan of power.
4. **Debris or ice jamming the fan blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse / low-voltage fuse** — No power reaching the unit or the control board.
6. **Failed control board (Infinity/Performance boards)** — Less common; the board fails to command the fan.
7. **Wiring damage** — Rodent chews or corroded terminals at the motor or capacitor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these with the system **off**. Do **not** open the outdoor electrical panel or touch the capacitor — it can hold a lethal charge even when power is off.

- **Thermostat:** Set to Cool and drop the setpoint several degrees to confirm a call for cooling. Replace thermostat batteries if the display is blank.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause a frozen coil and odd behavior. Replace if dirty.
- **Visible obstructions:** With the unit **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or ice on the fan blade. Clear loose debris by hand — do not force a bent blade.
- **Ice on the coil or lines:** If you see ice, shut the AC off and run just the indoor fan to thaw it (see our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide).
- **Airflow around the unit:** Make sure there's at least 2 feet of clearance and the coil fins aren't packed with cottonwood or grass clippings.

If the fan still won't spin after these, it's an electrical fault — call a licensed HVAC tech.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the call and power** — Verifies 240V at the disconnect and 24V at the contactor coil.
2. **Test the run capacitor** — Discharges it safely, then reads microfarads (µF) with a meter. A fan-side reading far below the rating (printed as, e.g., 45+5 µF) confirms a bad cap. This is a 5-minute test.
3. **Check the contactor** — Looks for pitted/welded contacts and confirms it pulls in when energized.
4. **Test the motor** — With a good capacitor, if the fan still won't run, the tech checks motor windings for continuity and shorts, and spins the shaft to feel for seized bearings.
5. **Inspect wiring and board** — Traces terminals for burns or breaks; on Infinity/Performance units, pulls any logged faults.

A good tech starts with the cheapest likely cause (capacitor) before condemning the motor. Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to motor or board replacement without a capacitor test.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't start; spins if nudged | Failed run capacitor | Shut off; do not run | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan silent, motor hot to touch | Seized/burned fan motor | Shut off at breaker | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Outdoor unit fully dead | Tripped breaker, contactor, or fuse | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, fuses, 24V supply |
| Fan blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Clear loose debris, power off | Straighten/replace blade, balance |
| Breaker trips immediately on start | Shorted motor or wiring | Stop resetting | Diagnose short, replace faulted part |
| Warm air indoors, unit humming | Compressor running, no fan | Shut off to protect compressor | Full electrical diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $12–$40 DIY part; **$150–$350** installed by a pro.
- **Contactor replacement:** $10–$30 part; **$150–$300** installed.
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** **$350–$650** installed (universal motors are cheaper; OEM Carrier motors cost more).
- **Fan blade replacement:** **$150–$350** installed.
- **Control board (Infinity/Performance):** **$400–$900** installed, depending on model.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** **$90–$180**, often credited toward the repair.

If a tech finds the compressor was damaged by running without the fan, repairs can exceed **$1,500–$2,800** — the reason to shut down immediately when the fan stops.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a lit error code on most Carrier equipment — it's a symptom you observe at the condenser (the outdoor unit). The large fan on top of the unit is supposed to pull air across the condenser coil to dump heat outside. When it stops turning, the system can't reject heat.

On single-stage Carrier condensers there is no display to read; you diagnose by observation and electrical testing. On **Infinity communicating systems**, the indoor control or thermostat may log a related fault, but the wording varies by board revision, so don't assume a specific code number.

The important thing: **if the compressor is running but the fan is not, turn the system off.** Running without condenser airflow can destroy the compressor — by far the most expensive part of the system.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** The single most common cause. The dual capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it weakens, the fan may hum, stutter, or sit still while the compressor runs.
2. **Seized or worn fan motor.** Bearings dry out and lock up, especially on older units. A motor that's hot, noisy, or won't spin freely by hand (with power OFF) is likely done.
3. **Contactor not pulling in or welded contacts.** The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts cause erratic or no fan operation.
4. **Jammed fan blade.** Debris, a bent blade, or ice can physically block rotation. Sometimes an easy fix, sometimes a sign the motor already burned out fighting the jam.
5. **Tripped internal overload / thermal protector.** A motor that overheats shuts itself down and may restart when cool — an intermittent no-fan symptom.
6. **Loss of low-voltage (24V) signal.** A control-board fault, bad transformer, or wiring problem can keep the contactor from energizing at all — neither fan nor compressor run.
7. **Control board fault (Infinity/communicating units).** Less common, but a failed outdoor control module can stop the fan output specifically.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the electrical access panel, touch the capacitor, or spin the fan by hand with power on.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the indoor coil to freeze, which can look like an outdoor problem. Replace if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box next to the condenser should be fully seated.
- **Visible obstructions:** With the system OFF at the thermostat, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest blocking the blade. Clear only what you can reach without removing panels.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or float switch trip can shut the whole system down; check for standing water at the indoor unit.
- **Don't repeatedly reset** a unit that keeps tripping or a fan that keeps stalling — you risk compressor damage.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical, safe sequence. Knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the call for cooling** and verifies 24V is reaching the contactor.
2. **Kills power and discharges the capacitor** before touching anything internal.
3. **Tests the capacitor** with a meter (microfarad reading vs. the rating printed on it). A weak fan section confirms the cheapest fix first.
4. **Checks the contactor** for pitting, chatter, and proper voltage in/out.
5. **Tests the fan motor** — resistance across windings, checks for grounded windings, and spins the blade by hand to feel for seized bearings.
6. **Checks the fan blade and shaft** for jams or damage.
7. **On Infinity systems,** reads any stored faults and confirms the board is sending the fan output.

A tech who wants to replace the motor without first testing the capacitor is skipping the most common, cheapest cause — ask them to show you the capacitor reading.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan sits still | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off | Discharge & test capacitor, replace |
| Fan won't turn, feels stiff by hand (power off) | Seized fan motor | Turn system off | Test windings, replace motor & capacitor |
| Nothing runs outside, breaker OK | No 24V / contactor not pulling in | Check breaker & disconnect | Test transformer, contactor, wiring |
| Fan tries to start then stops, buzzes | Weak capacitor or failing motor | Turn system off | Meter test, replace failing part |
| Fan blade visibly blocked | Debris or bent blade | Clear debris (power off) | Straighten/replace blade, check motor |
| Fan works then quits after a while | Motor thermal overload | Turn system off, let cool | Replace overheating motor |
| Fan output dead on Infinity unit | Outdoor board fault | Note any error text | Diagnose/replace control module |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor. Regional rates and Infinity boards push toward the high end.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $400 (part is cheap; labor and trip charge dominate)
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $300 – $600 (includes a new capacitor)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Low-voltage transformer:** $150 – $350
- **Infinity outdoor control board:** $400 – $800+

If the fan stalling has already damaged the compressor, costs jump to **$1,500 – $3,000+**, which is why shutting the unit off early matters.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Carrier condenser (the outdoor unit). The condenser fan pulls air across the outdoor coil to reject the heat your system pulls out of the house. When it stops, the compressor loses its ability to dump heat, pressure climbs, and the unit will either shut down on a high-pressure or thermal trip — or, worse, cook the compressor.

Most Carrier residential condensers use a single-phase permanent split capacitor (PSC) fan motor driven by a **dual run capacitor**, switched on by a **contactor** and commanded by the indoor control board or thermostat. A failure anywhere in that chain can leave the blade dead while the rest of the unit hums.

Because this often involves live 240V wiring, a charged capacitor, and refrigerant-side pressures, this is rated a **pro** repair. The safe checks below are the limit of DIY.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The single most common cause. A weak or blown dual run capacitor can't give the fan motor the phase kick it needs to start. Classic sign: the unit hums but the fan sits still (and sometimes spins if nudged — do not do this with power on).
2. **Seized or burned-out fan motor** — Bearings fail or windings burn open. The motor may be hot, smell scorched, or trip the breaker.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse** — If the whole outdoor unit is silent, power may be cut at the breaker or the pull-out disconnect by the condenser.
4. **Failed contactor** — The relay that sends 240V to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts can leave the unit partially or fully dead.
5. **Control board / thermostat not calling for cooling** — On Infinity and communicating systems, a board fault or lost communication can stop the outdoor call. Behavior here varies by model.
6. **Debris jamming the blade** — Sticks, cottonwood, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
7. **Wiring / low-voltage fault** — Chewed wires, loose 24V control leads, or corroded terminals.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these only; leave everything behind a panel to a pro.

- **Thermostat**: Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. If it's a digital stat, replace the batteries.
- **Indoor air filter**: A clogged filter can cause freeze-ups and shutdowns that mimic outdoor faults. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker**: Check both the indoor air-handler/furnace breaker and the outdoor AC breaker. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — that's a fault, not a fluke. Call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect**: There's usually a pull-out box on the wall near the condenser. Make sure it's fully seated (do not open it or touch fuses).
- **Visible vents and airflow**: Ensure supply and return registers indoors are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate**: Check that the drain pan isn't overflowing and the float switch (if present) hasn't tripped, which can stop the system.
- **Look, don't touch**: From a safe distance, check whether the outdoor coil is buried in leaves or grass clippings. Do **not** reach into the fan or open the top grille.

If the unit hums but the fan won't turn, shut it off at the thermostat and call a technician — running it risks the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified HVAC tech will typically:

1. **Cut and lock out power** at the disconnect, then verify zero voltage.
2. **Discharge and test the run capacitor** with a meter — checking microfarads against the rating printed on it (e.g., 45+5 µF). A weak cap is the usual culprit.
3. **Check the contactor** for pitted contacts and confirm it pulls in when a cooling call is present.
4. **Test the fan motor** — resistance across windings, checking for an open or shorted winding and a seized shaft by spinning it by hand (power off).
5. **Verify the 24V control signal** from the board/thermostat is reaching the outdoor unit.
6. On **Infinity/communicating systems**, pull fault history and check for communication or board faults.
7. **Inspect wiring** for rodent damage, loose lugs, and corrosion.

A good quote names the specific failed part and its measured reading. Be cautious of a diagnosis that jumps straight to "new motor" or "new unit" without testing the capacitor first — the cap is cheap and the most likely fault.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit hums, fan doesn't spin | Failed run capacitor | Turn off at thermostat; don't nudge blade | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan dead, motor hot/burnt smell | Seized or burned fan motor | Shut off system, don't run it | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Whole outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker / blown fuse | Reset breaker once; check disconnect seated | Diagnose fault, replace fuse |
| Intermittent fan, buzzing box | Pitted/stuck contactor | Leave panel closed | Replace contactor |
| Fan won't start, no call to unit | Board or thermostat fault | Check stat settings/batteries | Test 24V signal, board diagnostics |
| Blade won't turn, visible debris | Jammed blade | Turn off power; report to tech | Clear debris, check for bent blade |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted motor/winding fault | Stop resetting; call pro | Isolate short, replace component |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor. Regional rates and model complexity vary.

- **Diagnostic / service call**: $75–$200 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Run capacitor replacement**: $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement**: $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement**: $350–$650+ (universal motors are cheaper; Carrier OEM or ECM motors on higher-end units cost more)
- **Control board (communicating/Infinity)**: $400–$900+
- **Breaker reset / minor wiring fix**: $0 DIY to ~$200 for a repaired connection

If your unit is old (12–15+ years) and needs an expensive motor or board, ask whether repair is worth it versus replacement — especially if it still uses R-22 refrigerant.

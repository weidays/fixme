## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered fault code — it's a symptom you can see and hear at the outdoor condenser unit. The compressor may be humming while the top-mounted fan blade sits still, or the whole unit may be silent.

The outdoor (condenser) fan pulls air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system just pulled out of the house. If it stops, the refrigerant can't shed heat, pressures spike, and your compressor is at serious risk. Carrier units don't have a dedicated "fan stuck" display code the way furnaces flash ignition faults, so diagnosis is done by observation and electrical testing.

**Turn the system off at the thermostat now** if the fan isn't turning but you hear the unit buzzing or humming. Continuing to run it can destroy the compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. When it weakens or dies, the fan won't start (often humming) even though the compressor tries to run.
2. **Failed condenser fan motor** — Bearings seize or windings burn out. Often smells hot or the motor is too hot to touch.
3. **Contactor not pulling in or contacts burned** — The contactor is the relay that sends 240V to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts interrupt power.
4. **Debris or ice jamming the blade** — Sticks, a bird's nest, or a bent blade physically blocks rotation.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse** — No power reaching the outdoor unit at all (fan and compressor both dead).
6. **Low-voltage / control signal problem** — A bad thermostat wire, failed transformer, or control board issue means the contactor never gets its 24V call.
7. **Loose or corroded wiring** — Vibration and weather loosen spade connectors at the motor or capacitor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without opening the live electrical panel of the condenser:

- **Thermostat**: Set it to Cool and lower the setpoint several degrees. Confirm the indoor blower runs.
- **Air filter**: A clogged filter can freeze the indoor coil and cause odd shutdowns. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker**: Check the AC breaker in your main panel and the outdoor disconnect box. If a breaker is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop — call a pro.
- **Visible debris**: With the unit powered **off at the disconnect**, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest touching the blade. Remove obvious debris only — do not reach into the electrical section.
- **Ice**: If the coil or lines are frozen, shut the system off and let it thaw (see the frozen coil guide below).
- **Vents**: Make sure indoor supply and return vents are open and unblocked.

Do **not** open the capacitor compartment, discharge components, or push the fan blade to start it. Capacitors hold a dangerous charge even after power is off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. **Confirm the call and voltage** — Verify 24V is reaching the contactor and 240V is at the disconnect.
2. **Check the contactor** — Watch whether it pulls in; measure line voltage across the load contacts to spot burned contacts.
3. **Test the run capacitor** — With power off and the cap safely discharged, measure microfarads (µF) against the rating printed on the label. A reading well below spec confirms a bad capacitor.
4. **Test the fan motor** — Check windings for resistance and shorts, spin the blade for bearing drag, and feel for overheating.
5. **Inspect wiring** — Look for loose spade terminals, rodent damage, and corrosion at the motor and capacitor.
6. **Verify after repair** — Confirm the fan starts on its own and check amp draw against the motor's rated amps.

Testing the capacitor first is standard because it's cheap and the most likely culprit. Be wary of a quote for a new motor without a capacitor test being mentioned.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan won't turn | Weak/dead run capacitor | Shut off at thermostat; don't run unit | Test µF, replace capacitor |
| Fan and compressor both dead | Tripped breaker / blown fuse / contactor | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Replace fuse/contactor, find root cause |
| Fan turns hard by hand, hot smell | Seized bearings / burned motor | Power off, don't run | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Fan blocked, buzzing | Debris or bent blade | Power off, remove visible debris | Straighten/replace blade, check motor |
| Fan starts sometimes, stops | Failing capacitor or loose wiring | Power off | Test cap, tighten/repair connections |
| No 24V click at unit | Thermostat wire / transformer / board | Check thermostat batteries & setting | Trace low-voltage circuit |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included unless noted:

- **Run capacitor replacement**: $130–$300 (part is $15–$40 DIY, but capacitor discharge is a shock hazard).
- **Contactor replacement**: $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor replacement**: $300–$650 depending on motor type (PSC vs ECM) and tonnage.
- **Fan blade replacement**: $100–$250.
- **Disconnect fuse / breaker**: $50–$200.
- **Diagnostic / service call**: $75–$200, often applied toward the repair.

ECM (variable-speed) motors on higher-end Infinity units cost more than standard PSC motors. A capacitor fix is by far the cheapest and most common outcome.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a symptom you can see and hear at your Carrier condenser (the big unit outside). When cooling is running normally, the compressor hums and the top-mounted fan spins to blow heat out of the coil. If the compressor is running but the fan sits still — or the whole unit is dead — the system can't reject heat.

This matters because the compressor and fan usually share power. If the fan stops while the compressor keeps running, refrigerant pressure and temperature climb quickly. Carrier units often have internal overload protection that will trip the compressor to protect it, but you should not rely on that. **Turn cooling off at the thermostat** until the problem is diagnosed.

Most Carrier condensers show no diagnostic display for this. Higher-end **Infinity** and **Greenspeed** systems communicate faults to the indoor control or Infinity thermostat, so on those units you may see a related fault message — behavior varies by model.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The single most common cause. The dual capacitor provides starting torque to the fan (and often the compressor). When it weakens, the fan won't start on its own but may spin if nudged.
2. **Seized or burned-out fan motor** — Worn bearings or a failed winding. The motor may hum, get hot, or do nothing at all.
3. **Contactor not pulling in** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit. A pitted, stuck, or non-energized contactor means no power reaches the fan or compressor.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse** — No power to the whole outdoor unit.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Leaves, twigs, or ice can physically block the blade.
6. **Low-voltage / control-board fault** — A bad 24V signal, transformer, or (on Infinity/Greenspeed units) a control board issue.
7. **Wiring or connection failure** — Corroded, loose, or chewed wires at the motor or contactor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not remove the electrical access panel** — the capacitor holds a dangerous charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can freeze the indoor coil and shut things down; swap it if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated.
- **Visible debris:** With the unit powered OFF at the breaker, look through the top grille for leaves or sticks near the blade. Do not reach into or dismantle the unit.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the unit or lines, turn cooling off and run the fan-only mode to thaw — see our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan can trip a float switch and stop the system; check for standing water at the indoor unit.

If cooling doesn't return after these, it's a pro job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical, safe sequence — this is what a fair quote reflects:

1. **Verify power** at the disconnect and contactor with a meter.
2. **Discharge and test the capacitor** with a multimeter, comparing the reading to the rated microfarads printed on the label. A weak or dead cap is confirmed here.
3. **Check the contactor** — measuring the 24V coil signal and inspecting the contacts for pitting or sticking.
4. **Test the fan motor** — checking windings for continuity/shorts, spinning the shaft by hand for bearing drag, and checking for an overheated motor.
5. **Inspect wiring** at the motor, contactor, and control board for damage or corrosion.
6. **On Infinity/Greenspeed models,** pull stored fault codes from the control board or thermostat and check board outputs.

Ask which of these they actually tested. A tech who replaces a motor without first ruling out a $25 capacitor is guessing.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan doesn't spin; spins if nudged | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't nudge repeatedly | Discharge & replace capacitor |
| Whole outdoor unit is dead | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, or contactor | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test contactor, fuses, transformer |
| Fan hums but won't turn | Seized motor or bad capacitor | Turn off to prevent overheating | Test/replace motor and capacitor |
| Fan blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Power off, remove visible debris via grille | Clear jam, inspect/replace blade |
| Clicking at contactor, no start | Weak contactor coil or low voltage | None — call a pro | Replace contactor, check 24V circuit |
| Fault message on Infinity thermostat | Control/board or motor fault | Note the message | Read board codes, diagnose per model |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices, including parts and labor. Your area and unit size affect the total.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor (with matching capacitor):** $300 – $650
- **Blade replacement:** $150 – $300
- **Wiring/connection repair:** $120 – $300
- **Control board (Infinity/Greenspeed):** $400 – $900+
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80 – $180 (often applied to the repair)

The capacitor is the cheapest and most common fix — it's worth having a tech confirm the cap before authorizing an expensive motor. A single dead capacitor is the best-case outcome here.

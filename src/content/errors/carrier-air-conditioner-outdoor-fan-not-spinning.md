## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is not a numeric fault code on Carrier systems — it's a physical symptom you observe at the outdoor condenser unit. The compressor may hum or run while the large fan on top sits still, or the whole unit may be silent.

The outdoor (condenser) fan pulls air across the coil to dump the heat your AC removed from the house. If it stops, refrigerant pressure and temperature climb fast. On many Carrier units the compressor will then trip on an internal overload or a high-pressure switch. **Infinity and some Performance systems with communicating controls may log a related pressure or system fault at the thermostat**, but the classic Comfort-series condenser has no display and simply stops cooling.

Because the most common causes involve high-voltage capacitors and motors, this is rated a **pro** repair. A charged capacitor can shock you even with power off.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The single most common cause. The dual capacitor starts and runs both the fan and compressor. When the fan section weakens, the fan won't start on its own (but may spin if nudged).
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, overheating, or age. The motor won't turn even with a good capacitor.
3. **Bad contactor** — The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit can pit, weld shut, or fail to close, so the fan (and often compressor) get no power.
4. **Tripped high-pressure or compressor overload** — A dirty coil or prior fan failure can trip a safety, shutting the unit down until it cools/resets.
5. **Debris or ice jamming the blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Control board or relay fault (Infinity/communicating units)** — On board-controlled systems, a fan relay or the board itself can fail.
7. **Low-voltage / thermostat / wiring issue** — No 24V call reaching the contactor, blown low-voltage fuse, or rodent-chewed wire.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not open the electrical access panel on the condenser** — capacitors hold a dangerous charge.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Indoor filter:** A clogged filter can freeze the evaporator and shut things down. Swap it if dirty (see our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide).
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor air-handler/furnace breaker and the outdoor condenser breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Visible debris:** Look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a cottonwood mat on the coil. Do not reach in. Clear only loose debris with the power off at the disconnect.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a float switch and stop the system. Check the indoor pan and drain line.
- **Give it time:** If a safety tripped, turn the thermostat off for 30 minutes to let the unit cool, then restart once. Repeated resets can lock out or damage the compressor — don't do it.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence. Knowing it helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirms the call:** Verifies 24V is reaching the contactor coil (rules out thermostat/low-voltage issues).
2. **Checks the contactor:** Looks for pitted contacts and confirms it's pulling in and passing line voltage.
3. **Tests the capacitor** with a meter — comparing measured microfarads (µF) to the rating printed on the can. A weak fan side confirms this cheap fix.
4. **Tests the motor:** Checks windings for resistance/continuity and confirms the motor is not seized by hand (power off).
5. **Checks pressures and safeties:** Verifies the high-pressure switch and compressor overload aren't tripped, and inspects the coil for restriction.
6. **On Infinity/communicating units:** Reads any stored fault codes at the thermostat and checks the board's fan relay output.

Expect a diagnostic fee of **$80–$180**, often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan still, compressor hums | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't nudge blade with power on | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan spins if pushed by hand (power off) | Weak capacitor | Shut down, call pro | Confirm µF, replace capacitor |
| Whole unit silent, no hum | Bad contactor / no power / breaker | Reset breaker once | Test contactor & 24V, replace |
| Fan struggles, then stops; hot motor | Failing fan motor | Shut down | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Blade jammed by debris | Physical obstruction | Clear loose debris, power off | Inspect, straighten/replace blade |
| Unit ran fine then quit in heat | Tripped high-pressure/overload | Off 30 min, restart once | Clean coil, find root cause |
| No cooling, thermostat blank | Batteries / low-voltage fuse | Replace batteries | Check board & fuse (Infinity) |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Diagnostic fee:** $80–$180 (often applied to repair)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$300 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$650 installed (universal vs. OEM affects price)
- **Fan blade:** $80–$200 installed
- **Control board (Infinity/communicating):** $400–$900+ installed
- **Coil cleaning (if a safety tripped from restriction):** $100–$250

Capacitor and contactor jobs are quick and affordable. A motor or board replacement is the pricier end — and on an older unit, weigh it against overall system age and refrigerant type.

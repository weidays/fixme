## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a lettered fault code — most Carrier air conditioners don't flash a specific number for it. Your condenser (the outdoor unit) should have two things running when the AC calls for cooling: the compressor and the outdoor fan motor. When the fan blade on top of the unit sits still while the system is trying to cool, the unit can't shed the heat it's pulling from your home.

This matters because the outdoor fan cools the condenser coil. Without airflow, refrigerant pressure and temperature climb fast. The compressor's internal overload protector will eventually shut it down to save itself — but repeated cycling like this can cook the compressor, which is the single most expensive part in the system. That's why this is rated a **pro** repair: the safe checks are limited, and most real fixes involve capacitors, motors, or line voltage.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run/start capacitor.** The most common cause by far. The dual-run capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. A weak or dead capacitor lets the motor hum but not spin, or not start at all.
2. **Seized or failed condenser fan motor.** Bearings wear out, or the windings fail. If the blade won't turn freely by hand (power off), the motor may be seized.
3. **Bad contactor.** The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts can interrupt power to the fan.
4. **Tripped compressor overload / high-pressure cutout.** Some units cut the fan and compressor together when a safety trips.
5. **Debris jamming the blade.** Sticks, ice, or a wasp nest can physically block the fan.
6. **Control board or relay fault (variable-speed / Infinity models).** On ECM-driven fans, a failed module or board relay can stop the fan.
7. **Loose or burned wiring** at the motor, capacitor, or contactor terminals.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Keep this short — the condenser carries 240V, so most internal checks are for a pro.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is well below room temperature. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Filter:** A clogged indoor filter can cause freezing and knock-on problems; replace it if dirty.
- **Visible debris:** With the AC **off at the disconnect**, look through the top grille for sticks, leaves, or ice on the blade. Clear loose debris only — do not reach in with power on.
- **Ice:** If the outdoor coil or blade is iced over, turn the AC off (fan-only at the thermostat, or off entirely) and let it thaw. See our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide.

Do **not** open the electrical panel, discharge a capacitor, or spin the fan by hand while powered. If nothing above restores the fan, call an HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the call.** Verifies the thermostat is calling for cooling and 24V is reaching the outdoor unit.
2. **Check the capacitor.** Measures the fan's capacitance section against the printed µF rating (e.g. 45+5). Out-of-spec capacitors are replaced — this is the most common finding.
3. **Test the motor.** Checks the fan motor windings for continuity/shorts and spins the blade by hand (power off) to feel for seized bearings.
4. **Inspect the contactor.** Looks for pitted contacts and verifies it pulls in and passes voltage to the fan.
5. **Verify line voltage** at the motor terminals with the unit running.
6. **Check safeties/pressures** on units with high-pressure switches, and inspect the control board on variable-speed/Infinity systems.

Ask which specific part tested bad. "It's the capacitor" should come with a measured µF reading; "it's the motor" should come with a no-spin or open-winding result.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't start | Failed capacitor | Turn unit off, don't force blade | Test and replace dual-run capacitor |
| Fan starts if nudged with a stick | Weak capacitor | Shut off, note it for the tech | Replace capacitor |
| Blade won't turn freely by hand (power off) | Seized fan motor | Leave off, call pro | Replace condenser fan motor |
| No hum at all, compressor also silent | Bad contactor or lost 24V | Reset breaker once, check thermostat | Test contactor and control circuit |
| Fan blocked by debris or ice | Jam or frozen coil | Clear loose debris (power off); let ice thaw | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Fan stops after running a few minutes | Overload / high-pressure trip | Turn off, don't reset repeatedly | Find root cause: dirty coil, low charge |
| Fan dead on Infinity/variable-speed unit | Board or ECM module fault | Note model number for tech | Test board relay / ECM module |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Your rates vary by region and by whether you're on a diagnostic-plus-repair or flat-rate plan.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180
- **Dual-run capacitor:** $130 – $300 installed (the part is cheap; you're paying for the visit and expertise)
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300 – $650 installed, depending on horsepower and whether a universal or OEM Carrier motor is used
- **Fan blade:** $80 – $250 if damaged
- **Control board / ECM module (variable-speed / Infinity):** $400 – $900+
- **Wiring/terminal repair:** $100 – $250

If a tech quotes a compressor replacement stemming from a fan that ran dead too long, get a second opinion and weigh it against the age of the system — on a 12+ year old unit, replacement may make more sense.

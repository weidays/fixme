## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numeric fault code on most Carrier units — it's a physical symptom you observe at the condenser (the outdoor unit). The compressor may be humming while the fan blade sits still, or the whole unit may be silent.

The outdoor fan pulls air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system removed from inside. If it stops, the refrigerant can't cool down, head pressure spikes, and the compressor overheats within minutes. This is why we rate it a **pro** issue: continuing to run the unit risks a compressor failure, which is by far the most expensive part on the system.

On **Infinity** models with a communicating control board, you may see a related status or fault code in the thermostat's service menu. On **Comfort and Performance** series, there's usually no code — just a fan that won't turn.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The single most common cause. The dual capacitor supplies starting torque to both the fan and compressor. When the fan side weakens, the blade won't start on its own (but may spin if nudged).
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — Bearings fail or windings burn open. The motor hums, gets hot, or does nothing at all.
3. **Failed contactor** — The relay that sends power to the outdoor unit. Pitted or stuck contacts prevent the fan and compressor from getting line voltage.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — A tripped outdoor disconnect or breaker cuts all power to the condenser.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — A stick, ice, or nesting material physically blocking rotation.
6. **Control board or relay fault** — More common on Infinity/communicating systems where a board switches the fan.
7. **Compressor overload lockout** — After overheating, the internal overload may cut power, sometimes affecting the fan circuit too.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here and call a pro if none resolve it.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Air filter:** A severely clogged filter can trigger protective shutdowns and freeze-ups. Replace if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check the AC breaker in your electrical panel and the outdoor disconnect box near the condenser. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Visible debris:** With the unit powered OFF at the disconnect, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or ice on the blade. Clear anything obvious. Never insert fingers or tools while power is on.
- **Vents and airflow:** Make sure indoor supply/return vents are open and unobstructed, and that the outdoor unit has clear space around it.
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan or clogged drain can trip a safety switch on some setups. Check that the drain line is clear.

Do **not** open the electrical access panel, touch the capacitor, or attempt to spin or "help" the fan while the unit is powered. Capacitors store a lethal charge even after power is off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will work in roughly this order — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Kill power** at the disconnect and confirm with a meter.
2. **Discharge and test the capacitor** with a multimeter, comparing the reading against the microfarad (µF) rating printed on the label. A weak or dead cap is the usual culprit.
3. **Test the fan motor** — check windings for continuity and resistance, and spin the blade by hand (powered off) to feel for seized bearings.
4. **Inspect the contactor** for pitting, and test that the coil pulls in and passes voltage when calling for cooling.
5. **Verify incoming voltage** at the disconnect and control (24V) circuit.
6. **On Infinity/communicating units,** pull any stored fault codes and check the control board relays.
7. **Measure pressures and amp draw** once the fan runs, to confirm the compressor wasn't damaged by prior overheating.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan still | Failed run capacitor | Turn unit off; don't nudge blade repeatedly | Discharge & replace capacitor |
| Fan spins if pushed by hand | Weak capacitor | Turn off and stop running it | Test µF, replace capacitor |
| Unit totally silent | Tripped breaker/disconnect or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test power, replace contactor |
| Motor hums, hot, no spin | Seized/burned fan motor | Shut down immediately | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Blade blocked/rubbing | Debris or bent blade | Clear debris with power OFF | Straighten/replace blade or motor |
| Infinity code logged | Board relay or motor fault | Note the code | Read code, test board/motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor. Prices vary by region and unit access.

- **DIY breaker reset / debris clearing:** $0
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $400 – $650+ (higher for Infinity/ECM motors)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Control board (Infinity/communicating):** $500 – $900+
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $180, often credited toward the repair

If the compressor was run long with the fan stopped, ask the tech to verify it's undamaged — a failed compressor can run $1,500–$3,000+ and may push you toward replacing the outdoor unit.

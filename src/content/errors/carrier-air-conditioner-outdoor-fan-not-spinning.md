## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a specific numbered fault on most Carrier air conditioners — it's a symptom you can see and hear. When your system calls for cooling, the outdoor (condenser) unit should power up: the compressor hums and the large fan on top pulls air through the coil to dump heat outside.

If the compressor is running but the fan blade sits still, the unit can't reject heat. Pressures and temperatures climb fast, and the system will often trip out on its internal high-pressure or thermal protection — or, worse, damage the compressor.

On **Carrier Infinity** systems, the control board may log a related fault (motor or communication error) that you can read at the wall control. On **Comfort and Performance single-stage units**, there's usually no display — you're diagnosing purely by observation and meter readings.

**Do not keep running the system.** Turn it off at the thermostat until the fan issue is resolved.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run/start capacitor.** The single most common cause. The dual-run capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it weakens, the fan won't start on its own but may spin if nudged.
2. **Burned-out condenser fan motor.** Bearings seize or windings fail. The motor may be silent, humming, or hot to the touch.
3. **Failed contactor.** The relay that sends 240V to the fan and compressor can have pitted or stuck contacts, so power never reaches the motor.
4. **Debris jamming the fan blade.** Sticks, leaves, or ice can physically block the blade.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse.** Partial power can run the compressor's control circuit but not the fan, or the whole unit may be starved of power.
6. **Bad fan motor wiring or a loose connection.** Corroded or vibrated-loose spade connectors.
7. **Control board fault (Infinity/two-stage units).** Less common, but the board can fail to command the fan.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks you can do without opening the live electrical cabinet:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the temperature is below room temp. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter won't stop the outdoor fan, but it can cause freeze-ups and shutdowns that mimic other problems. Replace it if dirty.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Visible debris:** With the unit powered **off at the breaker and disconnect**, look through the top grille for sticks or leaves jamming the blade. Do not reach into the cabinet or touch electrical parts.
- **Ice:** If the outdoor coil or fan is iced, turn the system off and let it thaw. See our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide for the underlying cause.
- **Vents:** Make sure supply and return vents inside are open and unblocked.

If none of that helps, the problem is almost certainly inside the electrical cabinet — capacitor, motor, or contactor — which is pro territory.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works in a predictable order, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirms the call for cooling** and verifies 24V is reaching the contactor coil.
2. **Checks incoming 240V power** at the disconnect and contactor.
3. **Tests the capacitor** with a meter's capacitance function, comparing the reading to the rating printed on the label (e.g., 45+5 µF). A reading well below spec = replace.
4. **Tests the contactor** for pulled-in contacts and proper voltage on the load side.
5. **Tests the fan motor** — checks windings for continuity/shorts, spins the blade to feel for seized bearings, and confirms voltage is actually reaching the motor.
6. **On Infinity units,** reads any board fault codes at the wall control.

The whole diagnosis should take 20–45 minutes. Be wary of a quote to replace the motor without a capacitor test first — the capacitor is cheaper and fails far more often.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan still; spins if nudged | Failed capacitor | Shut off system; don't run it | Test & replace dual-run capacitor |
| Fan silent, no hum, no movement | Dead motor or no power to it | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test motor windings; replace motor |
| Whole outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker, bad contactor, blown fuse | Reset breaker once only | Test contactor and low-voltage fuse |
| Fan blade physically stuck | Debris or ice jam | Power off, clear visible debris | Inspect blade, bearings, shaft |
| Fan runs then stops, unit trips | Overheating motor / high pressure | Turn off, let cool | Diagnose motor & system pressures |
| Infinity control shows fault code | Board or motor fault | Note the code | Read code, test board and motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80 – $200
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150 – $400 installed (part is often under $40)
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300 – $650 installed, depending on horsepower; usually replaced with a matching capacitor
- **Low-voltage fuse / wiring repair:** $100 – $250
- **Infinity control board:** $400 – $900 installed

If your unit is old (12+ years) and uses R-22 refrigerant, weigh a costly motor or board repair against replacement — ask your tech for both numbers.

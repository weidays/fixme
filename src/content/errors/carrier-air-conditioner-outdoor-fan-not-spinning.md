## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a Carrier fault code — it's a physical symptom. On your outdoor condenser unit, the large top or side fan pulls air across the condenser coil to reject heat from the refrigerant. When that fan stops turning while the system is calling for cooling, heat has nowhere to go.

Most Carrier condensers don't display an error for this on the outdoor unit. Infinity-communicating systems may log a related fault (like a compressor high-pressure trip) at the indoor board or wall control, but the fan itself has no dedicated code on single- and two-stage units.

**This is time-sensitive.** A running compressor with a stopped fan builds head pressure fast and can damage the compressor. Turn the system off at the thermostat until it's fixed.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — The single most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor the electrical "kick" to start and run. Many Carrier units use a dual capacitor serving both the fan and compressor. A bulging, leaking, or out-of-spec capacitor leaves the fan humming or dead.
2. **Failed condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, a burned winding, or an open thermal overload. The motor may be hot to the touch, seized, or noisy before it quits.
3. **Contactor not pulling in** — A pitted, stuck, or failed contactor can interrupt power to the fan (and compressor). Sometimes only partial contact points fail.
4. **Debris or ice jamming the blade** — Sticks, leaves, or a bent blade physically blocking rotation. Ice buildup can also stall the fan.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — Loss of power to the outdoor unit or the 24V control circuit.
6. **Control board / relay fault** — On two-stage and Infinity units, a board relay or communication fault can stop the outdoor fan command.
7. **Wiring damage** — Rodent-chewed or corroded wires at the motor, capacitor, or contactor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor disconnect breaker. Reset a tripped breaker once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter can cause the whole system to short-cycle or freeze — replace if dirty.
- **Outdoor unit for debris:** With the system OFF at the thermostat, look through the fan grille for leaves, sticks, or a bent blade. Clear visible debris by hand.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the coil or lines, turn the AC off and run just the indoor fan for a few hours to thaw. See our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide.
- **Listen:** With cooling called, does the outdoor unit hum but not spin? A hum with no rotation strongly suggests a capacitor.

**Do not** open the electrical panel on the condenser. Capacitors store a dangerous charge even with power off, and there are exposed line-voltage terminals inside. That's pro territory.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. **Kill power** at the disconnect and verify it's dead before opening the access panel.
2. **Safely discharge and test the capacitor** with a meter, comparing microfarad reading to the rating printed on it (e.g., 40/5 μF). Out-of-spec = replace.
3. **Check the contactor** — measure voltage in and out, inspect the contacts, verify the 24V coil pulls it in.
4. **Test the fan motor** — check windings for continuity/short, spin the blade for bearing play, feel for a tripped thermal overload.
5. **Verify line and control voltage** at the unit and inspect wiring for damage.
6. **On Infinity/communicating systems**, read fault history at the wall control and confirm the outdoor board is commanding the fan.
7. **Check pressures** after repair to confirm the compressor wasn't damaged by running hot.

A capacitor is a quick swap; a fan motor takes longer because of blade removal and wiring. If a quote jumps straight to "new motor" without a capacitor test, ask why.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin; compressor runs | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off immediately | Discharge and replace capacitor |
| Fan and compressor both dead, unit silent | Contactor or breaker/fuse | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test contactor and control voltage |
| Fan won't start, motor hot/noisy or seized | Failed fan motor | Turn off; don't force it | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Blade visibly blocked or bent | Debris / bent blade | Clear debris with power off | Straighten or replace fan blade |
| Fan stops, then unit shuts off on a delay | High-pressure trip from no airflow | Shut off to protect compressor | Fix airflow cause, verify pressures |
| Intermittent — fan starts sometimes | Weak capacitor or worn motor | Note when it fails | Test cap under load, inspect motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts + labor, for common Carrier repairs:

- **Run capacitor:** $12–$30 part / **$150–$300 installed** with diagnostic.
- **Contactor:** $15–$40 part / **$150–$350 installed**.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $150–$350 part / **$400–$650 installed** (varies by HP and model).
- **Fan blade:** $30–$80 part / **$150–$300 installed**.
- **Control board (two-stage/Infinity):** $200–$600 part / **$450–$900 installed**.
- **Service diagnostic fee alone:** $80–$180, often credited toward the repair.

Capacitor and contactor jobs are the cheap, common fixes. If the compressor was run without the fan for a long time and is damaged, costs climb steeply — sometimes toward full condenser replacement — which is why shutting off promptly matters.

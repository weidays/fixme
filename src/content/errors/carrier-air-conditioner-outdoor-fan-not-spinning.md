## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered fault code — it's a symptom you can see. On a Carrier split-system air conditioner, the outdoor unit (the condenser) contains a fan that pulls air across the condenser coil to reject heat. When you call for cooling, the thermostat energizes the outdoor unit, and both the **compressor** and the **condenser fan motor** should start together.

If the fan blade sits still while the unit is trying to run — or hums, twitches, or spins slowly then stops — the system cannot shed heat. This is a **pro-level** problem for two reasons:

1. Running the compressor with a dead fan can overheat and permanently damage the compressor, the single most expensive part in the system.
2. The most common cause (a failed capacitor) involves a component that holds a **lethal stored charge** and should be discharged and replaced by a qualified technician.

Turn the system off at the thermostat until it's diagnosed.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** The dual run capacitor gives the fan motor the jolt it needs to start. When it weakens or fails, the fan hums but won't spin, or won't start at all. This is the #1 cause and the cheapest part.
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor.** Worn bearings, overheating, or age can lock or kill the motor. It may spin hard by hand and stop, or not move at all.
3. **Failed contactor.** The contactor is the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit. Pitted or stuck contacts can prevent the fan (and compressor) from getting power.
4. **Debris jamming the fan blade.** Sticks, leaves, or ice can physically block the blade from turning.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown high-voltage fuse / disconnect.** No power reaching the condenser means nothing spins.
6. **Overheated unit on internal safety.** Some units cut the fan/compressor on a pressure or thermal limit; the fan may resume after cooldown but will keep tripping until the root cause is fixed.
7. **Control board or low-voltage wiring fault.** Less common, but a damaged 24V signal or board relay can leave the outdoor unit unpowered. Behavior here varies by model — Infinity communicating systems diagnose differently than basic Comfort-series units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform. Do **not** open the electrical access panel on the condenser — that's where the lethal capacitor lives.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace thermostat batteries if it has them.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter can cause the system to overheat and trip out. Replace it if it's dirty.
- **Indoor breaker AND outdoor breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker for the AC. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Some units have a pull-out disconnect box near the condenser. Confirm it's fully seated (don't open the metal cover on the unit itself).
- **Visible debris:** With the system **off at the breaker**, look through the top grille for obvious sticks or leaves against the fan blade. Do not reach in with power on.
- **Ice:** If the outdoor coil or fan is iced, turn the system off and let it thaw. Persistent icing points to a refrigerant or airflow problem — see our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide.
- **Supply/return vents:** Make sure indoor vents are open and unblocked.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the steps helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm power** at the disconnect and contactor with a meter — verifying 240V is present at the condenser.
2. **Test the run capacitor** with a capacitance meter, comparing the reading to the microfarad (µF) rating printed on the cap. A weak or out-of-spec reading is the smoking gun.
3. **Check the contactor** for pitted contacts and confirm the 24V coil is pulling in.
4. **Test the fan motor** — checking windings for continuity/shorts and spinning the blade by hand to feel for seized bearings.
5. **Inspect wiring** and the fan motor's connections for burnt or loose terminals.
6. **Verify safeties/pressures** if the unit is tripping on a limit, using gauges to rule out a refrigerant charge or airflow issue.

A good tech tests the cheap part (capacitor) before condemning the expensive one (motor). Be cautious of a quote that jumps straight to a motor or compressor without a capacitor test.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | Shut system off | Discharge & replace capacitor |
| Fan won't start, no hum | Dead motor or no power | Check breaker once | Test motor windings & voltage |
| Fan spins slowly, stops | Weak capacitor / worn bearings | Shut system off | Replace capacitor or motor |
| Blade physically blocked | Debris in fan | Kill breaker, remove visible debris | Clear obstruction, inspect blade |
| Outdoor unit fully dead | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check circuit |
| Fan quits after running a while | Overheating / safety trip | Off, replace filter, thaw ice | Diagnose pressures/airflow |
| Communicating (Infinity) fault | Board / wiring signal | Note any thermostat error text | Read system fault, test board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $75 – $200 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $300 – $650 (OEM matched motors and ECM types cost more)
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Clearing debris / minor fix:** $75 – $150

If a tech recommends replacing the compressor or the whole condenser, get a second opinion — the fan itself is rarely the reason for that level of expense unless the compressor was already damaged by running without cooling airflow.

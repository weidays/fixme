## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Carrier condenser (the outdoor unit). The compressor may still be humming or running, but the large fan on top of the unit isn't turning to reject heat outside.

This is a **stop-now situation**. The outdoor fan's only job is to pull air across the condenser coil so the refrigerant can dump its heat. Without it, refrigerant pressure and temperature spike fast, and the compressor — the single most expensive part in the system — can overheat and fail permanently.

On single-stage and two-stage Carrier units, the fan is a standard PSC motor started by a **run capacitor** and switched on by a **contactor**. On some Infinity and Greenspeed variable-speed models, the fan is an **ECM motor** driven by a control board, which fails and diagnoses differently — a shop will check the board and communication, not a simple capacitor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual capacitor that starts both the fan and compressor weakens with heat and age. Classic sign: the fan hums but won't spin, or only starts if you nudge it (do NOT do this with power on).
2. **Bad condenser fan motor** — Worn bearings, a burned winding, or an open thermal overload. The motor may run hot, seize, or trip on its own overload after a few minutes.
3. **Stuck or pitted contactor** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit. Burned or welded contacts can partially power the compressor but not the fan, or nothing at all.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse / disconnect** — A partial power loss to the outdoor unit can leave the compressor without the fan.
5. **Debris jamming the fan blade** — Leaves, twigs, or a warped blade physically blocking rotation.
6. **Control board or ECM fault (Infinity/Greenspeed variable-speed models)** — On these units the fan is board-controlled; a failed module or driver stops the fan even when everything else looks fine.
7. **Low-voltage / thermostat wiring fault** — Loss of the 24V signal to the contactor coil.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Turn the thermostat to OFF first.** Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser, touch the capacitor, or handle any wiring — capacitors store a lethal charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Set to COOL and lower the setpoint several degrees. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** A filthy filter can cause the whole system to short-cycle or freeze; swap it if dirty.
- **Breaker:** Check your main panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — don't keep resetting.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Confirm the pull-out disconnect box near the condenser is fully seated (this is a plug, not a live terminal).
- **Visible debris:** With the system OFF at the breaker, look through the top grille for leaves or sticks obviously jamming the blade. Do not reach in or force the blade.
- **Vents:** Confirm supply and return vents inside are open and unblocked.
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan with a float switch can shut the system down; check for standing water.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, **shut the system off at the thermostat and call a technician.** Running it will risk the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech follows a repeatable sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify power** at the disconnect and confirm 24V at the contactor coil.
2. **Test the run capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads (μF). A reading well below spec confirms a failed cap — the most common finding.
3. **Inspect the contactor** for pitted, burned, or welded contacts and correct pull-in.
4. **Check the fan motor** directly: bearing play, resistance across windings, and whether it runs when powered with a known-good capacitor.
5. **On variable-speed Infinity/Greenspeed units**, read the control board for ECM/fan faults and check communication rather than testing a capacitor.
6. **Confirm the blade spins freely** and isn't bent or dragging.

A good tech should show you the capacitor reading or the motor test result — not just swap parts blindly.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | Shut off at thermostat; don't nudge blade | Test and replace capacitor |
| Fan spins only if nudged | Weak capacitor | Shut off; call pro | Replace capacitor |
| Compressor runs, fan dead | Bad fan motor or capacitor | Shut off immediately | Test motor, replace cap or motor |
| Nothing outside runs | Contactor, breaker, or power loss | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test contactor, 24V signal, wiring |
| Blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Kill breaker, look for obvious debris | Clear jam, check/replace blade & bearings |
| Fan trips off after minutes | Motor overheating / bad bearings | Shut off | Replace fan motor |
| Variable-speed unit fan dead | ECM/board fault | Shut off; call pro | Read board faults, replace module/motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Actual prices vary by region and unit access.

- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$150 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor (PSC):** $300–$650 installed
- **Fan blade:** $80–$200 installed
- **ECM / variable-speed fan module (Infinity/Greenspeed):** $400–$900+ — parts alone are costly and often board-specific

A DIY breaker reset costs $0, but the capacitor and motor repairs on this list involve lethal stored voltage and should be left to a licensed tech.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numbered fault code — it's a symptom. On most Carrier air conditioners the outdoor (condenser) unit contains three things that must work together: the **compressor**, the **condenser fan motor** (the fan you see on top), and the **contactor and capacitor** that power them. When your system calls for cooling but that top fan sits still, the unit can't shed the heat it's pulling from your home.

Some Carrier condensers have basic control boards or none at all (just a contactor and capacitor), while **Infinity variable-speed units** use an outdoor control board and an ECM fan motor. Because of that, there is no single stored code for this problem across the lineup — the diagnosis depends on your model.

**Important:** if the fan is stopped, turn the system off. Running the compressor with no condenser airflow causes overheating and high-pressure trips, and can permanently damage the compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — the single most common cause. The dual capacitor feeds both the fan and compressor; a weak or bulged cap leaves the fan unable to start (hums or sits still, but spins if nudged).
2. **Condenser fan motor failure** — worn bearings, an open winding, or a burned-out motor. Often smells hot or the motor is seized.
3. **Contactor not pulling in** — pitted, stuck, or a failed 24V coil means no power reaches the fan and compressor.
4. **Debris or ice jamming the fan blade** — sticks, a bird's nest, or ice binding the blade prevents rotation.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown high-voltage fuse / disconnect pulled** — no power to the outdoor unit.
6. **Failed capacitor start relay or loose/burned wiring** — corroded terminals or a spade connection that fell off.
7. **Control board / ECM fault (Infinity and variable-speed units)** — the board isn't commanding the ECM motor, or the motor's module has failed.
8. **Overheated motor on thermal cutout** — the motor shut itself off after running hot; it may restart cool but fail again.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — everything inside the condenser involves 240V and a capacitor that stores a lethal charge even with power off.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace dead thermostat batteries.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the indoor coil to freeze; check that the filter is clean and airflow is normal.
- **Breaker & disconnect:** Check the AC breaker in your panel and reset it once if tripped. If it trips again, stop — that signals an electrical fault for a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the pull-out disconnect box near the condenser is fully seated (some get pulled during service or yard work).
- **Visible debris:** With the system off at the thermostat and breaker, look through the top grille for leaves, sticks, or a bird's nest against the blade. Never reach in with power on.
- **Indoor vents & condensate:** Confirm supply registers are open and the condensate drain isn't backed up (a full pan can trip a safety float and stop the call for cooling).

If the fan is still dead after these checks, call a licensed HVAC tech. Do **not** repeatedly cycle the breaker to force the fan.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** and measure 24V at the contactor coil to confirm the thermostat/board is asking for the unit to run.
2. **Check line voltage** (around 240V) at the contactor's load side to confirm power is reaching the components.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter for microfarad value against the printed rating; a reading well below spec means replacement.
4. **Test the fan motor** — check windings for resistance/open, spin it by hand for bearing drag, and check for a tripped thermal overload.
5. **Inspect the contactor** for pitted contacts and confirm it pulls in.
6. **On Infinity/variable-speed units,** read the outdoor control board for stored faults and confirm the board is commanding the ECM motor.

Expect a diagnostic fee of roughly **$80–$180**, often credited toward the repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan won't start but spins freely if nudged | Weak/failed run capacitor | Shut off system; don't touch cap | Discharge & replace capacitor, verify µF |
| Fan hums, doesn't turn, gets hot | Seized motor or bad capacitor | Turn off to prevent burnout | Test/replace motor and capacitor |
| Compressor runs, fan dead | Fan motor failure or fan-side capacitor | Shut off to protect compressor | Replace motor; check dual cap fan leg |
| Nothing runs outdoors, no hum | Contactor not engaging or no power | Check breaker & disconnect | Test 24V coil, replace contactor |
| Blade physically stuck | Debris or ice jamming blade | Remove visible debris (power off) | Clear jam, inspect motor/bearings |
| Breaker trips when unit starts | Shorted motor or wiring fault | Don't keep resetting | Locate short, replace faulted part |
| Infinity unit, fan won't command on | Control board / ECM fault | Note any error on wall control | Read board faults, replace board/motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor (PSC) replacement:** $350–$650, usually including a new capacitor
- **ECM / variable-speed fan motor (Infinity):** $600–$1,200+ due to motor and module cost
- **Outdoor control board (Infinity/variable-speed):** $400–$900
- **Clearing debris / clearing an iced blade:** often covered by the diagnostic fee alone

Prices vary by region, unit tonnage, and whether the failure damaged additional parts. Get an itemized quote before authorizing work.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a symptom of your Carrier condenser (the outdoor unit). When the system calls for cooling, the compressor and the outdoor fan should both start. The fan's job is to pull air across the condenser coil so it can dump heat outside.

If the fan blade sits still while the unit hums, clicks, or runs the compressor, something in the fan's electrical path has failed. This matters because **running the compressor without the fan lets pressures and temperatures climb fast** — enough to trip the compressor on overload or, over time, destroy it. That's why this problem is rated pro-level: the safe move is to stop cooling and get it diagnosed.

Infinity-series systems may log a related board fault or communication message, but most Carrier split systems give you no code at all — just a hot, quiet unit that isn't cooling the house.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — By far the most common cause. The dual capacitor stores the jolt needed to start and run the fan (and compressor). When the fan side weakens or fails, the motor can't start and just hums.
2. **Seized or worn fan motor** — Bearings dry out and lock up, especially on older units. The motor may hum, smell hot, or trip a breaker.
3. **Contactor failure** — The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted, stuck, or burned contacts can cut power to the fan.
4. **Debris or ice jamming the blade** — A stick, plastic bag, or blade bent against the shroud can physically stop the fan.
5. **Control board or relay fault (Infinity/Performance)** — On communicating systems, a board that isn't commanding the fan can leave it idle even when hardware is fine.
6. **Broken/loose wiring or a tripped internal overload** — Vibration loosens spade connectors; a motor overheating repeatedly can trip its own thermal overload.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no panel removal, no touching capacitors (they hold a dangerous charge even with power off).

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the target temp is below room temp. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor AC breaker (and the disconnect box near the outdoor unit). Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause a chain of cooling problems. Replace if dirty.
- **Supply/return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked.
- **Look (don't touch) at the outdoor unit:** From the outside, check for obvious debris — leaves, a stick, or ice on the blade. Visible ice usually points to a bigger airflow or refrigerant issue (see our Frozen Evaporator Coil guide).
- **Condensate:** A full condensate pan or clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut down the system.

If the breaker holds and everything looks clear but the fan still won't spin, shut the system off and call an HVAC tech.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A tech should work in this order — use it to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling** and verify 24V reaching the contactor coil.
2. **Test the run capacitor** with a capacitance meter, comparing the reading against the rated microfarads printed on the label. A weak or dead fan section confirms the cheapest fix.
3. **Check the contactor** for pitted contacts and proper pull-in when energized.
4. **Test the fan motor** — resistance/continuity of windings, check for a tripped internal overload, and spin the blade (power off) to feel for a seized bearing.
5. **Inspect wiring** at the motor and capacitor for burned or loose connectors.
6. **On Infinity/communicating systems,** read board fault history to see if the fan was even commanded on.

A good tech replaces the capacitor as part of a motor job, since a weak capacitor is often what kills the motor in the first place.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit hums, fan won't start | Failed run capacitor | Shut off system, don't touch | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan hums, hot smell, trips breaker | Seized fan motor | Turn off, reset breaker once | Replace fan motor + capacitor |
| No hum, compressor also dead | Contactor or no 24V signal | Check thermostat & breaker | Test/replace contactor, trace 24V |
| Blade physically stuck | Debris or bent blade | Turn off power, look for obvious debris | Clear obstruction, inspect motor/blade |
| Fan idle, unit hot, cooling weak | Board not commanding fan (Infinity) | Note any thermostat messages | Read board faults, test relay/board |
| Fan runs, then stops after minutes | Overheating motor / weak cap | Shut off, avoid repeated restarts | Diagnose overload, replace parts |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts + labor. Diagnostic/service call fees ($75–$180) often apply on top or are folded into the repair.

- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$300
- **Condenser fan motor + capacitor:** $400–$700
- **Fan blade replacement:** $150–$350
- **Control board (Infinity/communicating):** $400–$900+
- **Clearing debris / minor wiring repair:** $90–$200

The capacitor is the cheapest and most common fix — don't be surprised if a $150–$300 repair gets your fan spinning again. Push back on a full-system replacement pitch until a capacitor, contactor, and motor have been individually tested.

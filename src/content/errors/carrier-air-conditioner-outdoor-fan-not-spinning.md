## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" isn't a numeric fault code — it's a physical symptom on your Carrier condenser (the outdoor unit). When the AC calls for cooling, the compressor and the outdoor (condenser) fan should start together. The fan pulls air across the condenser coil to reject the heat your system pulled out of the house.

If the compressor runs but the fan blade sits still — or if the whole outdoor unit is silent — the heat has nowhere to go. Pressure and temperature inside the compressor climb quickly, which is why this is a **pro-severity** problem: keep running it and you risk a compressor failure that costs several times more than the original repair.

On most Carrier split systems (Comfort, Performance, and many Infinity models) the fan is a single-phase PSC motor driven through a run capacitor and switched by a 24V contactor. Variable-speed Infinity condensers with ECM fan motors behave differently and are diagnosed by the control board, not a capacitor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** The single most common cause. The capacitor gives the fan motor its starting torque. When it weakens, the fan hums, won't start, or only spins if nudged. Often the same dual capacitor also serves the compressor.
2. **Seized or burned-out fan motor.** Worn bearings or a failed winding stop the motor entirely, sometimes with a burnt smell or tripped breaker.
3. **Failed contactor.** The contactor is the relay that sends power to the fan and compressor. Pitted or stuck contacts can leave the fan (and compressor) without power.
4. **Obstructed or damaged fan blade.** Debris, ice, or a bent blade can jam the fan. A broken blade may spin freely but move no air.
5. **Tripped high-pressure or safety switch / control-board fault.** Some units cut power to the outdoor section on a fault. On Infinity boards this may log a specific error.
6. **Wiring or connection failure.** Corroded terminals, chewed wires, or a loose spade connector at the motor or capacitor.
7. **No power to the unit.** A tripped breaker, blown fuse, or open disconnect stops everything — fan and compressor alike.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Never open the electrical panel on the condenser** — the capacitor holds a lethal charge even with the power off.

- **Set the thermostat to cool** and lower the setpoint several degrees to confirm the system is actually calling for cooling.
- **Check the breaker** for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor unit. Reset a tripped breaker once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the outdoor disconnect** (the pull-out box on the wall near the unit) is fully seated.
- **Replace a dirty air filter.** A clogged filter can cause freezing that ices the outdoor unit and stops the fan.
- **Look for obvious debris** — leaves, a stick, or ice — around the fan blade, visible from the top grille without removing panels. Do not reach in while power is on.
- **Check for ice** on the outdoor coil or lines. If you see ice, turn the system off (fan only, if available) to let it thaw before service.
- **Confirm indoor vents are open** and the condensate drain isn't backed up, which can trip a safety float switch and shut the system down.

If the fan still won't spin after these checks, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A quote should reflect this sequence:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling** and verify 24V is reaching the contactor coil.
2. **Test the contactor** — check whether it's pulling in and passing line voltage to the fan and compressor.
3. **Test the run capacitor** with a meter for its microfarad (µF) rating against the label. A capacitor out of spec is condemned.
4. **Check the fan motor** — verify voltage at the motor leads, test windings for resistance and shorts to ground, and check for a seized bearing by hand (power off).
5. **Inspect the blade and shaft** for damage or obstruction.
6. **Check safety switches and pressures** — a tripped high-pressure switch or low charge points to a deeper issue. On Infinity systems, they'll read stored board fault codes.
7. **Confirm the fix works** and verify amp draw is within the motor's rating.

A good tech won't just throw a capacitor at it — they'll confirm the motor spins freely and draws correct current so you don't pay twice.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but doesn't spin; starts if nudged | Failed run capacitor | Turn system off; don't force the blade | Test and replace capacitor |
| Compressor runs, fan dead silent | Bad capacitor, motor, or wiring | Shut system off to protect compressor | Diagnose fan circuit, replace part |
| Whole outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, disconnect, or contactor | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Test contactor and incoming power |
| Fan spins freely but no airflow | Broken/loose fan blade | Turn off; inspect visually | Replace blade or motor assembly |
| Burnt smell, breaker trips again | Shorted fan motor | Leave breaker off; call pro | Replace motor, check wiring |
| Ice on coil, fan stopped | Airflow restriction / low charge | Replace filter; thaw with system off | Find airflow or refrigerant cause |
| Fan cuts out then quits | Overheating motor or high-pressure trip | Shut down, don't repeat resets | Read fault, test motor and pressures |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area, unit size, and part availability affect the final number.

- **Run capacitor:** $150 – $300 installed. The part is cheap; you're paying for the diagnosis and labor.
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350 installed.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300 – $650 installed, depending on horsepower and whether a new capacitor and blade are included.
- **Fan blade:** $100 – $250 installed.
- **Wiring/connection repair:** $100 – $300.
- **Service call / diagnostic fee:** $80 – $180, often credited toward the repair.
- **High-pressure switch or control-board fault:** varies widely; boards on Infinity units can run $400 – $800+.

If your condenser is 12+ years old and the motor has failed, ask whether repair still makes sense versus replacement — especially if it also uses R-22 refrigerant.

## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is not a numeric fault code — it's a symptom. On most Carrier split-system air conditioners, the outdoor unit (condenser) contains a compressor and a fan that pulls air across the condenser coil to reject heat. When the compressor runs but the fan blade sits still, refrigerant can't shed heat, pressures climb fast, and the compressor is at risk.

Carrier's standard single-stage and two-stage condensers (Comfort, Performance series) run the fan off a **dual run capacitor** and a **contactor**, with no dedicated diagnostic display. Higher-end **Infinity** variable-speed systems use an ECM fan motor driven by a control board, and those units may log board faults instead — so behavior varies by model. If you have an Infinity system with a fault display, note the code before calling.

This is rated **pro** because diagnosing it means working around energized 240V circuits, a charged capacitor that can hold a dangerous shock, and sealed refrigerant pressures.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor** — the single most common cause. The fan side of the dual capacitor weakens or fails, so the motor lacks starting torque. Classic sign: the fan will spin if you nudge it (do NOT do this with power on).
2. **Seized or burned-out condenser fan motor** — worn bearings lock the shaft, or the windings fail. The motor may hum, get hot, or trip the breaker.
3. **Contactor not pulling in / burned contacts** — pitted or stuck contacts interrupt power to the fan and compressor.
4. **Debris or ice jamming the fan blade** — sticks, leaves, or a bent blade physically block rotation.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — partial power loss can stop the fan while other components try to run.
6. **Control board fault (Infinity/variable-speed units)** — on ECM-driven models, a failed board or motor module stops the fan; single-stage units usually have no board involved.
7. **Broken fan blade or loose set screw** — the motor spins but the blade doesn't turn with it.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or opening the unit:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace thermostat batteries if it's battery-powered and the display is dim or blank.
- **Air filter:** A clogged filter can cause the indoor coil to ice and pressures to trip; replace a dirty filter and let the system thaw.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor furnace/air-handler breaker and the outdoor disconnect/breaker. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — that's a fault, not a nuisance trip.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure vents are open and unblocked.
- **Look (don't touch) at the outdoor unit:** From outside the unit, look through the top grille for obvious debris, a stick, or an ice buildup on the blade. Do **not** reach in or spin the blade by hand.
- **Condensate:** Check that the indoor condensate drain isn't backed up and tripping a safety switch.

Do not open the electrical panel on the condenser, do not touch the capacitor, and do not manually push the fan while power is on. If the safe checks don't restore normal operation, shut the system OFF and call a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

1. **Kill power at the disconnect** and verify it with a meter before opening the unit.
2. **Discharge the capacitor** safely, then test it with a multimeter against its rated µF (e.g., 45+5). An out-of-spec fan section confirms a bad capacitor.
3. **Inspect the contactor** for pitted or welded contacts and verify the 24V coil pulls it in.
4. **Test the fan motor** — check windings for continuity/shorts, spin the shaft for bearing drag, and confirm it gets line voltage when the contactor closes.
5. **Check the blade and set screw**, look for a bent blade or debris.
6. **On Infinity/ECM units**, read board fault codes, verify the motor module signal, and check board outputs.
7. **Confirm pressures** with gauges to rule out an overcharge, restriction, or compressor issue once the fan is running.

A good tech should show you the failed part (a swollen capacitor top, a burned contactor, a locked shaft) rather than just replacing parts blindly.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compressor hums, fan still; spins if nudged | Failed run capacitor | Shut system off; don't touch capacitor | Discharge and replace capacitor, verify µF |
| Fan motor hums, hot, won't turn | Seized fan motor | Turn off at thermostat and disconnect | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Nothing runs outside, indoor blower OK | Bad contactor or blown fuse | Reset breaker once only | Test/replace contactor, low-voltage fuse |
| Blade visibly jammed by debris/ice | Physical obstruction | Turn off power; report debris | De-ice, clear debris, inspect blade |
| Breaker trips when unit starts | Shorted motor or wiring fault | Do not keep resetting | Locate short, replace faulted component |
| Motor spins but blade doesn't | Broken blade / loose set screw | None | Replace blade, tighten/secure set screw |
| Infinity unit shows fault, fan dead | Control board / ECM module | Note the code | Diagnose board and ECM motor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included unless noted:

- **Run capacitor (DIY part only):** $15–$40
- **Run capacitor (professional replacement):** $150–$300
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor replacement:** $350–$650, depending on horsepower and whether a matching motor or universal is used
- **Fan blade replacement:** $80–$200
- **Infinity/ECM control board or motor module:** $400–$900+ (variable-speed parts cost more)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$175, often applied to the repair

Prices vary by region, unit age, and part availability. If your Carrier condenser is 12–15+ years old and needs a major part like a motor plus board, ask your tech whether repair or replacement makes more financial sense.

## What this code means

"Breaker keeps tripping" is not a Lennox fault code. It is a symptom.

The circuit breaker feeding your outdoor AC unit (condenser) senses one of three things and shuts off power to protect the wiring:

- **Overcurrent:** the unit is drawing more current than the circuit is rated for.
- **Short circuit:** current is flowing along a path it shouldn't.
- **Ground fault:** current is leaking to the metal cabinet or ground.

How the unit behaves afterward:

- **It does not auto-reset.** The breaker stays off until someone resets it manually.
- **The thermostat may keep calling for cooling.** The indoor blower can run while the outdoor unit stays dead. This looks like the problem covered in "Lennox AC Fan Runs But No Cold Air."

How quickly it trips is a clue for the technician:

- **Instantly when you reset it:** points to a dead short or grounded component.
- **A few seconds after the compressor tries to start:** points to a hard-start or locked-rotor problem.
- **After minutes or hours of running:** points to overheating, high head pressure, or a weak breaker.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** The compressor can't start properly and draws locked-rotor amps until the breaker trips. This is one of the most common outdoor-unit failures on single- and two-stage Lennox units.
2. **Pitted, welded, or shorted contactor.** Burned contacts or a shorted coil can cause arcing, high current draw, or a short.
3. **Damaged or loose wiring.** Common culprits include:
   - Wires rubbed through by vibration inside the cabinet.
   - Rodent-chewed wiring.
   - Loose lugs at the contactor or disconnect.
   - A corroded or water-damaged disconnect box or whip (the flexible conduit to the unit).
4. **Compressor problems.** A compressor that is grounded (shorted to its shell), has a shorted winding, or has a locked rotor will trip the breaker almost immediately. This is the most expensive cause.
5. **Failing outdoor fan motor.** A fan motor that is shorted or has seized bearings can draw excessive current. A dead fan can also let head pressure and compressor amps climb until the breaker trips.
6. **High head pressure from a dirty condenser coil or restricted airflow.** Raised pressures increase compressor amperage. This usually causes trips after the unit has run for a while, especially on hot days. A badly clogged indoor filter can contribute too.
7. **Weak, undersized, or worn-out breaker.** Breakers fatigue with age. An installer may also have used a breaker that doesn't match the nameplate MCA (minimum circuit ampacity) and MOCP (maximum overcurrent protection).
8. **Inverter drive fault (variable-capacity models only).** On the XC25, SL28XCV, and similar models, a failed inverter board or a shorted component inside the drive can trip the breaker. The drive usually logs codes first.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Reset the breaker once.** Turn the thermostat to OFF first. Flip the breaker fully to OFF, then back to ON. Wait at least 5 minutes, then set the thermostat to COOL.
  - If it trips again, **stop**. Leave the breaker off and call a technician.
  - Do not reset it repeatedly.
- **Note the timing.** Did it trip instantly, at compressor start, or after running a while? Tell the technician; it speeds up diagnosis.
- **Check the air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter strains the system.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it is set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint. Replace the batteries if it uses them.
- **Look at the outdoor unit from outside only.** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around it. Keep about 2 feet of clearance.
  - Don't open any panels.
  - If you see melted wiring, a burned smell, or scorch marks, leave the breaker off.
- **Check the condensate line and drain pan area.** Water near the indoor unit can point to drainage problems. These don't trip the outdoor breaker, but mention them to the tech.
- **Make sure exterior service panels are seated and secured.** Do not remove them.

Anything inside the outdoor cabinet or the electrical disconnect box is technician work. That includes capacitors, contactors, wiring, and meter tests. The capacitor can hold a dangerous charge even with the power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically:

1. **Ask about trip timing and inspect for obvious damage.** They'll look for burned wiring, a scorched contactor, rodent damage, or water in the disconnect.
2. **Lock out power and discharge the capacitor.** Then they'll test the capacitor's microfarads (µF) against its rating. Readings more than about 6% below the rating are commonly considered failed.
3. **Megohm or ohm the compressor.** They check winding resistance between the common, start, and run terminals (C–S, C–R, S–R) and test each terminal to ground. Any reading to ground condemns the compressor, or at least prompts a megohmmeter test for a weak insulation breakdown.
4. **Test the fan motor** windings and check for a short to ground. They'll also spin the blade by hand to check the bearings.
5. **Inspect and test the contactor** coil and contacts, plus all wiring from the disconnect to the compressor terminals.
6. **Verify the breaker.** They'll compare its size to the nameplate MCA and MOCP, check the wire gauge, look for loose or overheated lugs, and may test the breaker itself.
7. **Measure running amps against nameplate RLA** if the unit can run. They'll also check pressures and condenser coil cleanliness for high-head conditions.
8. **Read inverter or control fault history** on variable-capacity or communicating models.

**Red flags in a quote:** being told you need a new compressor or system without any winding or ground readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Breaker trips instantly on reset | Dead short or grounded compressor or wiring | Leave the breaker off | Ohm/megohm the compressor and wiring; repair the short or replace the compressor |
| Trips a few seconds after outdoor unit hums | Failed run capacitor or locked rotor | Leave the breaker off after one reset | Test and replace the capacitor; possibly add a hard-start kit; evaluate the compressor |
| Trips after running a while on hot days | High head pressure: dirty coil, failing fan | Clear debris around the unit; replace the filter | Clean the condenser coil; test the fan motor; check the charge |
| Outdoor fan not spinning, then trip | Failed fan motor or its capacitor | Leave off; note that the fan wasn't turning | Replace the fan motor or capacitor |
| Buzzing or arcing at the unit, burned smell | Pitted or shorted contactor, loose lugs | Leave the breaker off; keep away | Replace the contactor; repair the wiring |
| Old breaker, warm panel, intermittent trips | Weak or undersized breaker, loose lug | Note the breaker rating | Electrician verifies MOCP and replaces the breaker |
| Variable-speed unit trips, codes shown beforehand | Inverter drive fault | Record any thermostat codes | Read the fault history; replace the inverter board or the component |

## Repair costs

Typical US installed prices, including parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic or service call | $100 – $250 |
| Run capacitor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Hard-start kit | $200 – $450 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Wiring, whip, or disconnect repair | $150 – $600 |
| Breaker replacement (electrician) | $150 – $400 |
| Outdoor fan motor replacement | $350 – $900 (variable-speed ECM motors run higher) |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100 – $300 |
| Inverter drive or control board (variable-capacity models) | $800 – $2,500 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $3,500+ |

**Notes on compressor replacement:**

- Lennox's 10-year limited parts warranty, valid with registration, often covers the compressor part. Labor and refrigerant are usually extra.
- For older or R-22 units, replacing the whole condenser ($4,000 – $8,000+) is often the better value.

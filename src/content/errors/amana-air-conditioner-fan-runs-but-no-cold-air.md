## What this code means

"Fan runs but no cold air" is a symptom, not a numbered fault code. The indoor blower is moving air through your vents, but the air is room temperature or only slightly cool. The refrigeration side of the system, which is the outdoor compressor and condenser, is not producing cooling.

This page covers the case where the **indoor fan works and the outdoor unit is either silent, humming, or running without cooling**. For broader problems, see the related pages listed at the bottom:

- **Weak cooling with everything running:** see Amana AC Not Cooling.
- **Visible ice on the indoor coil:** see the frozen evaporator coil page.
- **Outdoor compressor runs but its fan doesn't spin:** see the outdoor fan not spinning page.

Behavior depends on the model:

- **Single-stage and two-stage units** with conventional 24V thermostats usually have no diagnostic display for this symptom.
- **Amana communicating and inverter models**, such as the variable-speed communicating line, may log a fault on the outdoor control board LED or on the communicating thermostat. Common examples are high- or low-pressure faults and compressor faults. Check your outdoor unit's wiring diagram label or installation manual for that model's code chart.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Outdoor unit has no power.** The breaker is tripped, the outdoor disconnect is pulled or switched off, or a fuse in the disconnect has blown. The indoor blower runs on a separate circuit, so it keeps working.
2. **Failed run/start capacitor.** This is the most common component failure on outdoor units. Typical signs are a humming outdoor unit with the fan not spinning and the compressor not starting, or a unit that won't start at all.
3. **Bad contactor.** The contacts may be pitted or burned, the coil may have failed, or insects may be lodged in it. The thermostat calls for cooling, but the contactor never sends power to the compressor and fan.
4. **Thermostat or low-voltage control problem.** Causes include a wrong mode, a broken Y (cooling) wire, a blown low-voltage fuse on the furnace or air handler board, or a tripped float/condensate safety switch. Many installations wire that safety switch to cut the cooling call while leaving the blower running.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** The system runs but cooling is weak or absent. It may eventually freeze the coil or trip a low-pressure switch on models that have one.
6. **Compressor thermal overload or compressor failure.** A compressor that overheats shuts off internally and stays off until it cools down. A seized or grounded compressor won't run at all.
7. **High-pressure trip from a severely blocked condenser coil.** The outdoor coil is clogged with dirt, cottonwood, or grass. This applies to models equipped with a high-pressure switch.
8. **Inverter or communicating control fault** (variable-speed models only). Possible causes are a sensor, inverter board, or communication error between the indoor and outdoor units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Cool**, with the setpoint at least 3°F below room temperature and the fan on **Auto**. If the display is blank or dim, replace the batteries. Some thermostats delay the compressor for about 5 minutes after a change, so wait that long before judging.
- **Breakers:** At the main panel, find the breaker for the AC or condenser, usually a 2-pole breaker. If it has tripped, turn it fully off and then back on **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician. A repeat trip points to an electrical fault.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Check the box mounted near the outdoor unit. Make sure the handle or pull-out block is fully in the ON position. Do not open or work inside the box beyond this.
- **Listen at the outdoor unit** while the thermostat is calling for cooling. Note whether it is silent, humming, clicking, or running. A tech will want to know.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it is dirty. A clogged filter can contribute to a frozen coil and poor cooling.
- **Condensate line and drain pan:** Look for standing water in the pan or around the indoor unit. A full pan can trip the float safety switch, which shuts off cooling. If you can see a clog at the outside end of the drain line, clear it. You can also use a wet/dry vacuum on the drain line's outdoor end, then give the system several minutes to restart. If the float switch stays tripped or the pan stays full, stop and call a pro rather than clearing the line again and again.
- **Vents and outdoor unit:** Make sure the supply registers are open. Clear leaves, debris, and plants within about 2 feet of the condenser, working from the outside only. Do not remove panels or spray inside the cabinet.
- **Panels:** Make sure the exterior access panels on the indoor and outdoor units are in place and secure. Do not remove them; everything inside the cabinet is technician work.

If cooling doesn't return after these checks, set the thermostat to **Off**. This prevents compressor damage while you wait for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

1. **Confirm the call for cooling.** The tech measures 24V across the contactor coil at the outdoor unit. No voltage points them indoors, to the thermostat, wiring, a low-voltage fuse, or a float switch.
2. **Check line voltage** at the disconnect and at the contactor's line and load sides. This confirms power is getting into and through the contactor.
3. **Test capacitors with a meter** and compare the readings to the µF rating printed on the label. A reading more than 6% below the rating, or a bulging case, usually means the capacitor should be replaced.
4. **Check compressor windings** for resistance between the common, start, and run terminals and for a short to ground. They also check whether the internal overload has opened.
5. **Attach gauges or probes** to read suction and liquid pressures and calculate superheat and subcooling. If the charge is low, they should **find and repair the leak**, not just top it off.
6. **Inspect the condenser coil** and any high-pressure or low-pressure switches.
7. **Read fault history** on communicating or inverter models from the outdoor board or thermostat, then follow Amana's model-specific troubleshooting chart.

Red flags in a quote:

- Recommending a refrigerant recharge with no leak search.
- Condemning the compressor without winding and capacitor tests.
- Quoting a full system replacement before diagnosing the problem.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor unit completely silent | Tripped breaker, disconnect off, blown disconnect fuse | Reset the breaker once; confirm the disconnect is fully on | Test and replace the fuse, trace the electrical fault |
| Outdoor unit silent, breaker fine | No 24V call: thermostat, Y wire, low-voltage fuse, float switch | Check thermostat mode and batteries; clear the condensate drain | Test the control circuit, replace the fuse, repair wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or stuck compressor | Turn the system off to protect the compressor | Test and replace the capacitor; test the compressor |
| Clicking, unit won't start | Contactor or capacitor failure | Turn the system off | Replace the contactor or capacitor |
| Outdoor unit runs, air only slightly cool | Low refrigerant, dirty condenser coil | Replace the filter; clear debris around the unit | Leak search and repair, recharge, coil cleaning |
| Starts, then shuts off after minutes | Compressor overload, high-pressure trip | Clear the area around the unit; check the filter | Check pressures, motors, coil, and charge |
| Ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil | Low charge or restricted airflow | Turn the system off, run Fan only to thaw, replace the filter | Diagnose the charge and airflow (see frozen coil page) |
| Fault light on outdoor board or thermostat (inverter models) | Pressure, sensor, or communication fault | Note the code; cycle power once | Follow Amana's model-specific code chart |

## Repair costs

Typical US prices, including parts and labor:

| Item | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $75 – $200 |
| Capacitor replacement | $120 – $350 |
| Contactor replacement | $150 – $400 |
| Low-voltage fuse or float switch repair | $100 – $250 |
| Thermostat replacement | $150 – $450 installed (DIY thermostat $30 – $250) |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100 – $250 |
| Refrigerant leak search and repair, plus recharge (R-410A on older units or R-32 on newer Amana units) | $400 – $1,500+, depending on leak location and charge size |
| Inverter or outdoor control board (variable-speed models) | $500 – $1,500 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500 – $3,000+ (check your warranty first; see note below) |

Amana warranty coverage varies by model and depends on registration. Before authorizing a compressor replacement, check your model's warranty certificate and confirm your registration status.

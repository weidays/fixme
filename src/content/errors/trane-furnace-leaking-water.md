## What this code means

"Leaking water" is not a fault code on Trane furnaces — it's a physical symptom you'll notice as puddles, drips, or water stains around or under the cabinet. On modern high-efficiency (condensing) Trane furnaces, this almost always involves the **condensate system**, which handles the water produced when flue gases cool.

A standard condensing furnace can generate several gallons of condensate a day during heavy heating. That water is routed through the secondary heat exchanger, a collector box, a condensate trap, tubing, and a drain line. A blockage or failure anywhere in that chain sends water where it shouldn't be.

Because a persistent leak can flood the control board, rust internal components, or point to a **cracked secondary heat exchanger** (a combustion-safety issue), this is rated **pro-level**.

A plugged drain often backs water into the pressure switch tubing, so a leak and a pressure-switch fault frequently show up together. **How that fault is reported depends on your control board and model, not on the brand.** Some Trane/American Standard integrated controls use an LED that flashes a blink count; others display a two-digit numeric code on a small digital display; and communicating XL/ComfortLink systems report the fault at the thermostat instead. Don't assume a particular blink count applies to your furnace — read the diagnostic label printed inside the blower door, which lists the fault indications for the board actually installed in your unit.

**Note:** 80% (non-condensing) Trane furnaces don't produce combustion condensate. Water near those models usually comes from a flue leak, a whole-house humidifier, or an A-coil above the furnace — not the same causes below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged condensate drain line** — The most common cause. Algae, dust, and sludge build up and back water into the collector box until it overflows.
2. **Blocked or dirty condensate trap** — The trap fills with debris and stops draining; water backs up and spills from the trap or collector box.
3. **Cracked, loose, or disconnected drain tubing** — Cracked hoses or joints that popped off dribble water directly onto the floor.
4. **Failed or clogged condensate pump** (if your setup uses one) — A stuck float, dead motor, or clogged pump reservoir overflows.
5. **Blocked or improperly sloped drain** — A line that sags, lacks slope, or has a frozen/blocked termination won't carry water away.
6. **Cracked collector box or inducer housing** — Cracks in the collector or inducer assembly leak condensate outside the drain path.
7. **A-coil / evaporator condensate (during AC)** — If your AC coil sits on the furnace, a clogged coil drain can dump water into the furnace cabinet.
8. **Cracked secondary heat exchanger** — Less common but serious. A cracked exchanger leaks condensate and can leak combustion gases; requires a professional.

## Safety first: CO alarms and suspected combustion gas

If a carbon monoxide alarm is sounding, or you smell exhaust/combustion gases, or anyone in the house has headaches, nausea, dizziness, or flu-like symptoms that improve outdoors:

1. **Get everyone and all pets out of the house immediately.** Don't stop to find the source, open windows, or shut equipment down.
2. **Leave the door open behind you** as you exit.
3. **Call 911 / the fire department, or your gas utility's emergency line, from outside** — a neighbor's house, your car, or the sidewalk.
4. **Do not go back inside until the fire department or utility tells you it's safe.**

Only after the house has been cleared should you shut the furnace off at the service switch and schedule a technician. Nothing below replaces those four steps.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only things you should do yourself:

- **Check the thermostat and shut the system to OFF** if water is pooling near electrical parts — this protects the control board.
- **Replace a soaked or clogged air filter.** A restricted filter isn't a leak cause on its own, but a fresh filter rules it out and improves airflow.
- **Look at the condensate drain line** where it exits to a floor drain, sink, or outside. Confirm it isn't obviously kinked, crushed, disconnected, or blocked at the termination.
- **Empty a full condensate pump reservoir** if your unit has an external pump and it's overflowing — a bucket and shop vac are fine here.
- **Gently clear the drain line's exterior end** — you can pull leaves or debris off an outdoor termination or use a wet/dry vac at the open end to suck out a clog. Do this **only at the exterior/terminal end of the drain line**. Never pull vacuum on tubing connected to the pressure switch or the collector box, and never disconnect anything inside the cabinet to reach a hose.
- **Confirm the breaker and furnace switch** are set correctly if you cycled power.

Do **not** open the furnace cabinet, remove internal drain tubing, take apart the trap or collector box, or touch the inducer, heat exchanger, or control board. Those are technician tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

- Inspect for the water's origin — tracing it back from the puddle to the collector box, trap, tubing, pump, or heat exchanger.
- Remove and flush the **condensate trap and drain line**, often with a wet/dry vacuum and a vinegar/water flush, then verify free drainage.
- Check the **drain slope and termination** for backups, freezing, or negative pitch.
- Test the **condensate pump** float switch and motor if one is present.
- Inspect the **collector box and inducer housing** for cracks.
- Check the **pressure switch and tubing** for water intrusion, since a plugged drain often trips a pressure-switch fault — read out however your particular control board reports it (blink code, two-digit display, or a message at a communicating thermostat).
- If no drain fault is found, perform a **heat exchanger inspection** (visual, and sometimes combustion/CO analysis) to rule out a cracked secondary exchanger.

If a tech quotes a heat exchanger replacement, ask to see the crack or combustion readings — it's the most expensive repair and shouldn't be assumed without evidence.

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor unless noted):

- **DIY drain line flush / clearing termination:** $0–$30 (vinegar, brush, shop vac you likely own).
- **Professional condensate drain and trap cleaning:** $125–$300, especially once a diagnostic fee is rolled in.
- **Condensate pump replacement:** $175–$450.
- **Drain tubing / hose replacement or reseating:** $120–$300.
- **Collector box replacement:** $300–$600 on many models, and frequently more than $600 on modulating models, depending on parts availability.
- **Inducer assembly with cracked housing:** $400–$750.
- **Secondary heat exchanger replacement:** $600–$1,500+. The $600 low end really only holds when the exchanger itself is covered by the Trane parts warranty and you're paying labor only — Trane heat exchangers often carry a long or lifetime parts warranty, but labor is not included. Out of warranty, replacement on a condensing furnace commonly runs well above $1,500 once labor is added, and on older furnaces that often makes replacing the whole furnace more cost-effective.

Get the underlying cause confirmed before approving big-ticket parts. A drain cleaning in the $125–$300 range fixes the large majority of condensing-furnace leaks.

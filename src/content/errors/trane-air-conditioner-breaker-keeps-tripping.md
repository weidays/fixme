## What this code means

"Breaker keeps tripping" is not a Trane fault code. It means the circuit breaker feeding your Trane air conditioner is cutting power because it sensed one of two things:

- **Overcurrent:** too many amps for too long.
- **A short or ground fault:** current escaping to the cabinet or ground.

Most of the time it's the outdoor unit's breaker, usually a 2-pole 240 V breaker in the main panel. Occasionally it's the 120 V breaker for the indoor air handler or furnace.

The two trip patterns point in different directions:

- **Instant trip** the moment the unit tries to start usually means a dead short or grounded component. Typical culprits are a grounded compressor, a shorted fan motor or damaged wiring.
- **Delayed trip** after minutes or hours of running usually means sustained high current. Typical culprits are a weak capacitor, a hard-starting compressor, a dirty outdoor coil or a weak breaker.

The breaker does not auto-reset. It stays off until someone flips it back.

If you have a Trane heat pump rather than a cooling-only AC, see the separate heat pump breaker page. Heat pumps add defrost and electric-heat-strip causes that don't apply here.

Communicating and inverter models, such as XV units with ComfortLink II/Link controls, are covered here only in general terms. Their specific drive fault codes vary by model and are not listed on this page.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

This order is approximate. Actual frequency varies with the unit's age, installation and condition.

1. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** This is the most common service finding on Trane condensers. A degraded dual capacitor makes the compressor and fan motor draw excess current, or stall and pull locked-rotor amps until the breaker trips.
2. **Loose, burned or chafed wiring.** Look at the contactor, the compressor terminals, the disconnect or the line-voltage whip. Vibration rubs insulation through, and loose lugs overheat. This is common on older units.
3. **Pitted or failing contactor.** Burned contacts create heat at the terminals. They can also chatter or cause voltage drop, which can lead to hard starts.
4. **Hard-starting or locked compressor.** Worn compressor bearings or internal mechanical issues cause the compressor to repeatedly draw locked-rotor current. When this trips the breaker, it often shows up as a delayed trip, with the unit humming before the trip. More often, a locked compressor trips its own internal thermal overload, which resets itself, rather than the breaker. A unit that hums and shuts off without tripping the breaker is a related but different pattern.
5. **Grounded or shorted compressor winding.** Winding insulation breaks down and contacts the shell. This typically causes an instant trip every time. It's the most expensive cause and must be confirmed by testing, not assumed.
6. **Outdoor fan motor shorted or seized.** A shorted motor trips the breaker directly. A seized motor causes compressor overload from high head pressure. If the fan simply isn't spinning, see the outdoor fan page as well.
7. **High head pressure from airflow or charge problems.** A heavily clogged outdoor coil, a severely dirty indoor filter or coil, or a refrigerant overcharge or non-condensables all raise compressor amps at peak heat.
8. **Weak, failing or wrong-size breaker.** Breakers wear out with repeated trips. Sometimes the breaker is smaller than the maximum overcurrent protection (MOCP) printed on the Trane nameplate.
9. **Variable-speed (XV) inverter or drive fault.** On inverter models, a failed drive board can short to ground or trip the breaker. Diagnosis follows Trane's model-specific inverter troubleshooting, so check the thermostat or outdoor board for fault codes. Specific codes vary by model and are not listed here.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

**Stop first if you notice any danger signs.** If you smelled burning, saw smoke or sparks, or see scorch marks, or if the breaker panel or outdoor disconnect is hot or buzzing, leave the power off. Do not reset the breaker. Call a technician or electrician right away.

If none of those signs are present, these checks are safe:

- **Note the trip pattern.** Did it trip instantly at startup or after running a while? Was there humming, buzzing or a burning smell first? This information helps the technician most.
- **Reset the breaker one time only.** Do NOT reset if you smelled burning, saw smoke or sparks, or see scorch marks; leave it off and call. Otherwise, turn the thermostat to OFF first. Wait 5 minutes so the compressor pressures can equalize. Reset the breaker, then set the thermostat to COOL. If it trips again, leave the breaker off and call a pro. Do not keep resetting it.
- **Check that the outdoor disconnect is fully seated.** Look at the pull-out block or lever box beside the condenser. Only check that it's in the ON position. Don't open the box itself or touch anything inside.
- **Replace a dirty air filter.** A clogged filter raises system load and can freeze the indoor coil.
- **Clear the outdoor unit.** Remove leaves, grass clippings and debris from around the condenser, and keep about 2 feet of clearance. You can gently hose the outside fins from outside the unit with the power off. Don't remove panels.
- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's set to COOL with a reasonable setpoint. Replace the batteries if the display is weak.
- **Open supply registers and keep returns unblocked.**
- **Check the condensate line and float switch area for standing water.** A tripped float switch normally shuts the system off without tripping the breaker. Still, water near the air handler wiring is worth reporting.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician should do roughly this, which you can use to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Inspect the breaker and panel.** They check the breaker size against the nameplate's MOCP and minimum circuit ampacity (MCA). They also look for heat damage and confirm the wire gauge is correct.
2. **Do a visual inspection inside the condenser.** They look for burned wires, a melted compressor terminal plug, a pitted contactor and a bulging or leaking capacitor.
3. **Test the capacitor.** They measure microfarads against the rating, for both the HERM and FAN sides.
4. **Test the compressor windings.** They take ohm readings across C-S-R and do an insulation test to ground, ideally with a megohmmeter. A short to ground condemns the compressor. They should show you the readings.
5. **Check the fan motor windings and bearing.** They test the windings for shorts and check whether the motor spins freely.
6. **Take amp-draw readings.** With the unit running, if it will, they compare amps against the RLA and LRA on the nameplate. A clamp meter shows hard starting and overload.
7. **Check refrigerant pressures and coil condition.** They look for high head pressure, a dirty condenser coil or overcharge.
8. **On XV models,** they read the stored inverter fault history and follow Trane's drive diagnostics.

**Red flag:** someone condemns the compressor without measuring the windings to ground, or without first replacing a failed capacitor and retesting.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burning smell or scorch marks at the disconnect or panel | Loose or overheated connection | Leave the power off and call now; do not reset | Replace burned lugs, wire, disconnect or breaker |
| Trips instantly every time the AC starts | Grounded compressor, shorted fan motor or shorted wiring | Leave the breaker off after one reset | Insulation test of windings and wiring; repair the short or replace the component |
| Outdoor unit hums, then the breaker trips | Failed capacitor or locked compressor | One reset after a 5-minute wait, only if there was no burning smell | Test and replace the capacitor; add a hard-start kit or diagnose the compressor |
| Trips after running 20+ minutes on hot days | High head pressure, weak capacitor or worn breaker | Clean debris from around the unit; change the filter | Clean the coil; check charge, amps and the breaker |
| Outdoor fan not spinning before the trip | Fan motor or fan capacitor failure | Visual check only, from outside | Replace the fan motor or capacitor |
| Indoor air handler breaker (120 V) trips | Blower motor or control wiring short | One reset, then call | Test the blower motor and wiring |
| XV unit trips with a fault code on display | Inverter or drive failure | Record the code | Trane inverter diagnostics; replace the drive or board |

**Note:** If the outdoor unit hums and shuts off but the breaker does not trip, that is a related but different pattern. A locked or hard-starting compressor more often trips its internal thermal overload, which resets itself, than the breaker. The same capacitor and compressor checks apply, but this page focuses on breaker trips.

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, parts plus labor, as of 2026:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $90 – $200 |
| Run capacitor (dual) | $150 – $400 |
| Contactor | $150 – $350 |
| Hard-start kit | $200 – $450 |
| Wiring repair or replacement of the whip or disconnect | $150 – $500 |
| Breaker replacement (electrician or HVAC) | $150 – $350 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350 – $800 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100 – $250 |
| Inverter drive board (XV models) | $800 – $2,000+ |
| Compressor replacement | $1,800 – $3,500+ |

Trane limited warranties on the compressor and parts are typically 10 years with registration, and some models carry longer coverage. Terms vary by model and registration date, so check your model's warranty card and your registration first. Labor is usually not covered. On older R-22 systems, a grounded compressor often makes full system replacement more sensible.

## What this code means

"Ice on outdoor unit" isn't a fault code. Most Goodman heat pumps won't flash anything when it happens. Communicating (ComfortNet/ComfortBridge) and inverter models may store related fault codes; check your model's documentation. Because it isn't a code, there is nothing to reset. Ice either clears through a normal defrost cycle or keeps building until someone fixes the cause.

In heating mode the outdoor coil pulls heat out of cold air, so it runs below freezing and collects frost. Goodman units handle this with a defrost cycle:

- **Time/temperature boards (most GSZ and SSZ units):** The board checks the coil every 30, 60 or 90 minutes, depending on a jumper setting. If the coil sensor reads cold enough, it reverses the system to melt the frost.
- **Communicating and inverter models:** These may use different defrost logic. Check your model's documentation for how and when it defrosts.

**What's normal vs. not normal:**

- **Normal:** Light, even white frost that clears every hour or so, often with steam.
- **Not normal:**
  - Thick, clear or glassy ice
  - Ice on the top panel or fan blades
  - Ice that only builds on one side or on the bottom rows
  - A unit that is solidly encased

This page covers why ice builds up in general. If you have already confirmed the unit never goes into defrost, see "Goodman Heat Pump Frozen and Not Defrosting: Fixes."

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Water dripping or pooling onto the unit from outside.** Roof runoff, overflowing or missing gutters, or a downspout aimed at the unit puts water on the coil, and it freezes. This is a very common cause of heavy ice on the top or one side. No defrost cycle can keep up with it.
2. **Unit buried in snow or sitting too low to drain.** During defrost, meltwater has to drain away from the base. If the pad has sunk, snow is drifted against the cabinet, or the unit isn't raised off the ground in a snowy area, the meltwater refreezes. Ice then builds up from the bottom rows.
3. **Freezing rain or ice storm conditions.** Heavy icing during freezing rain can temporarily overwhelm a healthy unit. It should clear within a few cycles once the weather breaks.
4. **Defrost control problem.** Possible faults include:
   - A failed defrost board
   - A failed or loose coil (defrost) sensor
   - An interval set too long for local conditions

   Defrost logic differs between time/temperature boards and communicating or inverter controls, so the technician's checks differ too. See the related defrost page for detail.
5. **Outdoor fan problem.** A failed fan motor or capacitor stops airflow across the coil. On many boards the fan should also stop during defrost. If the fan keeps running, defrost can't finish.
6. **Low refrigerant charge (leak).** An undercharged system runs a colder coil. This often shows up as uneven frost, especially on the lower or inlet section.
7. **Reversing valve or its solenoid not shifting.** If the valve can't switch into cooling mode, defrost never actually warms the coil.

**A separate comfort issue, not an icing cause: low indoor airflow.** A clogged filter, closed registers or a blocked return cuts heating output. In heating mode the indoor coil is the condenser, so restricted indoor airflow raises head pressure; it does not explain ice on the outdoor coil. It's still worth fixing if your home feels short on heat.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Check for water hitting the unit.** Look above and around it for dripping gutters, roof edges or downspouts. Redirecting a downspout or clearing a gutter often solves the problem permanently.
- **Clear snow, leaves and drifts.** Keep at least 1–2 feet clear around the cabinet. Use your hands or a soft broom. Do not chip, scrape or pry ice off the coil or fan.
- **Check that the unit sits level and up off the ground.** Make sure meltwater has somewhere to go. Note it for your technician if the pad has sunk.
- **Replace or check the air filter.** A dirty filter reduces indoor airflow and heating output. It won't fix outdoor ice, but it helps comfort.
- **Open supply registers and clear return grilles.** Make sure furniture and rugs aren't blocking them.
- **Check thermostat settings and batteries.** Confirm the thermostat is in Heat, not Cool. Replace weak batteries.
- **Watch for one defrost cycle.** In cold, damp weather a working unit usually defrosts within about 30–90 minutes. Signs of a defrost cycle:
  - The fan stops
  - You hear a whoosh
  - Steam rises from the coil
- **Check the breaker and outdoor disconnect.** If the outdoor fan isn't spinning at all, check that the breaker and disconnect are on. If either has tripped or is off, reset it once. At the disconnect, only flip the handle or reinsert the pull-out; don't open the disconnect box or touch any wiring. If the breaker trips again, leave it off and call.
- **Switch to emergency heat if ice is heavy.** If ice is thick, covers the whole coil, or touches the fan, set the thermostat to EM Heat and call a pro. Don't pour hot water on the unit. The sudden temperature change can stress parts, and the water refreezes.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will usually work through these steps:

- **Inspect the site.** They will look for runoff, drainage problems, unit height and clearances. This is often the cheapest answer.
- **Check indoor airflow if heat is weak.** They will look at the filter, blower and static pressure. This addresses comfort, not the outdoor ice.
- **Force a defrost cycle and watch it.** Many Goodman boards have test pins that start defrost on demand. The tech confirms that:
  - The reversing valve shifts
  - The outdoor fan stops
  - The coil warms and clears
- **Test the defrost sensor and board.** They will check the coil sensor's resistance against its temperature chart, check sensor placement and clip contact, and confirm the board's interval jumper setting. On communicating (ComfortNet/ComfortBridge) or inverter models that store fault codes, they will read the stored fault history.
- **Test the outdoor fan motor and capacitor.** They will check the run capacitor, the motor windings and the fan relay.
- **Measure refrigerant pressures and temperatures.** They calculate superheat and subcooling to judge the charge. If it's low, they should leak-search before adding refrigerant. Topping off with no leak search is a red flag on a quote.
- **Check the reversing valve solenoid.** They will check the coil voltage and confirm the valve actually shifts.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heavy ice on top or one side, under the roofline | Roof or gutter runoff | Fix the gutter or redirect the downspout | Relocate or add a cover/shield if needed |
| Ice building up from the base | Poor drainage, unit too low, snow drift | Clear snow around the unit | Raise the unit on a riser or stand, re-level the pad |
| Light white frost that clears regularly | Normal operation | None | None |
| Weak heat indoors (separate from any ice) | Low indoor airflow (comfort issue, not an icing cause) | Replace the filter, open registers | Check the blower and static pressure |
| Ice never clears, no steam or whoosh | Defrost board or sensor fault | Switch to EM Heat | Test the board and sensor, force defrost, replace parts |
| Fan not spinning, coil iced | Fan motor or capacitor failure | Check the breaker and disconnect once | Replace the capacitor or motor |
| Frost only on the bottom rows | Low refrigerant charge | Switch to EM Heat | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Defrost "runs" but the coil stays iced | Reversing valve not shifting | Switch to EM Heat | Test the solenoid, repair or replace the valve |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges, parts and labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Gutter or downspout fix (DIY to handyman) | $0–$250 |
| Unit riser or stand and re-leveling | $150–$500 |
| Air filter | $10–$40 |
| Defrost sensor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Defrost control board replacement | $350–$800 |
| Inverter or communicating control | Can exceed $800 |
| Outdoor fan capacitor | $120–$300 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350–$800 |
| Reversing valve solenoid coil | $150–$350 |
| Reversing valve replacement (brazing and recharge) | $900–$2,000+ |
| Refrigerant leak search and repair with recharge | $300–$1,500+ |

Refrigerant costs vary widely by type, especially for R-410A vs. newer refrigerants. Many Goodman units carry 10-year parts warranties if they were registered, so labor is often the bigger share of the bill.

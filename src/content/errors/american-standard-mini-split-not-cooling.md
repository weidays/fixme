## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a single fault code. It is a symptom: your American Standard ductless system is running, or trying to run, but the room isn't getting cool. This page covers ductless and mini-split systems specifically. If you have a central ducted system, see the separate American Standard AC Not Cooling page.

Mini-splits behave differently from central AC in ways that matter here:

- **Inverter compressors.** These ramp speed up and down. A unit that is "running" may be throttled back because of a protection condition.
- **Indoor-head filters and coils.** The filter and coil sit inside the wall unit itself, so they clog faster.
- **Multi-zone systems.** On these, one outdoor unit cannot heat in one room and cool in another at the same time.

Many American Standard ductless models also show a fault code or blinking LED pattern on the indoor unit when a protection trips. Which codes appear, and whether the unit restarts on its own after a protection event or holds until power is cycled, **varies by model series**. Some inverter faults retry automatically after a timed delay. Others lock out. Check your owner's manual for your model number, which is on the label on the side of the indoor head.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty indoor-head filter or coil.** Restricted airflow across the indoor coil is the most common cause of weak cooling, and it can lead to coil icing.
2. **Wrong mode, setpoint, or remote issue.** The remote is set to Fan, Dry, Auto or Heat, the setpoint is too high, the batteries are dead, or a timer or sleep mode is active.
3. **Multi-zone mode conflict.** Another indoor head is calling for heat, so the outdoor unit won't cool. Some systems display a mode-conflict indicator when this happens.
4. **Outdoor unit blocked or not running.** Debris, vegetation, snow guards or covers can choke airflow. A tripped breaker or outdoor disconnect can also stop the unit.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Flare connections at the line set are a well-known leak point on ductless systems.
6. **Outdoor fan motor, temperature sensor (thermistor), or inverter/control board fault.** These usually trigger a displayed fault code.
7. **Indoor-to-outdoor communication wiring fault.** The outdoor unit never receives the call to cool. This usually also produces a code.
8. **Condensate backup.** On units with a condensate pump or float switch, a full pump or tripped float can shut down cooling.
9. **Inverter compressor failure.** This is the least common cause and the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Remote and mode.** Replace the remote batteries. Confirm the display shows **Cool**, not Fan, Dry, Auto or Heat. Set the temperature at least 3–4°F below room temperature. Cancel any timer, sleep or eco mode.
- **Multi-zone heads.** Set every indoor unit on the system to Cool, or turn off any head set to Heat.
- **Filters.** Lift the indoor unit's front panel as shown in your owner's manual. Slide out the mesh filters, rinse them with lukewarm water, let them dry completely, and reinstall them. Do not remove anything else inside the head.
- **Breaker and disconnect.** Check the breaker panel and the outdoor disconnect box. If either is tripped, you may reset it **once**. If the unit stopped with a fault code, you may also power-cycle it once: turn it off for about five minutes, then turn it back on. If it trips or faults again, stop and call a technician.
- **Outdoor unit clearance.** Clear leaves, grass and debris from around the unit. Remove any cover. Keep about 2 feet of clearance. Do not open or hose out the inside of the unit.
- **Indoor airflow.** Make sure furniture or curtains aren't blocking the head, and the louvers aren't stuck closed.
- **Condensate.** Look for water dripping from the head or a backed-up drain line outlet outside. If your system has a visible condensate pump with a full reservoir, note it for the technician.
- **Record any code.** Write down any code or blink pattern exactly as shown, plus the model numbers of both the indoor and outdoor units.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified technician should:

- **Read the fault code history** and compare it against the service manual for your specific series.
- **Measure temperature split** across the indoor coil and check indoor airflow. They should inspect the indoor coil and blower wheel for dirt or mold buildup.
- **Confirm the outdoor unit is responding.** That means the compressor and fan are running, and voltage and communication signals are present on the interconnect wiring.
- **Check refrigerant pressures and superheat/subcooling** against the manufacturer's charging chart. If the charge is low, they should **leak-test**, typically starting at the flare fittings, before recharging.
- **Test thermistors** (room, indoor coil, outdoor coil, discharge) against resistance tables.
- **Evaluate outdoor fan motor and inverter/control board** operation with meter readings, not guesswork.

Red flags in a quote:

- A recharge with no leak search
- A board replacement without checking sensors and wiring first
- A compressor diagnosis without pressure and electrical data

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, slightly cool air | Clogged filter or dirty coil | Clean the indoor-head filters | Deep-clean the coil and blower wheel |
| Air not cold, display shows fan icon | Wrong mode or remote problem | Set to Cool, replace remote batteries | None, unless the remote or receiver is faulty |
| One room won't cool on a multi-zone system | Mode conflict with another head | Set all heads to Cool | Check settings and controls |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or disconnect, communication fault | Reset breaker or disconnect once | Test power, interconnect wiring and board |
| Outdoor unit runs, air still warm, ice on lines | Low refrigerant from a leak | Turn the system off and let it thaw | Find and repair the leak, evacuate, recharge |
| Fault code on display | Sensor, fan motor, board or protection trip | Record the code, power-cycle once | Diagnose per the service manual, replace the failed part |
| Water dripping, unit shuts off | Condensate backup or pump/float switch | Check the drain outlet | Clear the drain, service or replace the pump |
| Compressor hums or trips, no cooling | Inverter compressor or drive failure | Turn off and call | Electrical and pressure diagnosis, compressor or board replacement |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges. Parts availability and labor rates vary by region and model.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Filter cleaning, mode fix, breaker reset | $0 DIY |
| Service call and diagnosis | $100–$250 |
| Indoor coil and blower deep cleaning | $150–$450 per head |
| Condensate drain clearing or pump replacement | $150–$450 |
| Thermistor (temperature sensor) replacement | $150–$400 |
| Communication wiring repair | $150–$500 |
| Leak repair at flare fittings plus recharge | $350–$1,200 |
| Line-set or coil leak repair plus recharge | $800–$2,000+ |

Warranty coverage may reduce parts cost. Check your registration status before approving major repairs.

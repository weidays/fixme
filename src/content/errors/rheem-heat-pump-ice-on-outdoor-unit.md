## What this code means

"Ice on outdoor unit" is not a numbered fault code. It is something you see on the condenser cabinet of your Rheem or Ruud heat pump.

**When it's normal**

In heating mode, the outdoor coil runs colder than the outdoor air, so moisture freezes onto it. Rheem heat pumps handle this with a defrost cycle. The unit briefly reverses into cooling mode and stops the outdoor fan, which warms the coil and melts the frost.

- **Older and entry-level models** run defrost on a timer. The interval is typically jumper-selectable at 30, 60 or 90 minutes, and defrost only runs if the coil sensor reads cold.
- **Many newer and variable-speed models** (including EcoNet-equipped units) use demand defrost. Sensors decide when to defrost.

**When it's a problem**

- Ice is thick and clear, or keeps getting thicker.
- Ice covers the top of the unit or fills the base.
- Only part of the coil is iced.
- The unit ices up in cooling season.

Ice at that level blocks airflow, cuts heating capacity and can damage the fan or coil. Because the causes behind persistent ice usually need refrigerant gauges or electrical testing, this is a **pro-level** problem.

**Resetting:** There is no stored code to clear. Normal frost clears automatically. Abnormal ice comes back until the underlying cause is fixed.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Water dripping or pooling onto the unit.** Leaking gutters, roof runoff, or melting snow can drip onto the top of the unit. That water refreezes into a cap of ice that defrost can't keep up with. Drifting snow or a unit sitting in a low spot can also bury the coil. This is the most common cause of a heavy ice "shell," especially ice on top of the cabinet.
2. **Restricted airflow.**
   - *Outdoors:* snow, leaves or shrubs around the coil, or a matted, dirty coil.
   - *Indoors:* a clogged filter or closed registers lower system pressures. This is the leading cause of icing in cooling mode.
3. **Base pan ice buildup.** Defrost meltwater can't drain if the unit sits flat on the ground or the pan's drain openings are blocked. The water refreezes from the bottom up and eventually reaches the coil and fan blade. Rheem installation guidance calls for raising the unit above expected snow levels in cold climates.
4. **Low refrigerant charge (leak).** A typical sign is ice on the lower part of the coil or near the service valves, while the rest of the coil stays clear. This happens in both heating and cooling. Low charge always means there is a leak.
5. **Defrost system fault.** Possible failures include the defrost control board, coil sensor or thermostat, outdoor air sensor (demand-defrost models), or reversing valve. The unit either never defrosts or defrosts incompletely. This is covered in depth on the Frozen, Not Defrosting page.
6. **Outdoor fan problem during heating.** A failed fan motor or capacitor (inside the cabinet) leaves the coil without airflow, so it ices quickly. Ice can also lock the fan blade itself.
7. **Expected heavy-frost conditions.** Freezing fog or temperatures near 32–40°F with high humidity can frost a healthy unit quickly between defrosts. The frost should still clear completely in each cycle.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Never chip, scrape or hammer ice off the coil, and never pour boiling water on it.

1. **Check the thermostat.**
   - Confirm it is set to Heat (or Cool in summer) and that the batteries are fresh.
   - If the unit is heavily iced, switch to **Emergency Heat** (if you have it). Electric or gas backup will keep the house warm while the heat pump rests.
   - In summer, set the system to **Off** with the fan **On** to thaw an iced coil.
2. **Replace the air filter.** A clogged filter is a common, cheap-to-fix cause, especially for summer icing.
3. **Open the supply registers and clear the return grilles.** Make sure furniture or rugs aren't blocking them.
4. **Clear around the outdoor unit from the outside.**
   - Gently brush loose snow off the top and sides with a broom.
   - Keep at least 2 feet clear around the cabinet and 5 feet above it.
   - Do not remove panels.
5. **Look for water sources.**
   - Is a gutter, downspout or roof edge dripping onto the unit? Redirect or repair it.
   - Is water pooling around the base? Clear snow and debris away from the pad so meltwater can run off.
6. **Check the breaker and disconnect.** Make sure the outdoor breaker and the disconnect box near the unit are on.
7. **Watch one cycle.** Observe for 60–90 minutes. A healthy unit goes quiet, the fan stops, steam rises and frost melts. If ice keeps building with no defrost, turn the heat pump off (use Emergency Heat) and call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician will usually work through these steps:

- **Inspect the ice pattern.**
  - Full coil, uniform ice usually points to defrost or airflow.
  - A cap of ice on top points to an external water source.
  - Ice only on the bottom rows or at the service valves suggests low charge.
  - A solid base pan points to drainage or a unit that sits too low.
- **Safely clear the ice.** They will force a defrost cycle (many Rheem boards have a test pins or test mode) or melt the ice with water, then confirm the fan blade spins freely.
- **Measure pressures and temperatures.** Readings of suction pressure, superheat and subcooling against Rheem's charging chart show whether the charge is low. If it is, they should leak-search rather than just "top off."
- **Test the defrost circuit.**
  - Check the coil sensor or defrost thermostat with a meter.
  - Check sensor resistance on demand-defrost boards.
  - Confirm the board energizes the reversing valve and stops the fan on defrost.
  - Verify that the reversing valve actually shifts.
  - Check any fault codes on the board LED or the EcoNet thermostat.
- **Check the outdoor fan.** They will test the fan motor and capacitor.
- **Check indoor airflow.** This includes static pressure and blower operation, especially for summer icing.
- **Review the installation.** They should look at pad height, clearances and drainage, and may recommend a riser stand.

Be cautious of a quote that adds refrigerant without a leak check, or replaces the board without first testing the sensors.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thick ice cap on top of the cabinet | Dripping gutter or roof runoff | Redirect the gutter or downspout; brush off loose snow | Clear the ice safely; check the fan and coil for damage |
| Ice building up from the base pan | Poor drainage; unit too low | Clear snow and debris away from the pad | Raise the unit on a riser; clear the pan drains |
| Light frost that clears every 30–90 minutes | Normal operation | None | None needed |
| Ice on the whole coil, never defrosts | Defrost board, sensor or reversing valve fault | Switch to Emergency Heat; call a technician | Test the defrost circuit; replace the failed part |
| Ice only on the lower coil or service valves | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn the heat pump off; call a technician | Leak search, repair and recharge |
| Ice in summer (cooling mode) | Dirty filter, blocked vents, low charge | Replace the filter; open the vents; run fan-only to thaw | Check airflow and charge |
| Outdoor fan not spinning while the compressor runs | Fan motor or capacitor | Check the breaker; call a technician | Replace the capacitor or motor |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges including labor. Prices vary by region and warranty coverage.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Air filter | $10–$40 (DIY) |
| Gutter or downspout redirect | $0–$300 |
| Service call and diagnosis | $100–$250 |
| Riser stand or raising the unit | $150–$500 |
| Defrost sensor or thermostat | $150–$350 |
| Outdoor fan capacitor | $120–$300 |
| Defrost control board | $250–$650 |
| Outdoor fan motor | $350–$800 |
| Leak search, repair and recharge | $400–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type, R-410A vs. R-454B) |
| Reversing valve replacement | $900–$2,000 |

Many Rheem parts carry a 10-year limited warranty when the unit was registered, but labor usually isn't covered.

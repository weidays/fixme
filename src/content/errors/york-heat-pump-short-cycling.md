## What this code means

"Short cycling" means your York heat pump starts and stops too often. It runs a few minutes, shuts off before the house reaches the setpoint, then restarts. York does not show this as a single fault code. It is a symptom.

Depending on the board, the outdoor control may log related events. Examples include high- or low-pressure switch trips or anti-short-cycle delay activity. On some models these show as LED flash patterns.

York outdoor boards include an anti-short-cycle timer, usually around 5 minutes. This delay keeps the compressor from restarting right away. After repeated pressure-switch trips in a short window, many York boards lock out the compressor. Some lockouts clear on their own after a set time. Others hold until power is cycled. Check your board's legend, which is usually printed inside the control panel cover, for your exact behavior.

This page covers heat pump compressor cycling. If you have dual-fuel and the gas furnace is the part cycling, see the York furnace short cycling page instead.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat problems.** These include a weak battery, poor placement (near a supply register, in sun, or by a drafty door), the wrong heat pump settings, or a cycle-rate setting that is too aggressive.
2. **Restricted airflow.** A clogged filter, closed registers or a blocked return can starve the indoor coil. In cooling this causes low pressure and coil icing. In heating it causes high head pressure. Either way, a pressure switch can trip.
3. **Dirty or blocked outdoor coil.** Debris, leaves or snow on the outdoor coil raise head pressure in cooling. This can trip the high-pressure switch.
4. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Low charge causes low suction pressure, so the low-pressure switch opens and closes repeatedly.
5. **Faulty pressure switch, wiring or control board.** A failing high/low switch, a loose connection, or a board fault can drop the call for the compressor.
6. **Failing compressor components or contactor.** A weak run capacitor, pitted contactor, or compressor overheating and tripping its internal overload all cause stop-start running.
7. **Oversized system.** An oversized unit satisfies the thermostat too fast. This is a design issue rather than a failure.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Replace the batteries. Confirm the thermostat is set for a heat pump system. Make sure it is in the right mode with a stable setpoint, and remove any aggressive schedules. If it sits near a supply vent or in direct sun, note that for the technician.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it is dirty. This is the most common fix.
- **Vents and returns:** Open the supply registers. Clear furniture and rugs away from the return grilles.
- **Outdoor unit, visible area only:** Clear away leaves, snow and debris, leaving about 2 feet of open space. Do not open the panels or spray inside the cabinet.
- **Condensate:** Check that the indoor drain line and any pan float switch aren't full or tripped. A tripped float switch can kill the cooling call.
- **Breaker/disconnect:** Make sure the breakers are fully on and the panels are seated.
- **One reset only:** If the unit seems locked out, turn the thermostat off. Then cycle the breaker off for about 5 minutes and back on, one time. If short cycling comes back, stop and call a pro. Repeated resets can damage the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- Watch a full cycle and note what drops out first: the thermostat call, the contactor, or the compressor on its internal overload.
- Read the outdoor board LED fault history or flash codes, and on communicating systems, the stored faults.
- Check the static pressure and the indoor blower airflow, and inspect the indoor coil for dirt or ice.
- Attach gauges or probes to measure suction and discharge pressures, superheat and subcooling. If charge is low, they should look for a leak before adding refrigerant.
- Test the high- and low-pressure switches for continuity and confirm their trip points.
- Check the capacitor's microfarad rating, the contactor contacts, the compressor amp draw and the winding resistance.
- Check thermostat wiring and voltage for loose conductors or an intermittent 24V supply.
- Compare equipment capacity against the home's load if the system seems oversized.

A good quote names the specific failed part or the measured problem. Be wary of a quote that just says "add refrigerant" without any leak check.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short runs, thermostat display blank or flickering | Weak batteries or loose thermostat wiring | Replace batteries | Repair or tighten wiring, replace thermostat |
| Cycles off quickly, weak airflow at vents | Clogged filter or blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents | Measure static pressure, clean blower or coil |
| Short cycling in cooling with ice on indoor lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant charge | Replace filter, turn system off to thaw | Check charge, leak search and repair |
| Short cycling in hot weather, outdoor coil dirty | High head pressure trip | Clear debris around unit | Clean coil, test high-pressure switch and fan |
| Outdoor unit stops but indoor fan keeps running | Pressure switch trip or compressor overload | One power reset only | Diagnose switch, capacitor, contactor, compressor |
| Runs short cycles, house reaches temperature fast | Oversized unit or thermostat placement | Note thermostat location | Adjust cycle settings, relocate thermostat, assess sizing |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges, including labor:

- Air filter: $10–$40 (DIY)
- Thermostat replacement: $150–$450 installed, or $40–$250 for a DIY-compatible model
- Service call/diagnosis: $90–$200
- Outdoor coil cleaning: $100–$250
- Run capacitor: $150–$350
- Contactor: $150–$350
- High- or low-pressure switch: $200–$450
- Outdoor control board: $350–$800
- Refrigerant leak search and repair plus recharge: $400–$1,500+, depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- Compressor replacement: $1,800–$3,500+, often enough to prompt a system replacement discussion

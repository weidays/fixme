## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code — it's the symptom you'll describe to a technician when your Trane air conditioner runs but the house won't reach setpoint, or the system won't come on at all. Trane single-stage units (XR and XL series) generally don't display detailed fault codes at the outdoor unit; you diagnose them by observing behavior. Communicating **ComfortLink II** systems (XV18, XV20i and matching thermostats) *do* log fault codes on the thermostat display, so if you have one of those, check the thermostat's alert/service menu for a specific code before assuming a generic problem.

Because refrigerant charge, compressor, and high-voltage electrical work are all involved in most no-cooling failures, this is rated a **pro** repair. There are a handful of safe checks you can do first, but the diagnosis usually requires gauges and a meter.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops cooling capacity and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer so warm air blows.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common electrical failure. The compressor and/or condenser fan can't start; you may hear humming or clicking.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system runs constantly but can't cool. Never a "top-off" fix; the leak must be found.
5. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit burns or sticks, so the condenser never energizes.
6. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — a filthy coil can't reject heat, so cooling drops off, especially on hot afternoons.
7. **Thermostat or wiring fault** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a broken low-voltage wire.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — often a symptom of a deeper electrical problem.
9. **Failed compressor** — least common but most expensive; the heart of the system won't pump.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Check the filter.** A clogged filter is the number-one cause. Replace a dirty one and wait to see if cooling returns.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Look for ice.** If you see frost or ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil/air handler, turn the system to OFF (fan can stay on) and let it thaw fully — several hours. Running a frozen unit makes it worse.
- **Check the breaker.** Look for a tripped AC breaker in your panel. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Check supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs or closed registers aren't starving the system of airflow.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a float switch and shut the system down. Clear standing water and a visible clog if you can reach it safely.
- **Rinse the outdoor unit gently.** With the power OFF at the disconnect, hose leaves and debris off the outside of the condenser coil from the outside in. Don't bend the fins.

Do these and give the system 15–30 minutes. If it still won't cool, shut it off at the thermostat and call a technician rather than letting it labor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable sequence — you can sanity-check a quote against this:

1. **Confirms the complaint** and checks thermostat call for cooling with a meter (24V at the contactor).
2. **Inspects airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition; measures temperature split across the indoor coil.
3. **Tests the capacitor** with a capacitance meter and inspects the **contactor** for pitting or chatter.
4. **Checks the run capacitor and fan motor** if the outdoor fan isn't spinning.
5. **Attaches gauges** to read suction and liquid pressures, and measures superheat/subcooling to judge refrigerant charge. Low charge triggers a **leak search** (electronic detector or dye).
6. **Reads ComfortLink II fault logs** on communicating XV-series systems for compressor, sensor, or communication faults.
7. **Checks compressor windings and amp draw** if all else is ruled out.

Be cautious of any tech who adds refrigerant without doing a leak search, or condemns a compressor without electrical tests.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Dirty filter / blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents | Measure airflow, clean coil |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, let thaw fully | Find airflow or charge problem |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Bad capacitor or fan motor | Power off, don't spin by hand | Replace capacitor or motor |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor dead | Contactor or control fault | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, low-voltage wiring |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| System totally dead | Tripped breaker / blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Find electrical fault |
| Water near air handler, shutdown | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, empty pan | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 professionally
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600 (varies by leak location and R-410A/R-454B pricing)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth weighing against full system replacement on older units)

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type, and whether your Trane is still under parts warranty — registered Trane units often carry a 10-year parts warranty, so ask.

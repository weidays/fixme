## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink or fault code on Trane equipment — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, thermostat calling) but the air coming out of your vents is warm or barely cool, and the indoor temperature won't come down to setpoint.

This happens across Trane split-system ACs and heat pumps (XR, XL, and XV series). On conventional systems there's usually no error display at all — you just feel warm air. On **communicating ComfortLink II systems**, the thermostat may show a system message or fault, and the specific text varies by model and software version. Check your thermostat screen for any code and cross-reference it if one appears.

Because "not cooling" can stem from anything from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you from the cheapest, safest checks up to what a technician should be doing.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged or dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the coil. Most common and cheapest.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. Vents blow warm because ice blocks heat transfer.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common outdoor-unit failure. The compressor or condenser fan won't start, so no cooling happens.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system runs constantly but can't cool. Requires certified diagnosis and repair.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling drops off, especially on hot days.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit fails to close, leaving the condenser dead.
7. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a broken wire not calling for cooling.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the unit runs but produces no cooling. Expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here — don't open sealed panels or touch refrigerant or wiring.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak cooling and frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker on the AC (there are usually two — indoor air handler/furnace and outdoor condenser). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil check:** Look at the outdoor unit's refrigerant lines and, if visible, the indoor coil. Ice on the copper line or coil means a frozen system — **turn the AC off, switch the fan to ON** to help it thaw (can take several hours), and replace the filter. If it re-freezes, you likely have low refrigerant or an airflow problem needing a pro.
- **Condensate:** Check that the condensate drain isn't clogged and overflowing — a full safety float switch can shut cooling down on some systems.

If none of these restore cooling, the next steps involve electrical testing, refrigerant, or sealed components — call a licensed HVAC technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path, so you can sanity-check the visit:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling** — verify the thermostat is sending a Y signal and the low-voltage circuit is intact.
2. **Check power at the outdoor unit** — measure voltage at the disconnect and contactor.
3. **Test the capacitor** — with a meter, confirm the run capacitor's microfarad rating is within spec. Weak or swollen caps get replaced.
4. **Inspect the contactor** — for pitting or failure to pull in.
5. **Measure refrigerant pressures** — gauges on the high and low sides, compared against target superheat/subcooling for your model. This reveals low charge (a leak) or restrictions.
6. **Check airflow and coil condition** — inspect the evaporator and condenser coils for dirt or ice.
7. **Test the compressor** — amp draw and windings if a start/run problem is suspected.

Expect a diagnostic fee of **$85–$175**, often credited toward the repair. Be wary of any tech who wants to "just add refrigerant" without leak-testing — that's a temporary fix on a system that will fail again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, dirty filter | Clogged filter restricting airflow | Replace filter | Verify airflow, check for coil damage |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Frozen evaporator (low airflow/charge) | Turn off, run fan to thaw, swap filter | Find airflow or refrigerant cause |
| Outdoor fan/compressor won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant from a leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Outdoor coil caked in dirt/grass | Dirty condenser coil | Gently rinse with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean, verify pressures |
| No response, blank thermostat | Dead batteries or thermostat fault | Replace batteries | Diagnose thermostat/wiring |
| Runs but no cooling, high amps | Failing compressor | None — call pro | Test compressor, quote replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Prices vary by region and model:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250 (or gentle DIY hose rinse, $0)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (older R-22 systems cost far more)
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,200+ (often the point where replacing the whole outdoor unit or system is worth pricing)

A full system replacement, if the unit is old and the compressor is gone, typically runs **$5,000–$12,000+** depending on size and efficiency.

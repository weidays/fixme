## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single Trane error code — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner is powered on and the thermostat is calling for cooling, but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house simply won't reach the set temperature.

On single-stage Trane units (many XR and XL condensers with a standard thermostat), there's usually **no diagnostic code at all** — you just get warm or weak airflow. On variable-speed communicating systems (XV series paired with a Trane ComfortLink II or similar communicating thermostat), you may see a specific fault or function code on the thermostat screen that points to the actual failed component.

Because "not cooling" can mean anything from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you through the likely causes in order, the checks you can safely do yourself, and what a fair diagnosis and repair should cost.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, reduces cooling and can freeze the coil. The single most common cause and a free fix.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" not "cool"), or a bad program schedule.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice on the indoor coil or copper line means no cold air until it thaws.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system slowly loses charge, cools poorly, then not at all. Requires a certified tech.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a common electrical failure; the outdoor fan or compressor hums but won't start.
6. **Bad contactor** — the outdoor unit won't energize even though the thermostat calls for cooling.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit dead, indoor blower may still run.
8. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — caked with dust, grass or cottonwood, so it can't reject heat.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; unit runs but produces no cooling, or trips out.
10. **Blocked condensate drain** — on some systems a full drain pan trips a float safety switch that shuts down cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. **Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

- **Air filter:** Pull it out and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of poor cooling and coil freezing.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel and the outdoor disconnect box for a tripped breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper line at the outdoor unit, **turn the system to OFF and set the fan to ON** to thaw it (this can take 2–4 hours). Never chip at the ice.
- **Condensate drain:** If your indoor unit has a drain pan that's full or overflowing, a safety float switch may have shut cooling down. Clearing standing water can restore operation; a persistent clog needs a tech.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass and debris from around the condenser and gently rinse the outside of the coil fins with a garden hose (power off).

If cooling doesn't return after these checks, it's a pro job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified HVAC tech should work methodically — this helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirm 24V is reaching the contactor.
2. **Check the outdoor unit** — is the contactor pulling in, is the fan spinning, is the compressor running or just humming?
3. **Test the capacitor and contactor** with a meter; these are cheap, common failures.
4. **Measure temperature split** (return vs. supply air) — a healthy system drops air roughly 15–20°F.
5. **Attach gauges and read pressures/superheat/subcool** to determine if the charge is correct — this is how they confirm a refrigerant leak.
6. **Leak search** if the charge is low — dye, electronic sniffer, or soap bubbles at joints and the coil.
7. **On communicating XV systems**, read stored fault codes from the ComfortLink thermostat to pinpoint the component.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add refrigerant" without searching for a leak — that's a temporary and non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm-ish air | Dirty air filter | Replace filter | None if that fixes it |
| Ice on indoor coil or line | Low airflow or low charge | Turn to OFF, fan ON to thaw | Find cause; repair leak or airflow |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed capacitor | None | Test and replace capacitor |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant leak | Note it for the tech | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Blower runs, no cold air at all | Compressor failure | None | Compressor test/replacement |
| Water pooling, unit shut off | Clogged condensate drain | Clear standing water | Clear line, service float switch |
| Outside coil caked with debris | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse fins gently, power off | Deep coil cleaning |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries | Check 24V and transformer |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor). Prices vary by region and whether you're under warranty:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Blower motor / capacitor:** $300–$700
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 (a leaking evaporator coil is at the high end)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500 (often worth weighing against a new system on older units)

If your Trane is still under its registered parts warranty (typically 10 years when registered), you may only pay labor and refrigerant on major components — ask the tech to check the serial number.

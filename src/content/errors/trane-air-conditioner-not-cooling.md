## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single blink code on Trane equipment — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, air moving) but producing warm or room-temperature air, or it may not be starting at all. Because Trane systems range from simple single-stage XR units to variable-speed XV models with communicating controls, the underlying fault can be electrical, airflow-related, or refrigerant-related.

On communicating Trane systems (ComfortLink II / XL and XV series with a matching thermostat), the thermostat itself often displays a fault or alert message that points to the failed component. On older or non-communicating single-stage units, there's usually no on-screen code at all — you diagnose by symptom. Where your model behaves differently, we flag it below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the indoor coil, which can freeze the coil and stop cooling entirely.
2. **Thermostat setting or wiring issue** — set to "fan" instead of "cool," a dead battery, or a schedule/hold problem.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse on the outdoor unit** — the outdoor condenser may be dead while the indoor blower still runs, giving warm air.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with grass, dust, or debris can't reject heat, so the system runs but barely cools.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from restricted airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant line.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling and, over time, coil freezing. Requires a certified tech.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit; pitted or stuck contacts stop the compressor from engaging.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive failure; the unit won't cool despite everything else being fine.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the single most common cause of weak or no cooling.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers** — check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker for the **outdoor unit** (there are usually two AC-related breakers). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain** — a full drain pan or clogged line can trigger a safety float switch that shuts down cooling. Check for standing water near the indoor unit.
- **Outdoor unit** — visually clear grass clippings, leaves, and debris from around and on top of the condenser. Do **not** remove panels or touch internal wiring.

If cooling doesn't return after the filter, thermostat, and a single breaker reset, it's a professional job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work through this sequence — use it to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirm the symptom** — measure supply vs. return air temperature split (should be roughly 15–20°F on a healthy system).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect the filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Verify power and controls** — test the contactor, capacitor, and 24V control signal; on communicating systems, read the fault message at the thermostat or control board.
4. **Test the capacitor** — a microfarad reading confirms whether the run/start capacitor is within spec.
5. **Gauge the refrigerant** — connect manifold gauges to read pressures and superheat/subcooling. Abnormal readings point to a leak or undercharge.
6. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, a proper tech finds the leak (electronic detector or dye) rather than just adding refrigerant.
7. **Compressor test** — measure amp draw and windings if everything upstream checks out.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without a leak search or pressure readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower runs, warm air only | Outdoor unit has no power | Reset the outdoor breaker once | Test contactor, capacitor, wiring |
| Weak airflow, long run times | Clogged filter | Replace the filter | Clean coil, check blower |
| Ice on indoor coil or lineset | Low refrigerant or restricted airflow | Turn AC off, run fan only to thaw | Leak search, recharge, coil service |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor fan runs, compressor doesn't | Bad capacitor or contactor | None — call a pro | Replace capacitor/contactor |
| Nothing runs, water near indoor unit | Condensate float switch tripped | Clear the drain line | Clear drain, check pan and pump |
| Dirty, matted outdoor coil | Blocked condenser | Rinse debris gently with hose | Chemical coil cleaning |
| Cools poorly even after service | Failing compressor | None — call a pro | Compressor amp/winding test, replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges for a Trane AC that isn't cooling. Labor rates and regional pricing vary widely.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat setting/battery:** $0 DIY; $150–$350 for a new thermostat installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing varies)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+
- **Compressor replacement:** $2,000–$3,500+ (often worth comparing against full-system replacement on older units)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$200, frequently credited toward the repair

A note on refrigerant: if your system is low, it has a leak. Paying only to "top off" refrigerant is a temporary fix — insist on a leak search.

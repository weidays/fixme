## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code — it's the most common complaint homeowners bring to a Trane air conditioner. The system is powered and often running, but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or barely below room temperature.

On Trane systems, "not cooling" can mean several very different things: the compressor never starts, the outdoor fan runs but nothing gets cold, the indoor blower moves warm air, or the system short-cycles and never satisfies the thermostat. Some Trane variable-speed and communicating systems (with ComfortLink II controls) will display a fault or error message that points you toward the cause. Single-stage XR-series units usually give no code at all — you diagnose by symptom.

Because refrigerant, electrical, and compressor faults are involved, this is a **pro-level** repair for anything beyond basic airflow and thermostat checks.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked airflow** — the most common and cheapest cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor coil, which then blows warm.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a miswired/failing thermostat.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler may not be getting power.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan hums, buzzes, or won't start.
6. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the lines, and long run times. Always a leak, never "used up."
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; ice blocks cooling entirely.
8. **Failed contactor or capacitor in the outdoor unit** — no compressor engagement.
9. **Blower motor or fan motor failure** — air moves poorly or not at all.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive outcome, usually the last thing confirmed after ruling out the above.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here — do not open sealed refrigerant lines, the compressor, or electrical panels beyond the breaker.

- **Air filter:** Pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Run the system 30 minutes and recheck cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, fan on **AUTO**, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker for the air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor unit:** Make sure the disconnect near the condenser is in the ON position. Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the coil (at least 2 feet of clearance).
- **Supply/return vents:** Confirm registers are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil / lines:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** to thaw it (can take a few hours). Ice usually points to a filter or refrigerant issue for a pro.
- **Condensate drain:** A full drain pan or safety float switch can shut cooling down. Check for standing water and clear a visible clog if you can reach it safely.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Trane technician follows a logical path — a quote should reflect this, not jump straight to "you need a new compressor":

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Airflow check** — inspects filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Electrical checks** — tests the capacitor(s), contactor, and low-voltage fuse; confirms 24V control signal reaches the outdoor unit.
4. **Refrigerant readings** — attaches gauges to check pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low readings mean a leak, which they'll locate with a detector or dye.
5. **Component testing** — checks the compressor and fan motor windings, and reviews any fault history on ComfortLink II/communicating boards.
6. **Report** — you should get a specific failed part or measured leak location, not a vague guess.

If a tech recommends adding refrigerant without finding the leak, get a second opinion — that's a temporary fix that you'll pay for again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm air, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Replace filter, clear vents | Confirm airflow, check coil |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, fan ON to thaw | Find restriction or leak, recharge |
| Outdoor fan runs, air stays warm | Dirty condenser or low charge | Clean coil, clear debris | Test pressures, locate leak |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| No outdoor unit operation | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor/fuse, check control voltage |
| Thermostat blank or wrong | Dead batteries/settings | Replace batteries, set COOL | Replace/rewire thermostat |
| System short-cycles | Overheating, low charge, control fault | Check filter and coil | Diagnose charge, controls, compressor |
| No cooling at all, long run times | Low refrigerant from leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $0 DIY spray – $150–$300 professional cleaning
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Low-voltage fuse:** $75–$150
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Blower or condenser fan motor:** $400–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,200+ (often makes replacing an older unit worth considering)

If your system uses R-22 refrigerant (older units), recharge costs are significantly higher and replacement often makes more financial sense.

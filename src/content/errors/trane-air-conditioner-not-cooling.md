## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Your Trane system may run, blow air, and even show no error at all, yet the air coming from your vents is warm or barely cool. On systems with a communicating thermostat or diagnostic-capable air handler board, you may see a related fault (airflow, sensor, or compressor lockout), but many cooling failures produce no code because the problem is mechanical or refrigerant-related.

The goal on this page is to help you narrow down *why* a Trane air conditioner or heat pump stops cooling, do the handful of checks that are genuinely safe, and understand what a technician should be doing so you can sanity-check the bill.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return airflow** — the most common cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor evaporator coil, which then blows warm air and can shut cooling down.
2. **Thermostat setting or wiring** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool/auto," wrong programmed schedule, dead batteries, or a loose C-wire.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser often has its own breaker or a fused disconnect near the unit.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or fan won't start, or the unit hums and clicks off.
5. **Dirty or blocked condenser coil** — the outdoor coil packed with grass, cottonwood, or dirt can't reject heat.
6. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the compressor/fan burns or sticks.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharge causes weak or no cooling and often a frozen coil. This always means a leak needs repair; refrigerant is not "used up."
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines at the indoor unit.
9. **Clogged condensate drain** — a full pan can trip a float safety switch and stop cooling to prevent water damage.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome. The unit runs but produces no cooling, or trips the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here — do not open sealed refrigerant lines, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the number-one cause of weak cooling and frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool** and a temperature well below room temperature. Set the fan to **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. There's often a separate breaker or a pull-out disconnect box on the wall by the outdoor unit. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for grass, leaves, or debris packed against the condenser fins. With the unit off, you can gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (low pressure, top to bottom).
- **Condensate drain:** Check for standing water in the drain pan or a wet spot near the indoor unit. A clogged drain can trip a float switch and stop cooling.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to **Off** and set the fan to **On** for a few hours to thaw it, then replace the filter. If it re-freezes, you likely have a refrigerant or airflow problem needing a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path, not a parts-swapping shotgun. Expect them to:

1. Verify the thermostat call and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor unit.
2. Check the contactor and measure the **run capacitor** with a meter (a cheap, common failure).
3. Confirm the compressor and condenser fan start and draw normal amperage.
4. Inspect the condenser and evaporator coils for dirt and the coil for ice.
5. Connect **refrigerant gauges** and take temperature readings (superheat/subcooling) to judge the charge and airflow.
6. If refrigerant is low, perform a **leak search** — an undercharged system has a leak that must be repaired, not just topped off.
7. Check static pressure/airflow if the coil keeps freezing.

Ask to see the capacitor reading, the pressure/temperature numbers, and the leak location before approving a recharge. A recharge without finding the leak is a temporary fix you'll pay for again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter / blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents | Check airflow, coil for ice |
| Fan on but warm air at vents | Thermostat set wrong | Set to Cool/Auto, replace batteries | Verify 24V control signal |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker / disconnect | Reset breaker once | Diagnose repeated trips |
| Unit hums then clicks off | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw with fan on, new filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor coil caked in debris | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse exterior with hose | Deep coil cleaning |
| Cooling stops, water near unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear standing water | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Runs constantly, no cooling | Low refrigerant / bad compressor | None — call pro | Leak search, charge, or compressor test |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US pricing including labor. Your area, unit size, and refrigerant type (R-410A vs older R-22) affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (R-410A); more for R-22 or if the coil must be replaced
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,000+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,200+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

If a tech quotes a large repair on a system 12+ years old, ask whether replacement makes more economic sense.

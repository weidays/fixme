## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom Trane owners describe most often. Your air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, thermostat calling) but the air from the vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the setpoint. Because Trane split systems don't put a single blink code on the wall thermostat for "no cooling," diagnosis comes down to tracing where the cooling chain breaks: airflow, refrigerant, electrical, or the compressor itself.

On communicating Trane systems (XV series with a ComfortLink II or newer thermostat), the thermostat **may** display a fault or alert for a specific subsystem. On older single-stage XR/XL units, there's usually no display at all — you're diagnosing by behavior. Where your unit shows a code, note it; the exact meaning varies by board and control generation.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil turns to ice and no cold air moves.
3. **Failed run/dual capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan won't start; you may hear a hum or clicking outside.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cooling gets weak, coils freeze, and it worsens over time. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means a leak.
5. **Failed contactor** — the electrical switch that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the compressor never engages.
6. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil is caked with dirt/grass, so heat can't be rejected and cooling drops.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor disconnect or panel breaker cut power to the condenser.
8. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/config issue.
9. **Failed blower motor or bad control board** — no indoor airflow or no call passed to the outdoor unit.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the unit runs but produces no cooling at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are homeowner-safe. Do them in order and stop if you're unsure.

- **Filter:** Pull the air filter. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL, the fan is on AUTO, and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breaker:** Check the panel and the outdoor disconnect for a tripped breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply vents are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** Check for standing water in the drain pan or a full float switch — a clogged drain can shut cooling off on many systems. Clear visible clogs and add drain tablets.
- **Frozen coil / ice on the outdoor line:** If you see ice on the copper lines or coil, turn the system OFF and set the fan to ON to thaw it (can take hours). If it re-freezes after thawing, you likely have low refrigerant or an airflow problem — call a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. That's licensed work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable process — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** and checks thermostat call, mode, and settings.
2. **Measures the temperature split** (return vs. supply air). A healthy system usually drops air roughly 15–20°F; a small split points to charge or airflow problems.
3. **Inspects airflow** — filter, coil, blower amp draw — to rule out the cheap stuff first.
4. **Checks electrical** — capacitor microfarads under load, contactor condition, voltage at the compressor and fan.
5. **Gauges refrigerant** — connects manifold gauges to read pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low charge sends them to a **leak search** (dye, electronic sniffer, or nitrogen).
6. **Tests the compressor** — windings, amp draw, and start behavior.
7. **Checks the coils** — a dirty condenser or iced evaporator confirms airflow/heat-rejection issues.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without a measured temperature split and a leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm/weak air, dirty filter | Clogged filter, low airflow | Replace filter | Check coil & blower if unresolved |
| Ice on coil or line set | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Find airflow/charge cause, leak search |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed capacitor | None (do not touch) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, breaker OK | Bad contactor or control | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, trace 24V |
| Cooling weak, gets worse over time | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Breaker trips on start | Electrical/compressor fault | Do not keep resetting | Diagnose short/compressor |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Failed compressor or big leak | None | Compressor/system evaluation |
| No indoor airflow at all | Blower motor or board | Check filter/breaker | Replace blower or board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Trane parts often carry a premium over generic equivalents; matched OEM parts may cost more but protect your warranty.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200 (or DIY with tablets)
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak search:** $100–$350
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $300–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type/amount)
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900
- **Control board:** $300–$800
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often makes older units candidates for replacement)
- **Full system replacement:** $6,000–$14,000+ depending on size and efficiency

Note: units still using R-22 refrigerant are expensive to recharge, since R-22 is phased out. If your Trane is older R-22 and leaking, ask whether repair or replacement is smarter.

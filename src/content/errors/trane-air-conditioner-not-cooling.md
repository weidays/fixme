## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific Trane fault code — it's a symptom. Your system may be running with the blower and outdoor unit both on, but the air coming from the vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On some communicating **XV-series** units (XV18, XV20i) with a Trane ComfortLink or XL/color touchscreen thermostat, related conditions *can* trigger a logged fault or alert at the thermostat — but standard **XR** and many **XL** single-stage systems have no digital display and give you no code at all.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a dirty filter to a dead compressor, the goal below is to help you separate the safe, free fixes from the problems that require a licensed HVAC technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection so the system can't dump heat.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks all cooling until it thaws.
4. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a miswired/failed thermostat not calling for cool.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan hums but won't start, or the fan spins slowly.
6. **Bad contactor** — pitted or stuck contactor won't energize the outdoor unit.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — warm air, longer runtimes, possible hissing or ice. Requires EPA-certified repair.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit dead while the indoor blower runs.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; outdoor unit runs but produces no cooling, or trips repeatedly.
10. **Clogged condensate drain safety switch** — a full drain pan float switch cuts cooling to prevent water damage.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the checks any homeowner can safely perform:

- **Filter** — Pull it out. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to **COOL**, fan on **AUTO**, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker** — Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Flip it fully OFF, then back ON **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals a real fault.
- **Supply and return vents** — Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed doors aren't blocking airflow. Restricted return air freezes coils.
- **Outdoor unit** — Look for the condenser coil (outer fins) being caked with grass or debris. You can gently rinse the *outside* fins with a garden hose (unit off), spraying straight in. Don't bend fins or open the unit.
- **Condensate drain** — If you see standing water in the drain pan or near the indoor unit, a clogged drain may have tripped a safety float switch. Clear obvious clogs at the drain line exit outside.
- **Ice check** — If you see ice on the copper line or indoor coil, set the system to **FAN ONLY** for 2–4 hours to thaw before running cool again, and replace the filter.

Do **not** open the outdoor disconnect, touch the capacitor, or attempt to add refrigerant.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech should work methodically — use this to sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirms 24V signal reaches the contactor.
2. **Measure temperature split** — return vs. supply air; a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F. A low split points to refrigerant or airflow issues.
3. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower, coil for ice or dirt.
4. **Check electrical components** — tests the capacitor with a meter, inspects the contactor, and checks the low-voltage fuse.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — reads suction/liquid pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge charge. Low charge triggers a **leak search** (dye, electronic sniffer, or nitrogen).
6. **Amperage test on the compressor** — confirms whether it's drawing correctly, locked, or failing.
7. **Read any stored faults** on communicating XV systems via the thermostat.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or "replace compressor" without amp readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower, ductwork |
| Ice on indoor coil / line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Fan-only to thaw, new filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor unit silent, blower runs | Tripped breaker, bad contactor/fuse | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, fuse |
| Fan or compressor hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — do not touch cap | Replace capacitor |
| Warm air, long runtimes, hissing | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search + EPA repair |
| Dirty outdoor coil, poor cooling | Blocked condenser | Rinse outer fins with hose | Deep coil cleaning |
| Water near indoor unit, no cooling | Clogged condensate/float switch | Clear drain exit | Flush drain, replace switch |
| Unit runs, no cold at all, trips out | Failed compressor | None | Compressor diagnosis/replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and model):

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 DIY.
- **Condenser coil cleaning** — $100–$250.
- **Run capacitor** — $150–$400 installed.
- **Contactor** — $150–$350 installed.
- **Low-voltage fuse / minor electrical** — $75–$200.
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch** — $100–$300.
- **Refrigerant leak repair** — $200–$1,500+ depending on leak location; a coil replacement pushes toward the top.
- **Refrigerant recharge (with leak fixed)** — $150–$600 depending on refrigerant type and amount.
- **Compressor replacement** — $1,500–$2,500+; on older units, replacing the whole system is often more cost-effective.

If your Trane is under its **10-year registered limited warranty**, covered parts may be free — you typically still pay labor.

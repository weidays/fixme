## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific Trane fault code — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner may be running, blowing air, and drawing power, but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

Because Trane systems span single-stage (XR series), two-stage (XL series) and variable-speed communicating systems (XV series), the exact behavior varies. Newer communicating systems may display a fault or comfort alert on the thermostat, while older single-stage units give no code at all — they simply run without cooling. This page covers the mechanical and electrical causes behind that symptom, from DIY-fixable basics to problems that require a licensed pro.

If you smell burning, hear loud buzzing or grinding, or the outdoor breaker trips repeatedly, shut the system off at the thermostat and breaker and call a technician before running it again.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, reducing cooling and often freezing the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat absorption so no cold air reaches the vents.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — Dirt, grass and debris insulate the coil so it can't reject heat, killing cooling capacity.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — A common failure; the fan or compressor won't start or runs weakly. Outdoor fan may hum but not spin.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Reduces cooling and often freezes the coil. Always a leak, never "used up." Requires a licensed tech.
6. **Failed contactor** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit; pitted or stuck contacts prevent the compressor from running.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — Outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
8. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — Dead batteries, wrong mode, or a loose wire prevents a cooling call.
9. **Failed compressor** — The worst case. The unit runs, fan spins, but no cooling — often preceded by hard-starting or breaker trips.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner:

- **Check the air filter.** Pull it out and hold it to the light. If it's gray and clogged, replace it. This is the #1 cause of poor cooling.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breakers.** Look in your electrical panel for tripped breakers labeled for the AC/air handler. Reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Look at the outdoor unit.** Is the fan spinning? Clear grass, leaves and debris from around it. Gently rinse the outside of the coil fins with a garden hose (unit off).
- **Check the indoor coil for ice.** If you see frost or ice on the copper lines or air handler, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw for a few hours before running again.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a safety float switch and stop cooling. Clear standing water if accessible.

Do **not** open sealed refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified Trane tech will typically:

1. Confirm the cooling call reaches the outdoor unit (24V at the contactor).
2. Test the run capacitor with a meter against its rated microfarads.
3. Inspect and test the contactor for pitting and proper pull-in.
4. Measure refrigerant pressures with gauges and check superheat/subcooling against Trane charging specs.
5. Check airflow — filter, blower speed, and evaporator coil condition.
6. Test compressor windings and amp draw if it's suspected.
7. On communicating XV/XL systems, pull fault data from the thermostat or control board.

Sanity check: a good tech measures before recommending. Be cautious of any quote to "add refrigerant" without also finding the leak — that's a temporary fix that will fail again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, dirty filter | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Check coil for resulting ice |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find airflow restriction or refrigerant leak |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker/contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant/leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Dirty, matted outdoor coil | Blocked heat rejection | Rinse fins gently | Chemical coil cleaning |
| Blower off, no cooling call | Thermostat/wiring | Check mode & batteries | Trace low-voltage fault |
| Fan spins, no cold, breaker trips | Compressor failure | Stop resetting | Test/replace compressor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $100–$350
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$300
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

Systems using R-22 refrigerant cost significantly more to recharge since it's phased out — a leaking older R-22 system is often better replaced than repaired.

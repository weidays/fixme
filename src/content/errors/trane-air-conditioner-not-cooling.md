## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code — it's the symptom you notice when your Trane air conditioner runs but the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint, blows warm or room-temperature air, or short-cycles without pulling down the temperature.

On Trane systems the outdoor condenser and the indoor evaporator coil have to work together: the compressor circulates refrigerant, the blower moves air across the cold coil, and safeties (condensate float switches, high/low pressure switches) shut things down if something is wrong. A "not cooling" complaint usually traces to one of those links failing.

Because Trane uses different control boards across the XR, XL, and XV series, the exact diagnostic LED or thermostat message varies by model. If your system shows a specific fault code on the thermostat or a blinking board LED, look that up separately — this page covers the general no-cool symptom.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, freezes the coil, and kills cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit has no power, so the indoor blower runs warm air.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "on" vs "auto"), or a miswired/failed thermostat.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or coil.
5. **Full condensate pan / tripped float switch** — a clogged drain trips the safety and shuts cooling off.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start; a very common wear part.
7. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — blocked by grass, cottonwood, or debris, so it can't reject heat.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces capacity and can freeze the coil; requires a certified tech.
9. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the compressor doesn't pull in.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the outdoor unit won't cool at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Filter:** Pull the air filter. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. Run the system 30 minutes and re-check cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Cool** and the fan to **Auto**. Lower the setpoint 5°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker labeled AC, condenser, or air handler. Flip it fully **off**, then **on**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — that's an electrical fault.
- **Vents:** Walk the house and confirm supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate:** Look for a full drain pan or standing water near the indoor unit. A clogged drain can trip a float switch and stop cooling. You can clear a visible clog and add drain tablets, but don't bypass the switch.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to **Off** and the fan to **On** for a few hours to thaw it, then replace the filter before restarting.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, jump out safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting down.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech should work through a logical sequence — this is what a fair quote reflects:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor unit.
2. **Check power and controls** — breaker, contactor pull-in, and capacitor microfarad reading against the rating on the part.
3. **Measure airflow and temperature split** across the evaporator coil (typically ~15–20°F drop when healthy).
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** to read suction/head pressures and check superheat/subcooling — this tells them if the charge is correct or if there's a leak or restriction.
5. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low — electronic detector, UV dye, or bubble test to find the source.
6. **Inspect coils and drain** — dirty condenser, iced evaporator, or a tripped float switch.
7. **Read board diagnostics** on XL/XV variable-speed systems, which log faults the basic XR does not.

If a tech quotes a compressor or refrigerant recharge without showing you gauge readings or a leak location, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blows warm air, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker / outdoor unit off | Reset breaker once; check thermostat mode | Diagnose electrical fault, contactor, or transformer |
| Weak airflow, house won't cool down | Dirty filter or blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents | Check blower, ductwork, static pressure |
| Ice on copper lines / coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw with fan on, replace filter | Measure charge, find and repair leak |
| Compressor hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Cooling stops, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate / tripped float | Clear visible clog, add tablets | Clear drain line, test float switch |
| Outdoor coil caked with debris | Dirty condenser | Gently rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean, verify pressures |
| Runs constantly, never satisfies | Low refrigerant / undersized charge | None — refrigerant is regulated | Leak search, repair, and recharge |
| No cooling at all, outdoor unit dead | Failed compressor or contactor | None — call a pro | Test compressor windings, replace part |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Trane parts run slightly higher than budget brands.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (more if the coil must be replaced)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing to a full system replacement on older units)

Diagnostic/service call fees of $80–$180 are typical and sometimes credited toward the repair.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on a Trane air conditioner — it's a symptom. Your system may be running (the indoor blower and outdoor fan spin) but the air coming from the vents is warm, lukewarm, or the house never reaches the setpoint. On systems with a control board or thermostat that logs faults, you may or may not see a stored code depending on the failure.

Cooling depends on three things all working together: airflow across the coils, a functioning compressor moving refrigerant, and a proper refrigerant charge. When any one fails, the house won't cool even if the equipment appears to be running.

On **communicating ComfortLink II systems**, the thermostat may display a system message or fault when something is wrong. On **conventional 24V systems**, there's often no display at all — you just get warm air. Behavior varies by model, so don't assume the absence of a code means the equipment is fine.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, can freeze the indoor coil, and stops cooling entirely.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — dirt, grass, and debris block heat rejection so the system can't dump heat outside.
3. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or coil.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cooling fades over days/weeks; often accompanied by hissing or an iced line.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or outdoor fan hums but won't start, or trips the breaker.
6. **Bad contactor** — the outdoor unit won't energize; you may hear no click when cooling is called.
7. **Failed compressor** — the outdoor fan runs but the compressor is dead; the most expensive failure.
8. **Thermostat or control wiring fault** — no cooling call is being sent to the outdoor unit.
9. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
10. **Condensate safety switch tripped** — a full drain pan or clogged line shuts the system down on some setups.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or the electrical panel of the equipment.

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the #1 cause of no cooling.
- **Thermostat** — set it to COOL and lower the setpoint 5°F below room temperature. Confirm it's not in FAN-only or HEAT mode. If it's battery-powered and blank or dim, replace the batteries.
- **Breakers** — check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser may have separate breakers). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor unit** — make sure the fan is spinning and the coil isn't buried in leaves, grass clippings, or cottonwood. Clear at least 2 feet of space around it. Gently rinse debris off the exterior fins with a garden hose (power off first).
- **Vents and registers** — confirm supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Ice on the lines** — if you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system OFF (fan can stay on AUTO/ON to help thaw) and let it fully melt for a few hours before running again. Persistent icing means a pro visit.
- **Condensate drain** — if there's a full drain pan or the unit shut off, a clog may have tripped a float switch. Clearing the line is DIY-friendly; a persistent shutdown is a pro job.

Do **not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work systematically — this list helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the cooling call** — confirm the thermostat is sending 24V to the outdoor contactor.
2. **Check power and electrical** — inspect the breaker, disconnect, contactor, and capacitor with a multimeter.
3. **Measure temperature split** — compare return-air and supply-air temperatures; a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F.
4. **Inspect airflow and coils** — check the filter, blower, and both coils for dirt or ice.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — measure suction and liquid pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, they should locate the leak (dye, electronic detector, or soap) rather than just adding refrigerant.
7. **Test the compressor** — verify it's drawing correct amperage and starting properly.

Be cautious of any tech who tops off refrigerant without finding the leak, or recommends a full system replacement without measuring pressures first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, filter is filthy | Clogged air filter | Replace filter, run again | — |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, let thaw fully | Find cause; leak search + charge |
| Outdoor fan runs, weak cooling | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse debris off exterior fins | Deep coil clean, verify charge |
| Cooling fades over weeks, hissing | Refrigerant leak | None — call a pro | Locate & repair leak, recharge |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call a pro | Test & replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, no click | Bad contactor or no 24V call | Check breaker once | Replace contactor, trace wiring |
| Fan spins, compressor silent/dead | Failed compressor | None — call a pro | Compressor test/replacement |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Electrical fault or shorted component | Reset once only | Diagnose short, replace part |
| Unit shut off, full drain pan | Clogged condensate line/float switch | Clear drain line | Clear clog, check pan/switch |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Repair thermostat/wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and installer):

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 DIY.
- **Condenser coil cleaning** — $75–$250 for a professional cleaning.
- **Run capacitor** — $150–$400 replaced.
- **Contactor** — $150–$350 replaced.
- **Thermostat replacement** — $150–$400 (more for communicating ComfortLink II models).
- **Condensate drain clearing** — $75–$250.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge** — $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location; a coil-level leak costs more.
- **Evaporator coil replacement** — $1,000–$2,500.
- **Compressor replacement** — $1,200–$2,200+, often approaching the cost of a new condenser. On older units, replacement of the whole outdoor unit is frequently the better value.

If your system uses older R-22 refrigerant, expect higher recharge costs and consider full replacement, since R-22 is phased out and expensive.

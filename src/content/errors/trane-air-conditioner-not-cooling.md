## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom homeowners most often report. Your Trane air conditioner may be running (the outdoor unit hums, air moves at the vents) but the air isn't cold, or the house never reaches setpoint. On variable-speed **XV-series** systems paired with a Trane communicating thermostat, an actual fault code or system message may appear. On base **XR** and many **XL** condensers there is no display at all, so diagnosis relies on airflow, temperatures and electrical readings.

Because Trane split systems share components with the indoor furnace or air handler, "not cooling" can originate indoors (blower, coil, filter) or outdoors (compressor, fan, refrigerant, capacitor). This page walks through both.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat settings or wiring** — set to "fan" instead of "cool," wrong programming, dead batteries, or a lost C-wire.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer so almost no cold air comes out.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a swollen or dead capacitor stops the compressor and/or condenser fan; the unit hums but won't cool.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Trane systems are sealed; low charge always means a leak, not "using it up."
6. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with dust, grass or cottonwood can't reject heat.
7. **Condenser fan motor failure** — outdoor fan not spinning causes the compressor to overheat and shut off on high pressure.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power, or the control board fuse is blown.
9. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck open.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive outcome; usually the last thing confirmed after ruling out the above.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, remove electrical panels on the live condenser, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

- **Filter:** Pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Restart and give it 30 minutes.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and fan is on **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and the outdoor AC breaker in your main panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Ice check:** Look at the copper lines and indoor coil. If you see frost or ice, switch the system to **Fan Only** for 2–4 hours to thaw, then replace the filter before restarting.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged condensate line can trip a float switch and shut cooling off. Check for standing water near the indoor unit.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (power off) if they look dirty — never a pressure washer.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path — this helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — filter, blower operation, coil condition, and whether the coil is frozen.
3. **Verifies power and controls** — 24V at the thermostat and contactor, board fuse, and (on XV units) reads any thermostat fault messages.
4. **Tests the capacitor** with a meter for microfarad drop and inspects the contactor for pitting.
5. **Checks the condenser fan and compressor** — is the fan spinning, is the compressor drawing correct amps and starting?
6. **Connects refrigerant gauges** — reads pressures and superheat/subcooling to determine if charge is correct, low, or overcharged, and leak-tests if low.
7. **Reports findings** before major work — you should get a diagnosis and price before compressor or refrigerant work begins.

Be cautious of any tech who "tops off refrigerant" without leak-testing, or who quotes a compressor without ruling out the capacitor, contactor and fan first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air from vents, unit running | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter, thaw on fan-only | Check airflow, coil, charge |
| Outdoor unit hums but fan won't spin | Failed capacitor or fan motor | None — power off, call pro | Test/replace capacitor or motor |
| Ice on copper lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw, replace filter | Leak test, verify charge |
| Outdoor unit completely dead | Tripped breaker / bad contactor / fuse | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor or board fuse |
| Blows cold briefly then warm | Low refrigerant / overheating compressor | None | Leak repair, recharge |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries / lost C-wire | Replace batteries | Fix wiring or replace thermostat |
| Weak airflow at all vents | Blower issue / clogged coil / duct leak | Check filter and vents | Inspect blower, clean coil |
| House never reaches setpoint on hot days | Undercharge / dirty condenser / undersized | Rinse condenser fins | Full performance check |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included unless noted:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / settings:** $0–$20 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $130–$400 installed
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$300
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$650
- **Coil cleaning (condenser or evaporator):** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often worth comparing to full system replacement on older units)

If your Trane system is 12–15+ years old and facing a compressor or coil replacement, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison — the older R-22 refrigerant is expensive and being phased out.

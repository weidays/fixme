## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific Trane fault code — your air conditioner is running (or trying to run) but the house isn't getting colder. On a standard Trane single-stage system (XR14, XR16, etc.) there's no diagnostic display, so you're troubleshooting behavior. On variable-speed communicating systems (XV18, XV20i with a ComfortLink II thermostat), the thermostat may show an actual alert or fault code that points to a specific cause.

The underlying issue falls into one of a few buckets: airflow is restricted, the refrigerant charge is off (usually a leak), an electrical component has failed, or the outdoor unit isn't running at all. This guide helps you rule out the safe, homeowner-fixable causes before you pay for a service call.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dirt blanket the outdoor coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Thermostat set wrong or losing power** — set to "heat," in "fan on" only, dead batteries, or a tripped program.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure. The compressor or fan hums, buzzes, or won't start.
5. **Dirty evaporator coil or frozen coil** — ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant line blocks airflow and cooling.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — warm air, ice on the small copper line, or hissing. Requires a certified tech.
7. **Failed contactor** — the outdoor unit is silent; the relay that powers the compressor isn't closing.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor fan doesn't spin; unit overheats and shuts down on a limit.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive failure; outdoor unit hums but won't start, or trips the breaker.
10. **Control board or communication fault (XV/communicating units only)** — the thermostat may display an error.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers** — check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker (both the indoor air handler/furnace and outdoor unit have breakers). Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Confirm return grilles aren't obstructed.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser and gently rinse the fins with a garden hose (water only, from outside in). Give it a 2-foot clearance.
- **Condensate drain** — if there's standing water in the drain pan or a full float switch, the system may shut off cooling. Clear a visible clog at the drain line opening.
- **Frozen coil** — if you see ice on the copper lines or coil, switch the thermostat to **OFF** (or fan-only) and let it thaw fully before running again.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Trane tech follows a repeatable process — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verifies the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower operation, and both coils for dirt or ice.
3. **Reads the thermostat and any codes** — on ComfortLink II / communicating XV systems, they'll pull stored alerts.
4. **Checks electrical** — tests the capacitor with a meter, inspects the contactor for pitting, verifies the fan motor and compressor draw amps within spec.
5. **Takes refrigerant readings** — connects gauges, reads suction/liquid pressures and superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct and whether there's a leak.
6. **Leak search if low** — dye, electronic sniffer, or nitrogen pressure test to find the leak before recharging.

Be wary of any tech who wants to "just add refrigerant" without looking for a leak — that's a temporary fix on a system that's losing charge.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm-ish air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower, ductwork |
| Outdoor unit dirty, poor cooling | Blocked condenser coil | Rinse fins with hose | Deep coil clean, fin comb |
| Ice on indoor coil or copper line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw, replace filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor fan spins, compressor hums/won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (fix under panel) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit totally silent | Contactor or breaker | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Outdoor fan won't spin | Failed fan motor or capacitor | None | Replace fan motor/cap |
| Warm air, hissing, oily spots | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak repair + recharge |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor or short | Stop, leave off | Amp-test compressor |
| Thermostat shows alert (XV units) | Board/comm fault | Note the code | Diagnose ComfortLink |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, including parts and labor:

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning** — $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor** — $150–$400
- **Contactor** — $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor** — $400–$700
- **Thermostat replacement** — $150–$500 (more for communicating ComfortLink II)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge** — $600–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV / metering device** — $500–$900
- **Compressor replacement** — $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing to full condenser or system replacement)
- **Control board (communicating XV units)** — $500–$900

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far pricier), and whether the part is under Trane's warranty — most Trane compressors carry a 10-year registered parts warranty, which can cut the compressor part cost dramatically (you'll still pay labor).

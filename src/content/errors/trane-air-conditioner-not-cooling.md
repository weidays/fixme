## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink or fault code on your Trane — it's the symptom you're seeing: the system is on but the house won't reach the setpoint. Trane air conditioners cool by circulating refrigerant between an indoor evaporator coil and an outdoor condenser, moving heat out of your home. When any part of that chain fails — airflow, electrical, or refrigerant — you lose cooling.

Some Trane systems, especially variable-speed **XV** models with communicating controls, will display a fault code on the indoor unit or in the Trane Home / Nexia app. Basic single-stage **XR** and **XL** units usually give you no code at all, so you diagnose by symptom. This page covers the symptom-based approach that applies across the lineup.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil. The single most common cause and a free fix.
2. **Dirty or blocked condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat when its fins are packed with grass, dust, or debris.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — a cheap part that starts the compressor and fan motor; when it dies the outdoor fan or compressor won't spin.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling and often causes ice on the lines. Requires a licensed pro.
5. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit; pitted or stuck contacts stop the condenser.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; the unit runs but blows warm or weak air.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a wiring fault.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Failed blower motor** — no indoor airflow, so no cool air reaches the vents.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the "heart" of the system, expensive to replace.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all safe for a homeowner. If any of them fixes it, you've saved a service call.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter alone can cause weak or no cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Try lowering it several degrees. If it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank, install fresh batteries.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (both the indoor air handler/furnace circuit and the outdoor condenser circuit). Reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips mean a fault.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser and gently rinse the outside of the fins with a garden hose (power off). Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch — a clogged condensate line can shut the system down. You can carefully empty a full pan and clear a visible drain line.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** to help it thaw, then call a pro. Running a frozen system can damage the compressor.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a breaker that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works the system logically, and knowing the steps helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** — checks setpoint, mode, and measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Inspects airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition; a frozen coil points to airflow or charge problems.
3. **Checks electrical** — tests the capacitor with a meter, inspects the contactor, and verifies voltage at the disconnect and control board.
4. **Reads any fault codes** — on communicating XV systems, pulls codes from the board or app.
5. **Measures refrigerant** — connects gauges to read pressures and superheat/subcooling. If charge is low, they'll search for the leak with an electronic detector or dye rather than just adding gas.
6. **Tests the compressor and motors** — confirms the compressor draws proper amperage and starts correctly.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add refrigerant" without looking for a leak — that's a temporary and non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower and ductwork |
| Indoor unit runs, outdoor unit dead | Failed capacitor or contactor | Check breaker only | Test and replace capacitor/contactor |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Find airflow issue or repair leak & recharge |
| Blows warm, unit cycles normally | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call a pro | Leak search, repair, EPA recharge |
| Outdoor unit hums but fan won't spin | Failed capacitor | Power off, don't touch | Replace capacitor |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker or blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Diagnose electrical fault |
| No air from vents | Failed blower motor | Check filter and breaker | Test/replace blower motor |
| Loud noise then no cooling | Failed compressor | None — call a pro | Compressor or system replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges. Prices vary by region, brand of part, and labor rates.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250 professionally, or DIY with coil cleaner (~$15)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Blower motor:** $450–$900 installed
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+; at this point, weigh it against replacing the system, especially on older units

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a blink-code on your Trane air conditioner — it's a symptom. Your system is either running without lowering the indoor temperature, or the outdoor unit won't start at all. Because a central AC has several stages (thermostat call, indoor blower, outdoor compressor and fan, refrigerant loop), the cause could sit anywhere along that chain.

This page applies broadly to Trane split-system condensers — **XR13, XR14, XR16, XL16i**, and variable-speed **XV18/XV20i** — paired with a Trane or matched indoor coil and air handler or furnace. Two-stage and variable-speed units add communicating control boards that can log fault codes; single-stage units (XR series) generally don't, so you diagnose them by symptom, not codes.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the most common and cheapest cause. Restricted airflow ices the indoor coil and kills cooling.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a wiring/C-wire issue so the AC never gets the call.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser is on its own breaker; the air handler another.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common outdoor failure. The compressor or fan hums but won't spin.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — warm air despite the unit running; often shows as an iced-up line or coil.
6. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil is caked with dirt/grass, so it can't reject heat.
7. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; blows the cooling entirely.
8. **Contactor failure** — the outdoor relay won't pull in, so the compressor never energizes.
9. **Compressor failure** — the worst case; the compressor is electrically or mechanically dead.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop at anything that requires opening the sealed system or electrical panels beyond the breaker.

- **Filter:** Pull and inspect it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the fan to **Auto**, and the setpoint several degrees below room temp. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model, even if the screen looks fine.
- **Breakers:** Check the indoor air-handler breaker and the outdoor condenser breaker/disconnect. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — don't keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Closed-off airflow can freeze the coil.
- **Condensate:** Check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch. A backed-up condensate line will shut the system down on many setups. Clear standing water if you can reach it safely.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outdoor coil fins with a garden hose (power off) if they look dirty.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Fan Only** for a few hours to thaw it, then run cooling again. If it re-freezes, you have a low-airflow or refrigerant problem — call a pro.

Do **not** attempt to add refrigerant, open the sealed refrigerant system, or bypass the float/limit switches.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works the chain in order — you can sanity-check their quote against this:

1. **Confirms the call** at the thermostat and checks 24V control voltage to the outdoor unit.
2. **Checks airflow** — filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil condition.
3. **Reads any fault codes** on two-stage/variable communicating boards (XV/XL series).
4. **Tests the capacitor** with a meter (microfarad reading vs. rating) and inspects the **contactor**.
5. **Measures amp draw** on the compressor and fan motor to spot electrical failure or a locked rotor.
6. **Attaches refrigerant gauges** and measures superheat/subcooling to judge charge and metering (TXV vs. fixed orifice).
7. **Leak-searches** if refrigerant is low — with electronic detector or dye — because a recharge without a repair is a temporary and EPA-noncompliant fix.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without airflow, capacitor, and leak checks first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air handler runs, warm air | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter; run fan to thaw | Check coil, airflow, charge |
| Outdoor unit silent, no start | Tripped breaker / dead thermostat | Reset breaker once; replace batteries | Test control voltage, contactor |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan/compressor won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — pro repair | Test & replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines/indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw on fan; replace filter | Find leak, correct charge |
| Weak cooling, hot outdoor unit | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean, check charge |
| Warm air, unit runs constantly | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call pro | Leak search & repair, recharge |
| Loud clicking, then nothing | Failed contactor | None — call pro | Replace contactor |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted compressor/motor | Stop resetting; call pro | Amp test, compressor diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Prices vary by region and by whether you have a single-stage or variable-speed system.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0–$15 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 replaced
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 replaced
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often near the cost of a new condenser)

If your Trane is under 10–12 years old, many parts may still be covered by the **registered limited warranty** — you typically pay labor only. Check your registration and model/serial before approving a big-ticket repair.

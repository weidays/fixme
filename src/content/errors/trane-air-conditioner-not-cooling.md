## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single Trane fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your air conditioner is running (or trying to run) but the house stays warm. Depending on your equipment, the thermostat or communicating control board (on XV18 and XV20i variable-speed systems) may display a specific alert, or you may get nothing at all beyond warm air from the vents.

Trane air conditioners share the same core failure points as any split system: airflow problems, refrigerant charge issues, electrical component failures, and heat-rejection problems at the outdoor unit. The trick is figuring out which category you're in before you spend money.

This page covers the whole "warm air" symptom. If your Trane furnace is showing a blink code instead, see the Related codes section.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — Restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can freeze the evaporator. The single most common cause of poor cooling.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or dead batteries** — Fan set to "ON" instead of "AUTO" blows room-temperature air; a dead thermostat won't call for cooling at all.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — From low airflow or low refrigerant. Ice on the indoor coil or the copper line at the outdoor unit blocks cooling entirely.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — A very common failure. The compressor and/or fan can't start, so the outdoor unit hums or does nothing.
5. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — Grass, cottonwood, and dirt block heat rejection, so the system runs but barely cools.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Reduced cooling capacity, longer run times, and sometimes coil icing. Requires leak repair, not just a "top-off."
7. **Failed contactor** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit pits or sticks, so the compressor never energizes.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — Outdoor unit dead while the indoor fan still runs.
9. **Compressor failure** — Least common but most expensive; the outdoor unit runs but produces no cooling, or trips on overload.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or bypassing a safety switch.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Then give a frozen coil several hours (fan ON, cooling OFF) to thaw before testing again.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL, set point below room temperature, and fan on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, leave it off and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (power off first). Don't bend the fins.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear the line if you're comfortable doing so.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Trane tech works from cheap-and-likely to expensive-and-rare — you should hear this sequence in their explanation:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat or communicating control, confirming the outdoor unit gets a signal.
2. **Check airflow** — filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil condition (looking for ice or dirt).
3. **Measure the run capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads.
4. **Test the contactor** for pitted contacts and proper 24V pull-in.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and take temperature readings (superheat/subcooling) to judge the charge — this is where low refrigerant and metering-device problems show up.
6. **Inspect the condenser coil and fan motor** for heat-rejection issues.
7. **Test compressor windings and amp draw** last, since replacement is the biggest expense.

If a tech quotes a compressor or refrigerant work without first checking the capacitor, airflow, and coil, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs | Filter clogged / thermostat on fan ON | Replace filter, set fan to AUTO | Verify airflow, inspect blower |
| Ice on coil or copper line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn AC off, fan ON to thaw, swap filter | Find cause, check charge & leaks |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — do not open the unit | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Runs constantly, weak cooling | Dirty condenser or low refrigerant | Rinse outdoor coil, replace filter | Measure charge, clean coil |
| Standing water, unit off | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain line | Clear drain, verify float switch |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Electrical short / compressor fault | Leave breaker off | Diagnose short or compressor |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your region, model, and warranty status change them significantly.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700 installed
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500 (often makes replacing the whole outdoor unit worth considering, especially on older systems)

Note: Many Trane compressors and parts carry a 10-year registered limited warranty on the part itself — you may still pay labor. Have your model and serial number ready to check coverage.

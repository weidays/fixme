## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code — it's the symptom you notice when your Trane air conditioner runs but the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint. On communicating **ComfortLink II** systems, the wall control may show a fault or a "cooling demand not met" condition; on standard 24V systems there's often no code at all, just warm air.

Cooling depends on three things working together: **airflow** across the indoor evaporator coil, a **refrigerant charge** that's correct and leak-free, and the **outdoor compressor and fan** running when called. A failure in any one shows up as "not cooling." Because several of the likely causes involve refrigerant or high-voltage components, this is rated **pro** — most fixes beyond airflow and thermostat should go to a licensed technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops capacity and can freeze the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. Ice blocks heat transfer, so the system runs but blows warm.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; you'll often hear the system run constantly without catching up.
4. **Failed dual run capacitor** — a common electrical failure. The outdoor fan or compressor may hum, struggle, or not start.
5. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't pull in, so the compressor never starts.
6. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood, it can't reject heat.
7. **Thermostat problem or wiring/settings** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a control fault.
8. **Stuck reversing valve (heat pumps)** — unit stays in heat mode and blows warm air in cooling.
9. **Compressor failure** — least common but most expensive; the unit won't cool at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here if a fix requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or touching high-voltage parts.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter. If it's gray or clogged, replace it. Run the fan a bit to restore airflow.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace batteries if it takes them.
- **Breaker:** Check the breakers for both the indoor air handler and the outdoor condenser. Reset one that's tripped **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (power off) if they look dirty.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines, turn the system to **OFF** and run the fan only for a few hours to thaw it, then replace the filter. Persistent freezing means call a pro.
- **Condensate:** Check that the drain line isn't clogged and the pan isn't overflowing; a full pan can trigger a float switch that shuts the system down.

**Do not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, bypass any safety or float switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap to expensive, and you can sanity-check their quote against this:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for dirt or ice.
3. **Tests electrical** — measures the run capacitor's microfarads against its rating, checks the contactor, and confirms voltage to the compressor.
4. **Gauges the refrigerant** — connects manifold gauges to read pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak, not "just needing a top-off."
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to locate the leak before recharging.
6. **Component tests** — checks the compressor windings, outdoor fan motor, and (on heat pumps) the reversing valve.
7. **On ComfortLink II systems** — reads stored fault history from the control for confirmation.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without a leak search and electrical testing first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter / blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents | Clean blower & coil |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Frozen evaporator | Turn off, run fan to thaw, new filter | Find airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — needs a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan hums/won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (high voltage) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Bad contactor or breaker | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor |
| Outdoor coil caked with debris | Dirty condenser | Rinse fins with power off | Deep coil cleaning |
| Blank/wrong thermostat | Dead batteries / settings | Replace batteries, set COOL | Repair control wiring |
| Water around indoor unit, system off | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain line | Clear line, test float switch |
| Heat pump blows warm in cool mode | Stuck reversing valve | None | Test & replace valve |
| Nothing cools, no other fault | Compressor failure | None | Replace compressor / weigh system |

## Repair costs

Broad, honest US ranges including parts and labor. Your area and model affect the price.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries / settings:** $0–$10 (DIY).
- **Run or dual capacitor:** $150–$400 replaced.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 replaced.
- **Condenser coil deep cleaning:** $150–$300.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250.
- **Outdoor fan motor:** $300–$700.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs older systems).
- **Reversing valve (heat pump):** $600–$1,300.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,200+; on older units, replacing the whole outdoor unit is often the smarter spend.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific Trane fault code — your Trane air conditioner is running (or trying to) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. Trane straight-cool ACs don't usually flash a diagnostic blink code for this the way a furnace board does; instead, you diagnose by airflow, refrigerant pressures, and electrical readings.

Cooling depends on three things working together: enough **airflow** across the coils, the correct **refrigerant charge**, and a healthy **compressor and electrical** path. When any one fails, you get warm air, weak air, ice on the lines, or an outdoor unit that hums but won't start. This page walks through all three so you can tell a fair diagnosis from an oversold one.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked return** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops cooling capacity and can freeze the indoor coil into a block of ice.
2. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat. Very common in summer.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "on" vs "auto," heat vs cool), or a miswired/failed thermostat.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs, so you get room-temperature air.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a cheap part that ages out; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — refrigerant never depletes on its own, so low charge means a leak somewhere in the coil or lineset.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low charge; ice blocks all cooling until it thaws.
8. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit pits or sticks and stops closing.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit won't cool no matter what else checks out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are homeowner-safe. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or handle refrigerant.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 cause of no cooling and frozen coils.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, fan to **AUTO**, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser may have separate breakers). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the pull-out disconnect box next to the outdoor unit is fully seated.
- **Vents and returns:** Open closed supply registers and clear furniture or rugs off return grilles.
- **Condensate:** If cooling recently quit and you see water near the indoor unit, a full condensate line may have tripped a safety float switch. You can clear a visible clog at the drain, but don't disable the float switch.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** to thaw it (can take a few hours), then run cooling again after replacing the filter. Persistent icing needs a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Trane technician should:

1. **Confirm the complaint** and check thermostat calls, filter, and airflow first.
2. **Measure the temperature split** — the difference between return and supply air (typically ~15–20°F on a healthy system). A low split points to airflow or charge problems.
3. **Inspect the coils** indoors and out for dirt and ice.
4. **Check electrical:** capacitor microfarads against the label rating, contactor operation, incoming voltage, and the outdoor fan/compressor amp draw.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and read pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge the charge — this requires EPA certification.
6. **Leak-search** if charge is low, using electronic detectors or dye, rather than just "topping off."

If a tech recommends a recharge without a leak search, or jumps straight to compressor replacement without checking the capacitor and airflow, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor unit runs, air isn't cold | Dirty filter or outdoor breaker off | Replace filter; check both breakers | Diagnose charge/electrical if cleared |
| Ice on copper lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find airflow/charge cause, leak search |
| Outdoor unit hums but fan/compressor won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — leave sealed unit alone | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace fuse/contactor, find root cause |
| Weak cooling, high humidity | Low refrigerant (leak) | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Warm air from every vent, thermostat blank | Dead thermostat batteries/power | Replace batteries | Replace/rewire thermostat |
| Water near indoor unit, system off | Clogged condensate, float switch tripped | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, service pan/switch |
| Nothing cools despite good charge & power | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test/replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Low-voltage fuse:** $75–$150
- **Condensate line clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (higher if the leak is in the evaporator coil, which may run $1,000–$2,000 to replace)
- **Outdoor fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,200+ (often the point where replacing the whole condenser makes more sense)

Variable-speed Trane systems (XV series) can cost more to diagnose due to communicating control boards.

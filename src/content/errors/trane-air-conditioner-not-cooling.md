## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific error code on most Trane air conditioners — it's a symptom. Single-stage Trane units (XR13, XR14, XL16i and similar) have no fault display, so "not cooling" simply describes what you're experiencing: the system runs but the house doesn't get cold, or the outdoor unit won't start at all.

On variable-speed models with the **Trane ComfortLink II** control (XV18, XV20i), the thermostat may show a specific alert or fault code that points to the actual problem — a communication error, a locked-out compressor, or a sensor fault. If you have a ComfortLink II thermostat, note any message it displays; it will help your technician.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, the goal here is to help you rule out the easy, free causes before you pay for a service call.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — The single most common cause. A dirty filter chokes airflow, ices the indoor coil, and kills cooling.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — Grass clippings, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection so the AC can't dump heat outside.
3. **Thermostat problem** — Dead batteries, wrong mode (Heat instead of Cool), or a fan set to ON instead of AUTO (blowing uncooled air between cycles).
4. **Tripped condensate float switch** — A clogged drain line trips the safety switch and shuts the system down to prevent water damage.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — The outdoor disconnect or panel breaker has tripped, so the condenser gets no power.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — A very common failure. The compressor or fan motor hums but won't start, or the outdoor fan doesn't spin.
7. **Bad contactor** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit fails, so nothing turns on outside.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Causes weak cooling, a frozen coil, or a compressor that short-cycles. Requires a certified tech.
9. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — The most expensive cause. Usually the last thing to blame after cheaper items are ruled out.
10. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Ice on the indoor coil from low airflow or low charge blocks cooling entirely.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all safe for a homeowner. Do them in order:

- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Replace the air filter.** If it's gray or clogged, swap it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls. Run the system 30 minutes and recheck.
- **Check the breakers.** Look at both the indoor air-handler/furnace breaker and the outdoor AC breaker in your main panel. Reset any tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check the outdoor disconnect.** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated.
- **Look at the outdoor unit.** Is the coil (the fins) caked with dirt or grass? Is the top fan spinning when the system calls for cooling? Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (power off) if it's dirty.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A full drain pan or a tripped float switch shuts the system down. If you see standing water at the indoor unit, the drain is likely clogged — this often needs a pro to clear.
- **Confirm vents are open.** Blocked or closed supply/return registers starve airflow and can freeze the coil.
- **Look for ice.** If the indoor coil or the copper lines outside are frosted, turn the system OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw for a few hours before restarting. Persistent icing means low airflow or low refrigerant — call a pro.

**Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass the float switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap to expensive, so you can sanity-check their quote:

1. **Verifies the complaint** — Confirms setpoint, airflow at registers, and measures the temperature split (return vs. supply air; a healthy system drops air roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — Inspects filter, blower, and coil for ice or restriction.
3. **Tests electrical** — Measures voltage at the contactor, tests the run capacitor with a meter (a weak capacitor is a classic culprit), and checks the contactor for pitting.
4. **Reads the thermostat/control** — On ComfortLink II systems, pulls any stored fault codes.
5. **Gauges the refrigerant** — Connects manifold gauges and measures superheat/subcooling to judge charge. Low readings point to a leak.
6. **Locates leaks** — Uses an electronic sniffer or dye if charge is low; refrigerant should never just be topped off.
7. **Tests the compressor and fan motor** — Checks windings and amp draw if the outdoor unit won't start.

Ask for the specific measured reading (capacitor microfarads, subcooling number, amp draw) behind any part they want to replace.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but not cold | Dirty filter or low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Check coil, blower, charge |
| Outdoor unit won't start | Bad capacitor/contactor or tripped breaker | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Weak cooling, warm on hot days | Dirty condenser coil or low refrigerant | Rinse outdoor coil | Measure charge, find leak |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant |
| System shuts off, water present | Clogged drain / tripped float switch | Check drain pan | Clear drain line, test switch |
| Fan runs, no cold, short cycles | Low refrigerant or failing compressor | None safe | Gauge system, test compressor |
| ComfortLink II shows a code | Control/comm or component fault | Note the message | Interpret code, repair |
| Warm air even between cycles | Fan set to ON | Set fan to AUTO | N/A |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $15–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $0 DIY hose rinse – $150 professional
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Fan motor (outdoor):** $300–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$2,000+ depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+
- **Compressor replacement:** $2,000–$4,000+ (often near the cost of a new unit)
- **ComfortLink II control board (variable-speed models):** $600–$1,200+

If your unit is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, ask your contractor to compare the repair against a full system replacement — the math often favors replacement on units over 12–15 years old.

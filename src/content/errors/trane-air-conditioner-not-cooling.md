## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Trane air conditioner runs (or tries to run) but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or barely cool. On Trane's variable-speed **XV** systems, the indoor board or the ComfortLink II thermostat may log a related fault or show a status message, but on most **XR** and **XL** single-stage systems there's no cooling-specific blink code — you're diagnosing by behavior.

The underlying causes range from trivial (a clogged filter) to serious (a refrigerant leak or dead compressor). Because several of the real causes involve refrigerant, sealed-system components, or high-voltage capacitors, this problem is rated **pro** — a few checks are safe for you, but most fixes are not.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; a coil covered in ice can't absorb heat, so vents blow warm.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — matted with grass, dust, or cottonwood, the unit can't reject heat and cooling drops off.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or condenser fan hums but won't start, or the outdoor fan spins while the compressor doesn't.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — never "used up." Warm air, hissing, or ice on the copper lines point here. Needs a pro.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
7. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, miswired, or a failed ComfortLink connection on XV systems.
8. **Contactor failure** — the relay that sends power to the compressor sticks open or its contacts burn.
9. **Compressor failure** — the worst case; the unit draws power but can't build pressure. Common on older systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no tools into the sealed system, no bypassing switches.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it takes them. Make sure the fan is on **AUTO**, not just **ON**.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (indoor air handler and outdoor unit often have separate breakers). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil fins with a garden hose (never a pressure washer) with the power **off** at the disconnect.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut cooling down. If you see standing water in the drain pan, that's a clue for your technician.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** to thaw it (can take a few hours), then call a pro — ice means low airflow or low refrigerant.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, discharge capacitors, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path, and knowing it helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Confirm power and controls** — tests the contactor, capacitor (with a meter), and low-voltage signal from the thermostat.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — reads suction and head pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to judge the charge. Low readings signal a leak.
5. **Leak search** — if the charge is low, they use electronic detectors or dye to find the leak rather than just adding refrigerant.
6. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw, windings, and start behavior if it won't run.
7. **On XV/variable-speed units** — pulls fault history from the ComfortLink II thermostat or indoor board to interpret communication or staging faults.

Be wary of any tech who wants to "just add refrigerant" without a leak search — that's a temporary fix on a system that's leaking.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, vents cool but low | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower & ductwork |
| Ice on coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, run fan | Find airflow issue or leak |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Failed capacitor or compressor | None (high voltage) | Test/replace capacitor or compressor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Tripped breaker / contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Warm air, hissing sound | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search & sealed-system repair |
| Vents blow warm, thermostat blank | Thermostat / batteries / wiring | Replace batteries | Rewire or replace thermostat |
| Standing water, unit shuts off | Clogged condensate drain | Clear visible debris | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Outdoor coil caked with dirt | Dirty condenser | Rinse fins (power off) | Deep coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10 – $40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat battery / basic thermostat:** $0 – $250 installed.
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150 – $400 installed.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $400.
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100 – $250.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150 – $400.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400 – $1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A costs more than older R-22 to source but is legal; R-22 systems are increasingly expensive to service).
- **TXV / expansion valve replacement:** $500 – $900.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800 – $2,500+. On an older system, this often prompts a full replacement conversation.
- **Full system replacement:** $6,000 – $14,000+ depending on size, SEER2 rating, and Trane series.

Prices vary by region, unit age, and refrigerant type. Get a written diagnosis before authorizing major sealed-system work.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner may be running, blowing air, and drawing power, but the air coming from your vents is warm or only slightly cool. On variable-speed **XV-series** systems, the indoor board or communicating thermostat may log a specific fault (low pressure, compressor lockout, sensor fault), while single-stage **XR** and two-stage **XL** units often give no code at all — they just underperform.

The root problem is almost always one of three things: **airflow** (the indoor side can't move heat), **refrigerant** (not enough charge to absorb heat), or **electrical** (a part that runs the compressor or fan has failed). This page helps you tell them apart before you pay for a diagnosis.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the indoor coil, causing it to ice over and cooling to collapse.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection, so the system can't dump heat outside.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or condenser fan won't start, so no cooling is produced.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharge means little heat is absorbed; often shows as ice on the copper line or weak, humid air.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse to the outdoor unit** — the indoor fan runs but the compressor is dead, so only warm air blows.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't pull in, leaving the condenser off.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — the result of causes 1, 2, or 4; must thaw fully before the system will cool again.
8. **Thermostat problem or wrong setting** — dead batteries, "fan on" vs "auto," or a miswired/failed stat.
9. **Failing compressor** — the least common but most expensive; the unit hums, trips, or runs without producing cold air.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or the outdoor electrical panel.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Cool** and **Auto** (not "Fan On"), and lower the setpoint 5°F below room temp. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breaker:** Check your panel for a tripped AC breaker (both indoor air handler/furnace and outdoor condenser). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser, keeping at least 2 feet of clearance. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose from the outside in — **do not** remove panels or the top fan grille.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** but leave the fan **On** to thaw it (can take several hours). Running a frozen system won't cool and can damage the compressor.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a safety float switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water at the indoor unit.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical airflow-to-refrigerant-to-electrical path. You can sanity-check a quote against this:

1. **Confirms the complaint** and checks thermostat call, then measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Inspects airflow** — filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Tests electrical** — measures the **capacitor** microfarads against its rating, checks the **contactor**, and verifies voltage reaching the compressor and fan motor.
4. **Gauges the refrigerant** — connects manifold gauges to read pressures and superheat/subcooling, which reveals undercharge, overcharge, or a restriction.
5. **Finds the leak** if charge is low — using electronic detector, UV dye, or a nitrogen pressure test. A legitimate shop will **find and repair the leak**, not just top off refrigerant.
6. **Evaluates the compressor** last — winding resistance, amp draw, and whether it starts and holds.

Ask any tech to show you the readings. "Low on Freon" with no leak search is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Clean blower/coil if neglected |
| Ice on copper line or indoor coil | Low refrigerant or dirty filter/coil | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find/repair leak, recharge |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, bad capacitor or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace capacitor/contactor |
| Outdoor unit hums but fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — power involved | Test and replace capacitor |
| Air is cool but humid, never satisfies | Undercharge or oversized load | Check filter/vents | Verify charge, airflow, duct sizing |
| Outdoor coil caked in dirt | Dirty condenser | Rinse fins from outside with hose | Deep coil cleaning |
| Unit trips breaker repeatedly | Shorted compressor or wiring | Stop resetting, call pro | Diagnose compressor/electrical fault |
| Standing water at indoor unit, no cooling | Clogged condensate, float switch open | Clear visible drain, check pan | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Prices vary by region and system size.

- **Air filter:** $0–$30 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$450
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 (more if the coil must be replaced)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often the point to weigh replacing the whole condenser or system, especially on older R-22 units)

If your system is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, ask for a quote to replace the outdoor unit or full system — sometimes it's the smarter spend.

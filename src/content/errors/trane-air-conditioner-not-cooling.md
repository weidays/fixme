## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code on your Trane air conditioner — it's a symptom that can come from a dozen different faults. Your Trane system may be running (fan spinning, air moving) but blowing warm or room-temperature air, or it may not turn on at all when the thermostat calls for cooling.

Trane split systems consist of an outdoor condenser (with the compressor and condenser coil) and an indoor air handler or furnace (with the evaporator coil and blower). Cooling fails when any link in that chain breaks: airflow, refrigerant, electrical control, or the compressor itself.

Communicating Trane systems (XV series with ComfortLink II or a variable-speed board) may display a specific fault or alert code on the thermostat when cooling fails. Single-stage XR and XL units generally have **no display** — you diagnose by symptom and by the LED on the indoor control board, which varies by board revision. When in doubt, note the exact symptom before calling a pro.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, causing the evaporator coil to freeze and cooling to drop off. Most common and cheapest fix.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a failed thermostat not sending the cooling call.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power, so the compressor and condenser fan never start.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil ices over and blocks cooling entirely.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan motor hums but won't start. Very common in hot climates.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; the system runs constantly but never satisfies the thermostat.
7. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit sticks or burns out.
8. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil can't reject heat, so cooling degrades on hot days.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the outdoor fan doesn't spin, causing the compressor to overheat and shut down.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the compressor won't start or has failed internally.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here — do not open the electrical panel of the unit or touch refrigerant lines.

- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" complaints.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check your breakers.** Both the indoor air handler/furnace breaker and the outdoor AC breaker (often a double-pole 240V breaker) must be ON. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Check supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed vents aren't blocking airflow throughout the house.
- **Look for ice.** If you see ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** but leave the fan on **ON** to thaw it (can take several hours). Ice means airflow or refrigerant trouble that needs a tech.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut off cooling. Clear a visible clog or add drain tablets; don't disassemble the plumbing.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will work through the system in order so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirm 24V control voltage reaches the contactor.
2. **Check line voltage and the contactor** — measuring whether the outdoor unit is getting power and whether the contactor is pulling in.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter for correct microfarad (µF) rating. A weak capacitor is a frequent, cheap find.
4. **Measure refrigerant pressures** with gauges and take temperature readings (superheat/subcooling) to determine if the charge is correct or if there's a leak.
5. **Inspect coils and airflow** — dirty condenser coil, frozen evaporator, or a failing blower motor.
6. **Test the compressor and condenser fan motor** for proper amp draw and start-up.
7. **On communicating XV units**, pull fault history from ComfortLink II or the board to confirm reported alerts.

A thorough tech should measure refrigerant and electrical values, not just guess. Be wary of anyone who "tops off" refrigerant without searching for a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with fan ON | Check airflow, refrigerant, blower |
| Outdoor unit completely dead | Tripped breaker / contactor / capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, capacitor, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan or compressor won't start | Failed capacitor | None — call a pro | Replace run/start capacitor |
| Runs constantly, never cools enough | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call a pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter; thaw with fan ON | Diagnose airflow/charge, thaw fully |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed condenser fan motor | Shut unit off to avoid overheating | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| No cooling, no thermostat response | Dead thermostat / no 24V | Replace batteries; check settings | Test control voltage, thermostat |
| Water leaking, then no cooling | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Your area and unit size affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / settings:** $0–$15 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (more if it's an evaporator or condenser coil leak)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full-system replacement on older units)

If your Trane AC is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, get a second opinion and a replacement quote before spending on a big repair.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a blink code — it's the symptom you notice when a Trane air conditioner runs but the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents isn't cold. Unlike a furnace fault, most residential Trane condensers don't flash a diagnostic LED for cooling problems (variable-speed and communicating systems like the XV20i are the exception and may report a fault at the thermostat or on the outdoor board).

So this page treats "not cooling" as a diagnostic starting point. The cause can be as simple as a clogged filter or as serious as a failed compressor or a refrigerant leak. The checks below help you rule out the cheap, safe fixes before paying for a service call.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricted airflow drops cooling capacity and can freeze the indoor coil. The #1 cause of weak cooling.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat when the fins are packed with grass, cottonwood, or dirt.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "on" vs. cool), or a miswired/failed thermostat.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a swollen or dead dual-run capacitor prevents the compressor or fan motor from starting; you may hear a hum or clicking.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes ice on the lines, long run times, and warm air. Refrigerant does not "run out" on its own — a leak exists.
6. **Failed contactor** — the 24V relay in the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the compressor never energizes.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — a downstream result of #1 or #5; blocks all airflow.
9. **Condensate safety switch tripped** — a full drain pan float switch can shut the system down to prevent water damage.
10. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive outcome; often the last thing confirmed after everything else checks out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop before opening electrical panels or the condenser cabinet.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Cool** and the fan to **Auto**, then drop the setpoint 3–4°F below room temp. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling.
- **Breaker:** Check the panel for a tripped AC breaker. Flip it fully off, then back on **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and weeds at least 2 feet around the condenser. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (never a pressure washer) with the power off.
- **Ice on the lines:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn cooling **off** and set the fan to **On** to thaw it. Running a frozen system risks the compressor.
- **Condensate drain:** If the drain pan is full or a float switch has tripped, clear the visible clog and empty the pan.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that won't stay running.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — this is what you're paying for and how to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** and checks thermostat calls and settings.
2. **Measures the temperature split** (return vs. supply air) — a healthy system usually drops air about 15–20°F.
3. **Checks airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition.
4. **Tests electrical components** — measures the run capacitor's microfarads, checks the contactor, and verifies 24V and line voltage at the condenser.
5. **Connects refrigerant gauges** and reads pressures plus superheat/subcooling. Abnormal readings point to a leak, overcharge, or restriction.
6. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low — with electronic detector or dye.
7. **Tests the compressor and fan motor** windings and amp draw if it still won't cool.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without measuring pressures and the capacitor first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air from vents is weak, house warm | Dirty air filter | Replace filter, open vents | Verify airflow, clean coil |
| Ice on copper lines / indoor coil | Low refrigerant or airflow restriction | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Find/repair leak, recharge |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Failed compressor or capacitor | Check breaker only | Test capacitor, compressor amps |
| Outdoor unit totally silent | Tripped breaker, contactor, or capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, capacitor, voltage |
| Humming, then nothing | Failed run capacitor | None — leave sealed cabinet closed | Replace capacitor |
| System shuts off, water in pan | Tripped condensate float switch | Clear drain, empty pan | Clear/flush drain line, test switch |
| Warm air, long run cycles | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse fins with hose, power off | Deep-clean coil, check charge |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Test/replace thermostat, check wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor included. Regional rates and system size affect the numbers.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher for R-410A or evaporator coil leaks)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often makes replacing an older system worth considering)
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$200, frequently credited toward the repair

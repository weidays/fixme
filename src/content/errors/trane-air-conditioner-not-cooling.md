## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a blink code — it's the most common complaint homeowners have about a Trane air conditioner. The system may be running (blower and fan spinning) yet blowing warm or room-temperature air, or the outdoor unit may not start at all.

Trane split systems and heat pumps don't always flash a numbered fault for a no-cool condition. Some communicating Trane systems with a ComfortLink II or similar thermostat will display an alert or fault text; older units with a standard 24V thermostat give you no code at all — just warm air. Because the cause can range from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, this is rated **pro**: the safe checks are quick, but the real repairs usually need a licensed HVAC technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode (Heat/Off instead of Cool), dead batteries, or a fan set to "On" so it blows air even when the compressor is off.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor condenser has its own breaker; if it trips, the indoor blower still runs.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or fan motor hums or won't start.
5. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the condenser never energizes.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer, so air stops cooling.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces or eliminates cooling. This is a repair, not a top-off — the leak must be found and sealed.
8. **Tripped condensate safety switch** — a full or clogged drain pan trips a float switch that shuts the system down to prevent water damage.
9. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked in grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat.
10. **Failed compressor** — the least common but most expensive; the unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all safe for a homeowner. Stop here and call a pro for anything electrical inside the unit or anything involving refrigerant.

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many no-cool calls.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to **Cool** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Set the fan to **Auto** rather than On.
- **Breakers** — check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and the outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser so it has room to breathe. Do not open panels.
- **Condensate drain** — if the system shut off and you see a full drain pan or standing water, the float switch may have tripped. Clear a visible clog at the drain line if you can reach it safely; otherwise call a pro.
- **Frozen coil / ice on the lines** — if you see ice on the copper lines or the indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** and run the fan only for a few hours to thaw it, then call a technician to find the cause.

Do not add refrigerant, open sealed panels, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path — this helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Confirms the complaint** and checks thermostat call for cooling at the equipment.
2. **Measures temperature split** across the indoor coil (return vs. supply air) — a healthy system drops roughly 15–20°F.
3. **Checks airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition.
4. **Tests electrical components** — measures capacitor microfarads, inspects the contactor, and checks the low-voltage fuse and control board.
5. **Verifies the compressor and fan** actually energize and draw normal amperage.
6. **Connects refrigerant gauges** (EPA-certified) to read pressures and superheat/subcooling — this reveals low charge, a leak, or a restriction.
7. **Performs a leak search** if refrigerant is low, using electronic detectors or dye.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without measuring pressures and testing the capacitor and contactor first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with fan only | Clean coil, restore airflow, find root cause |
| Fan runs, no cool air, warm at vents | Compressor not running / low charge | Check breaker & filter | Test capacitor, contactor, refrigerant pressures |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, contactor, or fuse | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, low-voltage fuse, or board |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Fan only to thaw, replace filter | Diagnose airflow vs. refrigerant leak |
| System shut off, water in pan | Tripped condensate float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear/flush drain line, test float switch |
| Air only slightly cool, gradual decline | Refrigerant leak or dirty condenser | Rinse outdoor coil gently | Leak search, seal, recharge to spec |
| No cooling at all, unit runs | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test and replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Regional rates vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / setting fix:** $0–$15 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Low-voltage (control) fuse:** $75–$200
- **Condensate float switch / drain clearing:** $100–$300
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,200+ (often worth weighing against a new system on older units)

If your Trane is still under its base limited warranty or an extended parts warranty, the compressor part may be covered — you'd typically pay labor and refrigerant only.

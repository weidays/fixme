## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink or fault code — it's the symptom homeowners report most often. Your Trane air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, compressor humming) yet delivering little or no cold air, or it may not start at all. Because this can stem from anything harmless (a dirty filter) to serious (a failed compressor), it's worth working through the checks below before assuming the worst.

Trane's communicating **ComfortLink II** systems may show an actual alert or fault message on the thermostat when cooling fails. Conventional 24V systems give you no on-screen code — you diagnose by symptom and by reading the outdoor board's LED (behavior varies by model, so check your unit's wiring-panel label).

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil into a block of ice.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — grass, dust, and cottonwood insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a miscalibrated/failing thermostat not calling for cooling.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the lines, or hissing; requires a pro to find and repair.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the condenser never energizes.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from restricted airflow or low refrigerant; blocks all airflow until thawed.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor disconnect or float switch cutting power.
9. **Clogged condensate line / full float switch** — a safety switch shuts the system off to prevent overflow.
10. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the costliest cause; the unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering it 5°F to confirm it clicks and calls.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Closed vents strangle airflow.
- **Outdoor unit:** Gently rinse away leaves, grass, and cottonwood from the coil fins with a garden hose (power off). Clear at least 2 feet of space around it.
- **Condensate:** If your system has a float switch and the drain pan is full, the unit will refuse to cool. Check for a clogged drain line and standing water.
- **Frozen coil:** See ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil? Turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** for 1–2 hours to thaw, then run cooling. If it re-freezes, you have an airflow or refrigerant problem for a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable order — this helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the call:** Confirms the thermostat is sending a 24V cooling (Y) signal and the indoor blower runs.
2. **Check the outdoor unit:** Measures voltage at the contactor, tests the run/start capacitor with a meter, and inspects the contactor for pitting.
3. **Reads pressures:** Connects gauges to the service ports to check high/low-side refrigerant pressures and superheat/subcooling — the definitive test for a refrigerant leak or restriction.
4. **Measures the temperature split:** Checks the difference between return and supply air (typically 15–22°F on a healthy system).
5. **Inspects coils and airflow:** Looks for dirty coils, a frozen evaporator, or a failing blower.
6. **Tests the compressor:** Checks amp draw, windings, and start behavior if the compressor won't run.
7. **On ComfortLink II systems:** Pulls stored fault data from the thermostat/control board.

Ask any tech to show you the pressure readings and capacitor microfarad reading before approving a refrigerant or capacitor charge.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, dirty filter | Clogged filter restricting airflow | Replace filter | None if resolved |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Compressor/fan hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (electrical) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw with fan ON, swap filter | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Weak cooling, hissing near unit | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search & repair, recharge |
| Unit off, water in drain pan | Full condensate float switch | Clear drain line | Flush line, verify float |
| Warm air, high energy bills | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil with hose | Deep coil cleaning |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test/replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and unit-size variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $20 batteries DIY, or $150–$400 installed
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$300
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Condenser coil deep cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 (higher for buried leaks or R-410A charges)
- **Blower or condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,200+ (often worth comparing to a new system on older units)

If your Trane condenser is out of warranty and the compressor has failed, get a replacement-system quote too — a repair over ~$1,500 on a 12+ year-old unit is often not worth it.

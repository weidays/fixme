## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code on a Trane air conditioner — it's a symptom you can see and feel: the system runs (or tries to run) but your home never reaches the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents isn't cold.

Trane central AC systems don't display detailed fault codes on the equipment the way furnaces or communicating (XV/ComfortLink II) systems do. On communicating XV-series systems, the thermostat may show an error or connection message. On conventional single- and two-stage units (XR, XL series), there's no display — you diagnose by observing airflow, temperature, and the outdoor unit's behavior.

Because cooling failures often involve sealed refrigerant, high-voltage capacitors, and the compressor, this is rated a **pro-level** problem. A few safe homeowner checks below can rule out the cheap causes first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer so no cold air reaches the vents.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a failed thermostat not calling for cooling.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan won't start, or hums and shuts down.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling; the sealed system needs a licensed tech to find the leak and recharge.
6. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil can't reject heat, so the system runs constantly and cools poorly.
7. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the compressor never energizes.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown disconnect fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Tripped condensate float switch** — a full or clogged drain pan cuts power to protect against water damage.
10. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive causes, usually last to be confirmed after cheaper faults are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If a check doesn't restore cooling, stop and call a pro.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If it's gray or you can't see through it, replace it. This is the #1 cause of poor cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the display is dim or blank.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (often a double-width breaker). Reset it once. If it trips again, stop — that's an electrical fault for a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure vents aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the coil.
- **Condensate drain:** Look for standing water near the indoor unit. A full drain pan can trip a safety float switch that shuts the system down.
- **Frozen coil / iced lines:** If you see ice on the refrigerant line or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF and the fan to ON. Let it thaw for a few hours, replace the filter, then try cooling again. Repeated freezing means a pro must check airflow and refrigerant.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass the float switch or any safety, or repeatedly reset a breaker that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech follows a logical sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor unit.
2. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Check electrical components** — test the capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and confirm the compressor and fan draw proper amperage.
4. **Measure refrigerant charge** with gauges, checking superheat and subcooling against Trane's spec for that model.
5. **Leak search** if the charge is low — electronic detector or dye, since simply adding refrigerant without fixing the leak is against best practice.
6. **Test the compressor** for winding resistance and grounding if it won't start after capacitor and contactor are ruled out.

A good tech won't jump straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without ruling out capacitors, airflow, and the contactor first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Check blower & duct if it recurs |
| Ice on line or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Failed capacitor or low charge | None (call pro) | Test/replace capacitor, check refrigerant |
| Outdoor unit dead | Breaker, disconnect, contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor/fuse, trace power |
| Runs constantly, cools poorly | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil gently with hose | Deep clean, verify charge |
| System shuts off, water present | Tripped float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, test switch/pump |
| Humming outdoor unit, no start | Bad capacitor or compressor | None (call pro) | Test capacitor, then compressor |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Check 24V transformer/wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Prices vary by region, model, and whether it's under warranty (Trane compressors often carry a 10-year registered parts warranty — labor is usually separate).

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$450 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700 installed
- **Compressor replacement:** $2,000–$2,800+ (often makes replacing an older unit more economical)

If your unit uses R-22 refrigerant (older systems), recharge costs are significantly higher and replacement is often the better value.

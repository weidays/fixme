## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single error code — it's a symptom. Trane air conditioners don't display a specific fault code for "no cooling" on most residential units. Instead, communicating systems (like those paired with a Trane ComfortLink II or XL824/XL1050 thermostat) may show a system status message, a red warning light on the thermostat, or a fault stored in the equipment's control board.

On non-communicating single-stage units, there's often no display at all — you just notice the house won't reach setpoint, the air from the vents is warm or weak, or the outdoor unit isn't running. This page covers the full range of causes behind a Trane AC that runs but won't cool, or won't run at all.

Because diagnosis varies by model type (single-stage vs. two-stage/variable-speed, communicating vs. non-communicating), we flag where behavior differs.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can freeze the evaporator into a block of ice, killing cooling entirely.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "heat" or "fan"), or a miswired/failed communicating thermostat.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser has its own breaker; if it's tripped, the compressor and fan won't run.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. A bad capacitor stops the compressor or condenser fan, so the unit hums or blows warm air.
5. **Low refrigerant / leak** — undercharge from a slow leak reduces or eliminates cooling and can freeze the coil. Requires a pro; refrigerant is never a DIY fix.
6. **Dirty condenser coil** — a coil caked in dirt, grass, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so cooling drops off on hot days.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the compressor. Burned or stuck contacts stop the outdoor unit.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — a downstream result of low refrigerant or restricted airflow; ice blocks air movement.
9. **Failed compressor or condenser fan motor** — the most expensive causes, usually last on the list.
10. **Control board or communication fault** — on communicating XV/ComfortLink systems, a lost link between indoor and outdoor units can halt cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner:

- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Replace the air filter.** A clogged filter is the #1 cause. Swap in a clean one and check whether airflow improves. If the coil is already frozen, turn the system to FAN-only for a few hours to thaw it before running cooling again.
- **Check the breakers.** Look for a tripped breaker for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop — that signals an electrical fault for a pro.
- **Look at the vents.** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A clogged drain can trip a float switch that shuts down cooling. Clear standing water from the drain pan and pour a drain tablet or a cup of vinegar down the line if accessible.
- **Look at the outdoor unit.** Gently rinse visible debris off the condenser fins with a garden hose (power off). Don't bend the fins or use a pressure washer.

Do **not** open the electrical panel to test the capacitor, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. Those require training and tools.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will typically:

1. **Confirm the symptom** — measure supply and return air temperature split (a healthy AC drops air ~16–22°F across the coil).
2. **Check thermostat call and low-voltage** — verify 24V is reaching the outdoor contactor.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter for microfarad (µF) value against the rating printed on it.
4. **Inspect the contactor** for pitted or burned contacts.
5. **Read refrigerant pressures** with gauges and check superheat/subcooling to determine charge and look for a leak.
6. **Check the compressor and fan motor** windings and amp draw.
7. **On communicating systems,** pull stored faults from the ComfortLink II or XL thermostat and verify indoor-outdoor communication.

A trustworthy quote will name the failed part and the measured value (e.g., "capacitor reads 22µF, rated 45µF"). Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to refrigerant or compressor replacement without showing pressure readings or a leak location.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air from vents, unit running | Low refrigerant or bad capacitor | Check/replace filter | Test capacitor, check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker or failed contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Ice on refrigerant lines or coil | Frozen coil from airflow/charge | Run FAN to thaw, replace filter | Correct charge, clean coil |
| Weak airflow, house won't cool | Clogged filter or dirty coil | Replace filter, rinse condenser | Deep coil clean, check blower |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed start/run capacitor | None — power off | Replace capacitor |
| Water around indoor unit, AC shuts off | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, add tablet | Clear line, check float switch |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries or lost power | Replace batteries | Check transformer, wiring |
| Communicating system shows fault | Lost indoor-outdoor comms | Note the message | Diagnose board/communication link |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and unit):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 DIY
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 DIY
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $0 DIY – $250 professional deep clean
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher for R-22 systems)
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **TXV / metering device:** $500–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ installed (often not worth it on aging units)
- **Control board (communicating):** $400–$900

If your unit is 12+ years old, uses R-22, and needs a compressor or major leak repair, weigh replacement against repair.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific Trane fault code. Your Trane air conditioner may be running — the indoor blower moves air and the outdoor unit hums — but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or barely cool. On systems with a communicating thermostat (ComfortLink II) or a diagnostic board, you may see a related fault or a flashing LED at the indoor control; on older single-stage systems there is often no code at all, just a comfort complaint.

Cooling failures fall into three buckets: **airflow problems** (filter, coil, blower), **electrical/control problems** (capacitor, contactor, thermostat, breaker), and **refrigerant problems** (leaks, restrictions). This page helps you sort the safe, free fixes from the ones that need a licensed pro.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, freezes the indoor coil, and chokes cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse on the outdoor unit** — the condenser sits idle while the indoor blower still runs, so you get warm air.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood, it can't reject heat and cooling drops off, especially on hot afternoons.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan motor won't start; you may hear humming or a fan that won't spin.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the copper lines, or long run times. Always a leak, never "used up."
6. **Faulty contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit fails or sticks, so the condenser never energizes.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/communication fault (more likely on ComfortLink II systems).
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from restricted airflow or low refrigerant; a solid block of ice on the indoor coil stops cooling entirely.
9. **Failing compressor** — the worst case; the unit runs but produces little or no cooling, often after years of service.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe and may save you a service call:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. If it's a battery model, swap in fresh batteries.
- **Breakers** — check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker to both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers** — walk the house and confirm supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser (at least 2 feet of clearance). Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose from outside in, power off.
- **Condensate** — a full drain pan or clogged condensate line can trip a float switch and shut cooling down. Check for standing water near the indoor unit.
- **Ice** — if you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully for a few hours before restarting. Persistent icing means you need a technician.

Do **not** open sealed refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Trane tech should:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply and return air temperatures. A healthy split should show roughly a 16-22°F drop across the coil.
2. **Check airflow** — inspect the filter, blower, and evaporator coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Test electrical** — measure the run capacitor's microfarads, check the contactor, and confirm the outdoor unit energizes on a cooling call.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures** — connect gauges to measure suction and liquid pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to determine charge.
5. **Leak-search if charge is low** — using electronic detectors or dye; adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a temporary and improper fix.
6. **Check the control/thermostat** — on ComfortLink II systems, pull fault history and confirm communication between the thermostat, air handler, and outdoor unit.

Ask to see the temperature-split reading and the capacitor test result. A quote to "add a pound of Freon" with no leak search is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, dirty filter | Clogged filter, frozen coil | Replace filter, thaw coil | Verify airflow, check charge |
| Indoor blows, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker/contactor/capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test capacitor, contactor, wiring |
| Warm air on hot days only | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil, clear debris | Deep coil cleaning, pressure check |
| Ice on copper lines/coil | Low refrigerant or airflow restriction | Thaw, replace filter | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Long run times, never cools | Undercharge or failing compressor | None | Diagnose charge/compressor |
| Humming outdoor unit, fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None | Replace capacitor |
| No cool + standing water inside | Clogged condensate/float switch | Clear visible drain | Clear line, test float switch |
| Nothing runs at all | Thermostat/control fault | New batteries, check mode | Test thermostat & board comms |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Breaker reset / debris clearing** — $0 DIY
- **Run capacitor** — $150–$350
- **Contactor** — $150–$300
- **Thermostat replacement** — $150–$400 (more for communicating ComfortLink II stats)
- **Condenser coil cleaning** — $150–$300
- **Condensate line clearing** — $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge** — $600–$1,200+ depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement** — $1,000–$2,000+
- **Compressor replacement** — $1,500–$1,800+ (often worth weighing against a new system on older units)

Prices vary by region, unit size, refrigerant type (R-410A vs older R-22 systems are much pricier), and whether parts are under Trane's limited warranty.

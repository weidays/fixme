## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a blink code — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, air moving) but the air coming from the vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On newer Trane communicating systems (XV18, XV20 with a ComfortLink II thermostat), you may also see an on-screen fault or alert rather than a blinking board light.

Because "not cooling" can stem from anything from a dirty filter to a dead compressor, this page walks you from the cheapest, safest checks up through the diagnostics a technician performs — so you can tell a fair quote from an inflated one.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, cutting capacity and eventually freezing the coil solid.
2. **Thermostat setting or wiring issue** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," wrong mode, dead batteries, or a loose wire.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dust blanket the coil so it can't reject heat.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan hums but won't start.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling, causes coil icing, and never happens without a leak somewhere.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — result of low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks all cooling until thawed.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit pits or sticks and won't close.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor fan doesn't spin, compressor overheats and trips.
9. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor disconnect fuse or panel breaker cut power to the condenser.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the unit runs but produces no cooling. Often end-of-life.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto** (not On). Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers** — check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — that's a fault, not a fluke.
- **Vents and registers** — make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit airflow** — visually check the condenser for leaves, grass, or debris packed against the fins. You can gently rinse the outside with a garden hose (power off at the disconnect first).
- **Condensate drain / pan** — a full pan or clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut down cooling. If you see standing water, note it for the tech.
- **Frozen unit** — if you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** but leave the fan **On** to thaw it (can take a few hours), then run again with a clean filter.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, remove the electrical panel on the condenser, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Trane tech will work in a logical order, and you can sanity-check their quote against these steps:

1. **Confirm the complaint** — measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and evaporator coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Verify call for cooling** — confirm 24V signal reaching the contactor; check thermostat wiring.
4. **Test electrical components** — measure the capacitor's microfarads against its rating, inspect the contactor, and confirm the fan motor and compressor draw proper amps.
5. **Take refrigerant readings** — attach gauges, check suction/liquid pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak, not a "recharge."
6. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, use an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before adding refrigerant.
7. **On communicating systems** — read fault history from the ComfortLink II thermostat or connect Trane's service tool to the board.

Be cautious of any tech who tops off refrigerant without searching for a leak, or condemns the compressor without electrical readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but isn't cold | Dirty filter / low refrigerant | Replace filter; check thermostat mode | Measure temp split, check charge, find leak |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set to Off, fan On to thaw; new filter | Diagnose airflow vs. charge, fix leak |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor or fan motor | Shut off to prevent damage | Test/replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker / blown fuse / contactor | Reset breaker once | Test disconnect fuses, contactor, wiring |
| House never reaches setpoint | Dirty condenser coil or undersized charge | Rinse condenser, clear debris | Clean coil, verify charge & airflow |
| Runs constantly, no cooling | Failed compressor | None — call a pro | Electrical test, compressor diagnosis |
| System short-cycles | Float switch tripped / control fault | Check for full condensate pan | Clear drain, test float switch & board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Prices vary by region and by whether you have a single-stage or communicating variable-speed Trane:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 (more for communicating ComfortLink models)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$650
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type — older R-410A vs. newer systems)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800–$3,500+ — at this point, weigh it against full-system replacement, especially on older units.

If your Trane is under its base limited warranty (typically 10 years on registered parts), covered components may drop your cost to labor only — ask the tech to check the serial number.

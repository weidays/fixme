## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom homeowners search for when a Trane air conditioner is running (or trying to run) but the house won't get cool. On most Trane split systems (XR, XL, and variable-speed XV series), there's no single blink code for this; instead the unit behaves in one of a few telltale ways: the outdoor unit hums but doesn't start, air blows from the vents but it's warm, the indoor coil ices over, or the system short-cycles.

Variable-speed **XV communicating systems** are the exception — they may display a fault message on the thermostat or store a code on the control board (for example, low-pressure or high-pressure lockouts). If your thermostat shows an actual code, look that up specifically. This page covers the general "runs but won't cool" problem across Trane central ACs.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" not "cool"), or a setpoint above room temperature.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure. The compressor or condenser fan hums but won't spin up.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak or no cooling and often a frozen coil. Always a leak, never normal loss.
5. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
6. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with dirt/grass can't reject heat, so the house never cools.
7. **Failed contactor** — the outdoor relay that pulls in the compressor is pitted or stuck, so nothing starts.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Failed compressor or condenser fan motor** — the most expensive failures, usually last on the list.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening a sealed system or touching high-voltage components.

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to **COOL**, the fan is on **AUTO**, and the setpoint is at least a few degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker** — check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers in your panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Condensate drain / safety switch** — if your unit has a float switch and the drain pan is full, the system may shut off cooling. Clear obvious standing water and clogs at the drain line.
- **Ice check** — if you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper lines, turn the system to **OFF** but leave the **FAN ON** for 1-2 hours to thaw, then run cooling again. If it re-freezes, you likely have low refrigerant or an airflow problem — call a tech.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, remove electrical panels on the outdoor unit, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps stopping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap to expensive — you can sanity-check their quote against this order:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature; a healthy split should show roughly a 15-20°F drop across the coil.
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for dirt or ice.
3. **Tests electrical components** — reads the **capacitor** with a meter (microfarads out of spec = replace) and checks the **contactor** for pitting or a weak coil.
4. **Checks power and controls** — confirms 24V control signal and 240V at the outdoor unit; checks the low-voltage fuse on the board.
5. **Reads refrigerant pressures** — gauges on the system reveal low charge (leak) or high pressure (dirty condenser, overcharge). On XV communicating units, they'll also pull stored fault codes.
6. **Leak search if low** — dye or electronic detector to locate the leak before any recharge.
7. **Compressor/fan test** — amperage and winding checks if the compressor won't start after capacitor and contactor are ruled out.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without checking the capacitor, contactor, and airflow first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/no cool air, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Clean coil, check blower |
| Warm air, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries, check breaker | Trace 24V control fault |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None (high voltage) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Ice on coil or copper lines | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Thaw with fan on, replace filter | Leak search, repair & recharge |
| House warm, outdoor coil filthy | Dirty condenser | Gently rinse coil (power off) | Deep coil clean, pressure check |
| Nothing runs outside | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check fuse |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low charge or failing compressor | Confirm filter/airflow first | Pressure test, compressor test |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Regional and after-hours rates vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** ~$5 (DIY)
- **Service/diagnostic call:** $75–$200
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Low-voltage fuse:** $75–$150
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (depends on leak location; coil leaks cost more)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often makes replacing an older unit worth considering)

Refrigerant handling is federally regulated and must be done by an EPA-certified technician — never a DIY job.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific Trane fault code — it's the symptom you're experiencing when the air conditioner runs but the house won't reach the setpoint, or the air from the vents isn't cold. Depending on your system, the thermostat or communicating control may show a generic alert, no error at all, or a board fault (see **Related codes** below for furnace-side blink codes on your air handler).

Because "not cooling" can stem from anything from a $20 filter to a failed compressor, the goal of this page is to help you rule out the cheap, safe causes yourself and understand what a technician should check before you approve a big repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode (fan "on" vs "auto"), dead batteries, or a bad setpoint.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the condenser (outdoor unit) may be off while the indoor blower runs, giving warm air.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — leaves, grass, and dirt block heat rejection, killing cooling capacity.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or no airflow.
6. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a common failure; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces or eliminates cooling. Requires EPA-certified repair, not a top-off.
8. **Contactor failure** — the outdoor unit won't energize.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor or blower motor** — poor or no airflow across coils.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit may trip breakers or hum and shut down.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or an electrical panel beyond a breaker.

- **Filter:** Pull and inspect it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. Then let the system run to see if cooling returns.
- **Thermostat:** Set mode to **Cool** and fan to **Auto**. Lower the setpoint 3–5°F below room temp. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from the condenser and gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (power off first). Keep 2 feet of clearance around it.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to **Off** and set the fan to **On** for 1–2 hours to thaw it, then run cooling again. If it re-freezes, you need a technician.
- **Condensate:** Check that the drain line isn't clogged and the drain pan isn't full — a tripped float switch can shut down cooling.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Trane tech should work through a logical sequence, not jump straight to "you need refrigerant":

1. **Verify airflow** — check filter, blower operation, and coil condition first.
2. **Measure electrical** — test the capacitor with a meter, check the contactor, and confirm voltage at the condenser.
3. **Check the control board / communicating fault history** — variable-speed XV systems log faults the tech can read.
4. **Take refrigerant measurements** — attach gauges and check superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct and whether there's a leak.
5. **Leak search** — if the charge is low, they should find the leak (electronic detector or dye), not just top it off.
6. **Test motors and compressor** — amperage draw and ohms readings on the compressor windings.

Ask to see the readings. A quote for a compressor or refrigerant work should come with measured evidence, not a guess.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs | Outdoor unit off (breaker/contactor) | Reset breaker once; check outdoor unit runs | Replace contactor, trace electrical fault |
| Weak airflow, warm rooms | Clogged filter or frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with fan on | Clean coil, correct airflow, check charge |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw coil, replace filter | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Cooling slowly weakened over months | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search and repair, recharge |
| No cold air, high electric bills | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse outdoor coil | Deep chemical coil cleaning |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor or short | Stop resetting; shut off | Compressor/electrical diagnosis |
| Water near indoor unit, unit off | Clogged condensate/float switch | Clear drain, use tablets | Clear line, replace float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor); your region and system size will shift these:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries or replacement:** $0–$300
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan or blower motor:** $400–$900
- **Coil cleaning (chemical):** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (depends on leak location; R-410A vs R-454B affects cost)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often near the cost of a new condenser — get a replacement-vs-repair quote)

If your system is over ~12–15 years old and facing a compressor or coil, ask for a replacement estimate to compare.

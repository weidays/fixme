## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code on a Trane air conditioner — it's a symptom. Your Trane outdoor unit and indoor air handler or furnace work together to remove heat from your home. When the system runs but the air coming from your vents is warm, room temperature, or only slightly cool, something in that chain has failed.

Unlike a furnace, most Trane AC condensers don't have a simple LED blink readout you can see. Some communicating systems (XV series with a ComfortLink II thermostat) will show a fault or error message on the thermostat screen, but single-stage XR and XL units usually give no code at all — you just feel warm air. Because diagnosis often requires refrigerant gauges and electrical testing, this is rated **pro**.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — chokes airflow across the indoor coil, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil solid.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — ice blocks airflow; caused by low airflow or low refrigerant. You'll feel weak, warm air.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system can't absorb heat. Always a leak, never "used up."
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a cheap part that stops the compressor or fan motor from starting; often the outdoor fan won't spin.
5. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the compressor is pitted or stuck open.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
7. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — heat can't escape, so cooling drops on hot days.
8. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/settings fault.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit hums but won't cool, or trips the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the target temperature is below room temperature. Set the fan to AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause and a frequent cause of frozen coils.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips mean an electrical fault.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the thermostat to OFF (or fan-only) and let it thaw for several hours before running cooling again.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water near the indoor unit; you can clear a visible clog and pour drain tablets down the line.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical order. Their quote should reflect it:

1. **Confirm the complaint** — check thermostat call for cooling and that the outdoor unit is energized.
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower operation, and the evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Measure electrical** — test the capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and verify voltage to the compressor and fan.
4. **Take refrigerant readings** — connect gauges to check susuperheat and subcooling against Trane's charging chart. Low readings point to a leak.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, they use an electronic detector or dye to find the leak before recharging.
6. **Compressor test** — check windings and amp draw if the compressor won't start or trips the breaker.

Be wary of any tech who "just adds a couple pounds of Freon" without finding the leak — that refrigerant will leak out again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, dirty filter | Clogged filter restricting airflow | Replace filter, run fan to thaw coil | Verify airflow, clean coil if needed |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Shut off, thaw, replace filter | Test charge, find leak, correct airflow |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor | None — leave power off | Test and replace capacitor |
| Unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor or contactor | None | Replace capacitor/contactor, test compressor |
| No power to outdoor unit | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Diagnose electrical fault, replace parts |
| Cools weakly on hot days | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Rinse debris off outdoor coil (power off) | Deep-clean coil, check refrigerant |
| Water near indoor unit, no cooling | Clogged condensate drain tripped float | Clear visible clog, add tablets | Clear drain line, test float switch |
| Runs constantly, no cooling | Low refrigerant leak or failed compressor | None | Leak search, recharge, or compressor test |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0–$50 DIY; $100–$200 professional.
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$2,000+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (older R-22 systems cost far more).
- **Refrigerant recharge alone (small leak):** $250–$800 — but this only lasts until the leak returns.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+; often makes replacing an aging unit more economical.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180, often credited toward the repair.

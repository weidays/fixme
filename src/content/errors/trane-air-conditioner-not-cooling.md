## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific Trane fault code — it describes an air conditioner that runs (or tries to run) but fails to bring your home down to the thermostat setpoint. On Trane systems with communicating controls (ComfortLink II) or a diagnostic thermostat, you may see a specific alert or fault instead. On older single-stage XR-series units, there's often no code at all — just warm air, weak airflow, or an outdoor unit that won't start.

Because cooling depends on the whole system — airflow, refrigerant charge, the compressor, and electrical controls — "not cooling" can range from a $15 filter you can swap yourself to a failed compressor costing thousands. This page helps you triage what's safe to check and what needs a licensed HVAC technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The single most common and cheapest cause.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer, so the unit runs but blows warm or weak air.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, dust, and debris insulate the coil so it can't reject heat. Very common late summer.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; requires EPA-certified leak repair and recharge, never a DIY top-off.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor won't start; a frequent, moderately priced failure.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the compressor never energizes.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/communication fault (especially on communicating ComfortLink systems).
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor disconnect or breaker has tripped.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit hums, trips, or does nothing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform. If none resolve it, call a pro.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Set the fan to AUTO. If the display is blank or dim, replace the batteries.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling and freeze the coil.
- **Breaker / disconnect:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Restricted airflow mimics a failing system.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance. Do **not** open the unit or spray water into electrical components.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper line at the outdoor unit, turn the system to OFF (fan ON is fine) and let it thaw fully — this can take a few hours — then replace the filter before restarting.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and stop cooling. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear the drain line if you're comfortable doing so.

Do not add refrigerant, open the sealed refrigerant system, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Trane technician follows a logical sequence — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint and airflow** — check filter, blower operation, and static pressure; confirm the coil isn't frozen.
2. **Check thermostat call and control voltage** — confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor unit; on communicating systems, they'll read ComfortLink fault data.
3. **Inspect electrical components** — test the capacitor (microfarad reading), inspect the contactor, and check the disconnect and breaker.
4. **Measure refrigerant charge** — connect gauges, read pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low readings prompt a leak search (electronic detector, dye, or bubble test).
5. **Check the compressor** — test windings, amp draw, and start components to confirm whether it's functional.
6. **Inspect coils** — evaluate condenser and evaporator cleanliness and airflow across both.

Expect a diagnostic fee of roughly $80–$180, often credited toward the repair. A trustworthy quote names the specific failed part and shows readings — not just "needs refrigerant."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check blower, static pressure |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find airflow restriction or leak |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, capacitor |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None | Test and replace capacitor |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant (leak) | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor coil caked in debris | Dirty condenser | Clear debris, gentle rinse | Deep coil cleaning |
| Blank thermostat | Dead batteries or power | Replace batteries | Diagnose wiring/24V fault |
| Standing water, no cooling | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain line | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Unit hums then trips breaker | Failing compressor | None | Compressor test/replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included where noted. Prices vary by region and unit size.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Thermostat batteries:** under $10 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$450 (more for communicating ComfortLink models)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A pricing varies)
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,500+ — often worth comparing against full-system replacement on older units

If your Trane unit uses R-22 (typically pre-2010), refrigerant is scarce and expensive; a leak may make replacement more economical than repair.

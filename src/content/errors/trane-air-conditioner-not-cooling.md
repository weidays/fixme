## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single blink code — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner may be powered on, the thermostat may be calling for cool, and air may even be moving through the vents, but the temperature isn't dropping.

On non-communicating Trane systems (most XR-series units), there's usually **no fault display at all** — you diagnose by observing which components run. On communicating **ComfortLink II** systems (many XL and XV units with the color thermostat), the thermostat itself may show a specific fault or alert code that points to the outdoor unit, indoor blower, or a communication loss. Always check the thermostat screen first on those models.

Because "not cooling" can be anything from a $15 filter to a dead compressor, the goal below is to help you rule out the easy stuff safely, then understand what a fair technician diagnosis and quote should look like.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — restricted airflow is the #1 cause. It can freeze the indoor coil into a block of ice, which then blows warm.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or dead batteries** — set to "on" instead of "auto," wrong mode, or a stuck screen on battery-powered stats.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power, so only the indoor fan runs.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor and/or condenser fan can't start; you may hear a hum or clicking outside.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer.
6. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood, the unit can't reject heat.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — poor cooling, longer run times, sometimes ice on the copper line.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit welds shut or won't pull in.
9. **Bad condenser fan motor** — compressor runs but overheats and shuts down without airflow across the coil.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; unit hums, trips the breaker, or does nothing outside.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Stop at anything involving refrigerant, wiring, or opening the sealed system.

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a huge share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm mode is **Cool**, fan is **Auto**, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breaker:** Check the panel for a tripped AC breaker (both indoor air handler and outdoor unit often have their own). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a float switch and cut cooling. If you see standing water, clear the visible drain line and add drain tablets. Don't disable the float switch.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper line outside, **turn the system to "Off" and set the fan to "On"** for a few hours to thaw, then run cooling again with a clean filter. Persistent icing means low airflow or low refrigerant — call a tech.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Trane tech should work in this order, and you can sanity-check the quote against it:

1. **Confirm the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check the call and power** — verifies 24V is reaching the outdoor contactor and that line voltage is present.
3. **Reads any ComfortLink codes** — on communicating systems, pulls fault history from the thermostat/board.
4. **Tests the capacitor and contactor** — measures capacitor microfarads against the rating on the label; inspects contactor points.
5. **Inspects the coils and fan motor** — looks for dirty condenser, weak fan motor, or a frozen evaporator.
6. **Checks refrigerant charge** — connects gauges, reads pressures and superheat/subcooling. Low charge triggers a **leak search**, not just a "top-off."
7. **Evaluates the compressor** — checks amp draw and windings if all else is ruled out.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without showing you a measured reading.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor fan runs, warm air, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or dead capacitor/contactor | Reset breaker once; replace filter | Test/replace capacitor or contactor, restore power |
| Ice on indoor coil or copper line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw, clean filter | Find airflow restriction or refrigerant leak |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None — call a pro | Replace capacitor, verify amp draw |
| Weak cooling, long run times | Low refrigerant / dirty coils | Clean debris from outdoor unit exterior | Pressure test, leak search, recharge |
| Compressor runs, fan outside not spinning | Bad condenser fan motor | Shut unit off to prevent damage | Replace fan motor and capacitor |
| Nothing runs at all | No power, blown 24V fuse, dead thermostat | Check breaker and thermostat batteries | Trace low-voltage fault, replace fuse |
| Thermostat shows ComfortLink alert | Communication or component fault | Note the code, don't reset repeatedly | Read fault, test board and wiring |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area and unit age will vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / settings:** $0–$15 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200 (or DIY with tablets)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full replacement on older units)

If your Trane is over ~12–15 years old and facing a compressor or coil, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison before committing.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific Trane fault code — it is a symptom. Your Trane air
conditioner may run continuously, cycle normally, or barely turn on, yet the air coming
from your vents is warm, lukewarm, or simply not cold enough to satisfy the thermostat.

On communicating **ComfortLink II** systems, a genuine hardware fault often *does* produce
a specific code on the thermostat or indoor board. If you have an actual numbered code
displayed, look that up instead — this page covers the broader "it just won't cool"
complaint on both communicating and non-communicating Trane systems.

Cooling depends on three things working together: enough **airflow** across the coils, a
sealed **refrigerant charge**, and functioning **electrical components** (compressor,
capacitor, contactor, controls). A failure in any one of them shows up as poor cooling.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks cooling entirely.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — undercharged systems cool weakly, then ice up or trip on high head/low pressure.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the fan or compressor hums but won't start, or starts and stops.
6. **Bad contactor** — pitted or stuck contacts stop the outdoor unit from energizing.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, bad wiring, or a mis-configured communicating stat.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** on the control board.
9. **Failed compressor** — the unit runs but produces no meaningful cooling (least common, most expensive).

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Stop if anything requires opening the
sealed refrigerant system, bypassing a safety switch, or handling electrical parts inside
the unit.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery-powered model. Make sure the fan is on AUTO, not ON (constant ON can make air feel warm at the vents).
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 cause of weak cooling and coil freeze-ups.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and the outdoor (disconnect/breaker) circuits. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping means a fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture or rugs. Closing too many rooms starves airflow.
- **Outdoor unit:** Visually check that it's not buried in leaves, grass clippings, or cottonwood. Gently rinse loose debris off the *outside* fins with a garden hose (never a pressure washer) with the power OFF at the disconnect.
- **Condensate drain / ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw for a few hours, then replace the filter. Check that the condensate drain isn't clogged and tripping a float switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Trane tech should work methodically — you can sanity-check a quote against this:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat mode, call for cooling, and low-voltage signal at the equipment.
2. **Measure temperature split** (return vs. supply air) — a healthy system is typically 15–20°F difference. A low split points to airflow or charge issues.
3. **Inspect airflow:** filter, blower, evaporator coil for dirt or ice.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures** with gauges and check superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct or if there's a leak. This requires EPA certification.
5. **Check electricals:** capacitor microfarad reading, contactor condition, compressor amp draw, and voltage.
6. **On communicating systems,** pull stored fault codes from the ComfortLink II control and check board/sensor status.
7. **Leak search** if the charge is low — with electronic detector, bubbles, or dye. Adding refrigerant without finding the leak is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with fan ON | Diagnose airflow / charge, clear ice cause |
| Outdoor unit won't start, humming | Failed capacitor | None (electrical) | Test & replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Contactor / breaker / transformer | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, low-voltage circuit |
| Weak cooling, ice on lines | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Runs constantly, house won't cool | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse loose debris (power off) | Deep coil clean, verify charge |
| Nothing responds at thermostat | Dead batteries / config | Replace batteries, set to COOL | Check stat wiring & board |
| Runs but zero cooling | Failed compressor | None | Test compressor, quote replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges. Actual prices vary by region, unit age, and warranty status.
Trane parts are often under warranty (typically 10 years registered) even after labor
warranty expires — always ask.

- **Air filter (DIY):** $10–$30
- **Thermostat batteries (DIY):** $5–$15
- **Diagnostic / service call:** $80–$150 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$300 installed
- **Low-voltage transformer / board fuse:** $150–$400
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,500+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (part often covered under warranty; labor is not)
- **Full system replacement (if aging R-22 unit):** $6,000–$14,000+

If your unit uses **R-22 refrigerant** (pre-2010 units), a major refrigerant repair can cost
more than the unit is worth — ask about replacement instead of chasing an expensive leak.

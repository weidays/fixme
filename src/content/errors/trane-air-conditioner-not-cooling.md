## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code — it's the symptom most Trane air conditioner owners describe when the system runs but the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents isn't cold. Depending on your model, the indoor control board may or may not flash a diagnostic code.

On many single-stage Trane systems (XR13, XR14, XR16) there's no fault display at all for a low-charge or airflow problem — the unit simply runs and underperforms. On two-stage and variable-speed systems (XL/XV series with communicating ComfortLink II controls), you may see a thermostat message or a stored fault about compressor lockout, low pressure, or a communication error.

Because "not cooling" can be anything from a dirty filter to a dead compressor, the goal here is to help you rule out the cheap, safe causes before you pay for a service call.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can ice the indoor coil until no cold air comes out.
2. **Dirty or blocked condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — dense grass, cottonwood, or dirt on the fins traps heat and kills cooling.
3. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — set to "fan" instead of "cool," wrong setpoint, or dead batteries on a battery-powered stat.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or outdoor fan hums or won't start.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, a frozen coil, or the outdoor unit short-cycling. Requires a certified tech.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; you'll see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit dead, indoor blower may still run.
8. **Failed contactor or capacitor at the outdoor unit** — the fan and compressor never energize.
9. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive cause; the unit runs but produces no cooling, or trips on overload.
10. **Control board or communication fault (XL/XV)** — variable-speed systems can stop cooling if the board loses communication.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or opening sealed components:

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breaker:** Check the panel for a tripped AC or air-handler breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — don't keep resetting.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply vents are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the fins with a garden hose (power off) if they look dirty.
- **Condensate:** Check the drain pan and line. A clogged drain can trip a safety float switch and shut down cooling on some setups.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the coil or refrigerant lines, turn the system to **Fan only** (or Off) and let it thaw fully — several hours — before running again. Persistent icing means low airflow or low charge and needs a tech.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, remove electrical panels on the outdoor unit, or attempt to add refrigerant.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verifies the complaint** — confirms setpoint, measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower operation, and coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Measures electrical** — tests the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, and confirms the compressor and fan are getting voltage.
4. **Gauges the refrigerant** — connects manifold gauges to read pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct.
5. **Locates leaks** — if charge is low, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak rather than just topping off.
6. **Reads stored faults (XL/XV)** — pulls codes from the ComfortLink II control on communicating systems.
7. **Compressor test** — checks windings and amp draw if all else looks good.

Be cautious of any tech who adds refrigerant without checking for a leak, or recommends a full system replacement before diagnosing the actual failure.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but isn't cold | Dirty filter or low charge | Replace filter; check for ice | Gauge refrigerant, find leak |
| Ice on coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set to Fan, let thaw fully | Diagnose airflow/charge, repair leak |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker, bad capacitor/contactor | Reset breaker once | Test and replace capacitor/contactor |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor, verify compressor |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low charge or failing compressor | Check filter and coil | Measure charge, test compressor |
| Weak cooling, dirty outdoor coil | Blocked condenser | Rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean, straighten fins |
| Short cycling | Low charge or control fault | Check filter | Read faults, gauge system |
| No cooling, thermostat message (XL/XV) | Communication/board fault | Check breaker, thermostat power | Diagnose ComfortLink II wiring/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Regional pricing and brand markup vary widely.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$400
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,200+ (often near the cost of a new outdoor unit — get a replacement quote too)
- **Control board (XL/XV communicating):** $400–$900

If your system uses older R-22 refrigerant, recharge costs are much higher, and replacement may make more financial sense than repair.

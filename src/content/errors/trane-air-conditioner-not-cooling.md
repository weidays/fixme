## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code — it's the symptom you're chasing when your Trane air conditioner runs (or tries to) but the house never reaches setpoint. On modern Trane systems with a communicating thermostat (like the XL824 or a ComfortLink II setup), you may see a fault message or an error number on the display instead of a blink code. On older single-stage systems there's often no code at all — just warm air.

Because Trane uses several different outdoor units (XR13/14/16, XL16i/18i, XV18/20i) and multiple indoor control boards, the exact diagnostic path varies. What stays constant: cooling requires good airflow, clean coils, a working compressor and fan, correct refrigerant charge, and a control circuit that's actually calling for cool. This page walks through all of them, most-likely first.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil into a block of ice.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — a coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so nothing gets cold.
3. **Tripped breaker or disconnect to the outdoor unit** — indoor fan runs and blows air, but the outdoor unit is dead.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — very common. Compressor and/or fan won't start; you may hear a hum or clicking.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks airflow and drips water.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — weak cooling, long run times, ice on the copper lines. Requires a certified tech.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit sticks or burns out.
8. **Thermostat or wiring fault** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a broken low-voltage wire so no "cool" call reaches the equipment.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor unit overheats and shuts down on high-pressure lockout.
10. **Compressor failure** — the worst case; the unit draws no load or trips the breaker instantly.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything smells burnt or looks damaged.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering it 3–4 degrees to force a call.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker — there's usually a separate one for the outdoor unit. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro (never repeatedly reset).
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box by the condenser should be fully seated.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't starving airflow. Open them all.
- **Condensate:** Check the drain pan and line. A clogged drain can trip a float switch and stop cooling. Clear a visible clog and empty a full pan.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the outdoor copper lines, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** for a few hours to thaw before running again. Persistent icing means a real problem — call a tech.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. Those are pro-only.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Trane tech works airflow-out, not guess-and-replace. Expect them to:

1. **Confirm the call for cool** — verify 24V is reaching the outdoor unit's contactor.
2. **Check the contactor and capacitor** — measure capacitor MFD against the rating; test the contactor coil and contacts.
3. **Measure amp draw** on the compressor and fan motor to spot a locked or weak compressor.
4. **Inspect and wash the condenser coil**, and check the evaporator for ice or dirt.
5. **Attach gauges and take temperatures** — measure superheat/subcooling to judge refrigerant charge. Low charge means they'll **leak-search** (dye, electronic detector, or nitrogen pressure test), not just top off.
6. **Read the thermostat/board faults** on communicating systems for stored codes.

A quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without a capacitor test, amp reading, and leak search is worth a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor fan blows warm air, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or dead contactor | Reset breaker once; check disconnect | Replace contactor, verify 24V control circuit |
| Weak airflow, house never cools | Clogged filter or blocked vents | Replace filter, open all registers | Check static pressure, clean blower/coil |
| Ice on indoor coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw, change filter | Find airflow restriction or leak-search charge |
| Outdoor fan runs, compressor hums then stops | Failed run capacitor | None — pro repair | Test and replace capacitor, check amp draw |
| Long run times, warm supply air, iced lines | Low refrigerant from a leak | None — pro repair | Leak search, repair, evacuate and recharge |
| Breaker trips instantly on the outdoor unit | Shorted compressor or wiring | Do not keep resetting | Meg-test compressor, inspect wiring |
| Water around indoor unit, cooling drops out | Clogged condensate drain, float switch tripped | Clear visible drain clog, empty pan | Flush drain line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Regional rates and system size vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Coil cleaning (condenser or evaporator):** $150–$500
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location (a leaking evaporator coil is at the high end)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800–$2,500+ (often the point to weigh replacing the whole outdoor unit)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $90–$180, usually credited toward the repair

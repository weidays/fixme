## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific Trane fault code — but it's one of the most common calls Trane owners make. Your thermostat calls for cooling, but the air coming from the vents is warm, weak, or nonexistent.

Trane air conditioners don't display a single "not cooling" code. Instead, the cause shows up as behavior: the outdoor unit doesn't start, it starts but the compressor doesn't run, the indoor coil freezes, or everything runs but the air stays warm. On variable-speed **Comfortlink II** systems (XV18, XV20i), the indoor control board or the thermostat may log a communication or charge-related fault that points to the real problem.

This page walks through the causes in order of likelihood so you can rule out the easy stuff before paying for a service call.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or dead batteries** — set to "fan," "heat," a too-high setpoint, or a battery-powered thermostat that has died.
3. **Tripped breaker or disconnect** — the outdoor unit has its own breaker and often a pull-out disconnect near the condenser.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks cooling entirely once iced over.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure that stops the compressor and/or condenser fan from starting. You may hear humming.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — cools weakly, ices up, or long-cycles. Requires a pro to find and seal the leak.
7. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil packed with dirt, grass, or cottonwood can't reject heat.
8. **Failed contactor** — the electrical switch that powers the compressor; pitted or stuck contacts stop cooling.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit runs but produces no cold air, or trips the breaker.
10. **Communication fault (Comfortlink II)** — on variable-speed models, a lost data link between thermostat, air handler, and outdoor unit can stop cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening a sealed refrigerant system or touching high-voltage components.

- **Air filter** — pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers** — check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers in your panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect** — the pull-out box near the condenser should be seated fully. Don't touch internal wiring.
- **Vents and registers** — make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture or rugs. Closed vents reduce airflow and can freeze the coil.
- **Frozen coil check** — if you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the thermostat to OFF (or fan-only) and let it thaw for a few hours before running again.
- **Condensate** — check the drain pan and line. Many systems have a float switch that shuts off cooling when the drain clogs. Clearing a visible clog is DIY; wiring is not.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Trane tech should work systematically, not just "top off the refrigerant." Expect:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 14–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Electrical test** — measure the capacitor microfarads, test the contactor, and confirm the compressor and fan motor draw correct amperage.
4. **Refrigerant measurement** — connect gauges, check pressures, and calculate subcooling/superheat to determine if the charge is correct. Low charge means a **leak search**, not just adding refrigerant.
5. **Comfortlink II diagnostics** — on variable-speed models, they'll read fault history from the thermostat/control board over the data bus.
6. **Compressor test** — if power reaches the compressor but it won't run, they'll check windings and start components before condemning it.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without a leak search or capacitor/contactor test, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower & coil |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker / dead thermostat | Reset breaker once; new batteries | Test control wiring & transformer |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None (high voltage) | Replace capacitor |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Filter, thaw on fan-only | Find airflow issue or leak |
| Cools weakly, long run times | Low refrigerant leak | None | Leak search, seal, recharge |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Failed compressor | None | Compressor test/replacement |
| Runs then shuts off, water near unit | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, test float switch |
| Air handler on, outdoor unit dead | Bad contactor | None (high voltage) | Replace contactor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type; older R-22 systems cost more)
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,500+ (often makes replacing an old system more cost-effective)

Costs vary by region, system age, and whether your unit is under Trane's parts warranty. Registered systems typically carry a 10-year parts warranty — always ask the tech to check your serial number.

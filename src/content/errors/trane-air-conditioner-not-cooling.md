## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it is a symptom. On most Trane systems the air handler or condenser is still receiving a call for cooling from the thermostat, but the home is not reaching the set temperature. On variable-speed and communicating systems with a **ComfortLink II** thermostat, you may instead see a numbered alert on the display; those codes point to a specific board or sensor fault. This page covers the general "runs but won't cool" or "won't turn on" scenarios on Trane split-system air conditioners and matched heat pumps.

The behavior varies by system type. A single-stage XR unit either runs or it doesn't. A two-stage XL or variable-speed XV unit may run at low capacity and never ramp up, which feels like weak cooling rather than none at all.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil into a block of ice.
2. **Dirty or blocked outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, or dryer lint on the coil prevents heat rejection.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the fan may spin but the compressor won't start (or hums and shuts off).
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes long run times, ice on the copper line, and warm supply air. Always indicates a leak.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — result of low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks the coil entirely.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the condenser is pitted or stuck, so the compressor never energizes.
8. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or lost communication on ComfortLink systems.
9. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — least common but most expensive; usually on older systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breaker** — check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker for the AC. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Make sure it has at least 1–2 feet of clearance on all sides.
- **Supply and return vents** — open closed vents and remove furniture or rugs blocking them.
- **Condensate** — check that the drain pan isn't overflowing; a full pan can trip a safety float switch and stop cooling. Empty a full pan if you can reach it safely.
- **Ice** — if you see ice on the outdoor copper line or the indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** to thaw it. Do not run cooling until the ice is fully melted.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, remove electrical panels on the condenser, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. Verify the thermostat call and confirm 24V reaches the contactor.
2. Check line voltage and inspect the contactor for pitting or a stuck state.
3. Test the run/start capacitor with a meter against its rated microfarads.
4. Measure superheat and subcooling with refrigerant gauges to judge charge — this is how they confirm a refrigerant leak versus a mechanical problem.
5. Inspect the condenser and evaporator coils for airflow restriction.
6. Check compressor amp draw and windings if it won't start.
7. On ComfortLink II systems, connect to the board or read the thermostat alert log for stored fault codes.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, ask where the leak is — topping off a leaking system is a temporary and non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check duct/blower if unresolved |
| Indoor blower runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, find electrical fault |
| Fan spins outside, compressor won't start | Failed capacitor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper line or coil | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find/repair leak, recharge |
| Long run times, house never cools | Low refrigerant or dirty coil | Clean debris off condenser | Gauge charge, repair leak |
| Buzzing/humming then silence | Weak capacitor or contactor | None | Replace failed component |
| Nothing runs at all | Thermostat, power, or board | Check mode/batteries/breaker | Diagnose control circuit |
| Two-stage unit only runs "weak" | Stuck in low stage | Confirm thermostat settings | Check staging control/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and system):

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Breaker reset / no parts:** $0 DIY, or $75–$150 diagnostic fee
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often near the cost of a new outdoor unit)

If your system is older than 10–15 years and needs a compressor or coil, ask your technician to price full replacement — repair may not be worth it, especially with the phase-out of older refrigerants.

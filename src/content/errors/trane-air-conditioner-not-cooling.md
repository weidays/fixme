## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single blink code — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner may be powered on, the thermostat may be calling for cool, and the indoor blower may even be running, but the air coming from the vents is warm or only mildly cool, and the house never reaches the setpoint.

On Trane systems this can happen with **no fault code at all** (the outdoor unit is simply low on charge or has a bad capacitor), or it can be accompanied by a code on ComfortLink II thermostats or the indoor control board. Because Trane uses several control platforms, the exact diagnostic feedback varies:

- **Standard 24V systems (most XR/XL models):** typically no self-diagnostic display. Diagnosis is done with gauges and meters by a technician.
- **ComfortLink II (many XV variable-speed systems):** the wall control may show a fault or communication error that points to the outdoor unit or airflow.

If your Trane is a **furnace-based** system throwing a blink code, see the related furnace pages below — this page covers cooling-side symptoms.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops capacity and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer, so vents blow warm.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, dust, and cottonwood clog the coil so it can't reject heat.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common outdoor failure; the compressor or fan hums, struggles, or won't start.
5. **Low refrigerant charge (leak)** — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means a leak that needs repair, not a top-off.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** to the outdoor unit — indoor blower runs, outdoor unit is dead.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't pull in.
8. **Thermostat problem or wrong setting** — dead batteries, set to "fan on," or wiring/comm fault.
9. **Failed compressor** — least common but most expensive; unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop at anything you're unsure about.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, fan to **Auto** (not On), and the setpoint is a few degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and check it against a light. If it's gray and clogged, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers in your panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — don't keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris away from the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (no pressure washer).
- **Condensate drain:** If the drain is clogged, a float switch can shut cooling off. Check for standing water at the indoor unit and clear the drain line if you're comfortable doing so.
- **Look for ice:** If you see frost or ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, **turn the system off at the thermostat, set the fan to On**, and let it thaw for a few hours before calling. Running an iced unit can damage the compressor.

**Do not** attempt to open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** and confirm 24V is reaching the contactor.
2. **Measure supply/return temperature split** (should typically be ~15–20°F).
3. **Inspect airflow** — filter, coil condition, blower operation.
4. **Check the run capacitor** with a meter (microfarad reading vs. rating).
5. **Test the contactor** and incoming voltage at the outdoor unit.
6. **Connect gauges** to read pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling — this reveals low charge, restriction, or a failing compressor.
7. **Leak search** if charge is low (dye, electronic detector, or bubble test).
8. **On ComfortLink II systems**, read stored faults and communication status at the control.

A good tech won't just "add a pound of freon" and leave. If charge is low, they should find the leak first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vents blow warm, indoor fan runs | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Check airflow, coil, blower |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Find airflow issue or leak |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, wiring, fuse |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor |
| House warm, drain area wet | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain line | Clear line, verify float switch |
| Weak cooling, high energy bills | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse outside of coil | Deep clean, check charge |
| Runs constantly, small temp split | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call pro | Leak repair + recharge |
| Runs but zero cooling | Failed compressor | None — call pro | Compressor test/replace |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Coil cleaning (condenser or evaporator):** $150–$450
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Fan motor (outdoor):** $400–$700
- **TXV / metering device:** $400–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,200+ (often makes replacement worth considering on older units)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $90–$200, frequently applied to the repair

Prices vary by region, refrigerant type (R-410A vs older systems), and whether your unit is still under Trane's parts warranty — check your registration, as many Trane compressors and coils carry multi-year coverage.

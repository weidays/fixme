## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific Trane fault code — but it's one of the most common calls technicians get. Your air conditioner may be running (the outdoor fan spins, air blows from the vents) yet the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint, or the air coming out feels barely cool or warm.

On Trane systems this points to one of a handful of problems: restricted airflow, a frozen evaporator coil, an electrical fault stopping the compressor, or a refrigerant charge problem. Some Trane thermostats and communicating systems (like ComfortLink II) will display their own alerts, but a basic "warm air" complaint usually has no code at all.

The good news: several causes are safe, cheap DIY fixes. The rest need a licensed HVAC tech — especially anything involving refrigerant or the compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The #1 cause and completely DIY.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. Ice on the indoor coil or the copper line blocks heat transfer, so air blows warm.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" or "heat"), or a miscalibrated/failed thermostat.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor condenser has no power, so only the indoor fan runs.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor and/or condenser fan can't start; you may hear humming or clicking.
6. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil is caked with dirt, grass, or debris, so the system can't reject heat.
7. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, cutting power to the compressor.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling and causes coil freezing. Requires a certified tech to find and fix.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive failure. The condenser runs but doesn't compress refrigerant, so no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these first — they're safe and fix a surprising number of "not cooling" complaints:

- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, clogged, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone often restores cooling.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breaker.** Look in your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (both the indoor air handler/furnace circuit and the outdoor condenser circuit). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Look at the outdoor unit.** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Make sure the fan is spinning when the system calls for cooling.
- **Check for ice.** If you see frost or ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** (fan can stay ON to help thaw) for a few hours, then run it again. Repeated freezing means low refrigerant or airflow — call a tech.
- **Check vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Check the condensate drain/pan.** A clogged drain or full pan can trip a safety float switch and stop cooling. Clear standing water if you can safely reach it.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good Trane tech works methodically — here's what a fair diagnosis looks like so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat operation and settings.
2. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower, evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Measure the temperature split** across the evaporator (return vs. supply air); ~14–22°F is typical for a healthy system.
4. **Check electrical components** — test the capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and confirm voltage reaches the compressor.
5. **Attach gauges and read pressures** — this reveals low charge, restrictions, or compressor problems. Requires EPA certification.
6. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low — dye, electronic detector, or soap bubbles.
7. **Test the compressor** for proper amp draw and starting.

Ask for the measured readings (capacitor rating, temperature split, refrigerant pressures). A tech who quotes a compressor without showing you pressures and amp readings deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but feels warm | Dirty filter / low airflow | Replace filter, open vents | Check coil, airflow, charge |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Frozen evaporator (low airflow/charge) | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find cause; leak search |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker or blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Diagnose why it tripped |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call a pro | Test & replace capacitor |
| Outdoor coil caked in debris | Dirty condenser coil | Gently rinse fins, clear debris | Deep coil cleaning |
| Warm air, weak cooling over time | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call a pro | Leak repair & recharge |
| Condenser runs, no cooling at all | Failed compressor | None — call a pro | Compressor replacement |
| Nothing runs, standing water in pan | Clogged condensate / float switch | Clear drain if reachable | Clear drain, replace switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 installed
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$300
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200 (often credited toward the repair)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (sometimes worth replacing the whole condenser instead)

If your Trane is under warranty, the compressor part may be covered — you'd still pay labor and refrigerant. Have your model and serial number ready to check registration.

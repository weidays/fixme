## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific blink code — it's the symptom you notice when your Trane air conditioner runs but the house stays warm, or when the outdoor unit won't kick on at all. Some Trane systems with communicating controls (like ComfortLink II thermostats) may display a fault or lockout on the wall unit, but most classic single-stage systems give you no code at all. You just feel warm air from the vents.

Because Trane offers everything from basic single-stage condensers to variable-speed XV20i units, the exact diagnostic path varies. This page covers the most common, well-documented reasons a residential Trane split system stops cooling — and what's a safe homeowner check versus a pro repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — Restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can freeze the evaporator, which stops cooling entirely.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer. Vents blow weak, cool-then-warm air.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — The outdoor condenser has its own breaker/disconnect; if it's tripped, the fan and compressor won't run.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — A very common failure. The compressor or fan motor hums but won't start.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — Reduces capacity and often freezes the coil. Requires a licensed tech.
6. **Bad contactor** — The relay that sends power to the compressor gets pitted or stuck, so the condenser won't energize.
7. **Clogged condensate drain / safety float switch** — A full drain pan trips a float switch that shuts the system off to prevent water damage.
8. **Thermostat or control issue** — Dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/communication fault on communicating systems.
9. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — The most expensive outcome, usually the last thing diagnosed.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform:

- **Thermostat**: Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. On battery-powered models, replace the batteries.
- **Air filter**: Pull it out. If it's gray and clogged, replace it with the correct size. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Breakers**: Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers in your panel. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect**: The box near the condenser should be fully seated/on.
- **Vents and registers**: Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain**: If your drain line is clogged and the pan is full, a float switch may have shut the system off. You can carefully clear a visible clog and empty the pan.
- **Frozen coil**: If you see ice on the refrigerant line or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF and set the fan to ON for a few hours to thaw it. Do not run cooling on a frozen unit.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety float switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap to expensive, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify airflow** — Checks filter, blower operation, and coil for ice or dirt buildup.
2. **Confirm power and controls** — Tests the thermostat call, 24V control voltage, contactor, and low-voltage safeties (including the float switch).
3. **Test the capacitor** — Uses a meter to check microfarad rating against the label.
4. **Check the contactor** — Looks for pitted contacts or a coil that won't pull in.
5. **Measure refrigerant charge** — Connects gauges, reads superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak, which they'll then locate with dye or an electronic detector.
6. **Test the compressor and fan motor** — Amp draw, windings, and starting components — done only after cheaper causes are ruled out.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "compressor" or "recharge" without first confirming airflow and the capacitor.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil with fan ON | Verify airflow, inspect coil, check charge |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, check wiring |
| Compressor or fan hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (capacitor holds charge) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on refrigerant line | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| System shuts off, water in pan | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear visible clog, empty pan | Clear line, test float switch |
| No cooling, thermostat blank | Dead batteries or control fault | Replace batteries | Diagnose control/communication fault |
| Cools poorly on hottest days | Undercharge or dirty condenser coil | Rinse outdoor coil gently | Check charge, clean coil |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **Air filter**: $10–$40 — DIY
- **Condensate drain cleaning**: $75–$250 professionally
- **Run/dual capacitor**: $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor**: $150–$350 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge**: $500–$1,800+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far pricier)
- **Condenser fan motor**: $400–$700 installed
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement**: $1,000–$2,500+
- **Compressor replacement**: $1,800–$2,900+ — often the point where replacing the whole condenser or system makes more financial sense

If your unit is under warranty, Trane covers many parts (typically 10 years with registration), but labor is usually separate — confirm before authorizing work.

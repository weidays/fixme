## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single blink code — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner may be running but blowing warm or lukewarm air, short-cycling, or not turning on at all. Trane central AC systems don't usually flash a numeric fault for "no cooling" the way a furnace board does; instead, the problem shows up as poor performance while the thermostat keeps calling for cool.

That said, some Trane variable-speed condensers (XV18, XV20i) and communicating systems paired with a ComfortLink II thermostat **will** log fault codes on the outdoor board or thermostat display. If you have one of those, note any codes shown — they help a technician narrow the cause fast. On single-stage units (XR13, XR14, XR16) there's typically no display, so diagnosis is done with gauges and meters.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can freeze the evaporator so no cool air moves.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil ices over and cooling stops entirely.
3. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system can't absorb heat properly. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means a leak.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the outdoor fan may spin but the compressor won't start, or vice versa.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling capacity drops on hot days.
6. **Bad contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the compressor fails to close.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/setting fault.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive cause; usually a last resort after other checks.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the single most common cause of weak or no cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the target temp is below room temp. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. Set the fan to **AUTO**, not ON (ON alone blows uncooled air).
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the air handler or outdoor unit. Reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Outdoor unit:** Check that the condenser fins aren't buried in leaves, grass clippings, or dryer lint. Keep 2 feet of clearance. Gently rinse debris from the outside with a garden hose (power off first).
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** to thaw it (can take a few hours). Replace the filter, then try cooling again.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trigger a float switch that shuts down cooling. Look for standing water near the indoor unit.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, remove electrical panels on the outdoor unit, or bypass any safety switches.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work through this in roughly this order:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirm the thermostat sends 24V and the system responds.
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Measure electrical** — test the capacitor's microfarad rating, check the contactor, and read amp draw on the compressor and fan motor.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — read suction and liquid pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct. On variable-speed units they may pull codes from the ComfortLink board first.
5. **Inspect the condenser coil** — check for restriction and adequate heat rejection.
6. **Leak search** — if the charge is low, use electronic detectors or dye to find the leak. A reputable tech won't just top off and leave.

If a technician wants to add refrigerant without looking for a leak, that's a red flag — ask why.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air handler runs, warm air | Dirty filter / frozen coil | Replace filter, thaw coil | Check airflow, coil, charge |
| Ice on refrigerant lines | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, thaw, swap filter | Find restriction or leak, recharge |
| Outdoor fan spins, no cold air | Bad capacitor / contactor | None (electrical) | Test & replace capacitor/contactor |
| Nothing runs outdoors | Tripped breaker / blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Diagnose short or failed component |
| Weak cooling on hot days | Dirty condenser coil / low charge | Rinse coil exterior | Deep-clean coil, check charge |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries | Trace 24V wiring & transformer |
| Loud hum, no start | Failing compressor / capacitor | None | Electrical test, compressor check |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; regional prices vary):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ (depends on leak location and refrigerant type; R-410A vs. older R-22 systems cost more)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$3,000+ (often worth weighing against a new system if out of warranty)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200, often credited toward the repair

Trane compressors and coils often carry a 10-year limited parts warranty **if registered** — check your original registration before paying for parts.

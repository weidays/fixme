## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific Trane error code — it's a symptom. Your system runs (or tries to), but the air coming from your vents is warm, weak, or barely conditioned. On modern Trane variable-speed systems (XV series) with communicating controls, you may see a fault or status code on the thermostat or indoor unit board, but most single- and two-stage Trane ACs (XR, XL series) don't display a numeric code for general cooling loss.

That means diagnosis relies on **symptoms and measurements**, not a single blink pattern. The root cause is almost always one of a handful of issues: restricted airflow, a frozen coil, low refrigerant from a leak, or a failed electrical component in the outdoor unit.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops efficiency, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The single most common and cheapest cause.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or malfunctioning** — set to "fan on" instead of "cool," wrong temperature, or dead batteries mimicking a cooling failure.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer, so the unit runs but doesn't cool.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the outdoor fan or compressor won't start reliably; you may hear humming or clicking with no fan spin.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity and can freeze the coil. Never a "just top it off" fix — the leak must be found.
6. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — blocked by grass, leaves, or dirt, so heat can't be rejected outside.
7. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit; pitted or stuck contacts stop the compressor.
8. **Blower motor or compressor failure** — the most expensive causes, requiring professional replacement.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here — don't open the sealed refrigerant system or the outdoor electrical panel.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. Do this first — a dirty filter causes a huge share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, leave it off and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear grass, leaves, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (power off).
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** and run the **fan only** for a few hours to thaw it, then replace the filter. If it re-freezes, you need a technician.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trigger a safety float switch that stops cooling. Check for standing water near the indoor unit.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech follows a repeatable process — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects filter, blower operation, and coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Reads the outdoor electrical** — tests the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor for pitting, and confirms the compressor and fan draw proper amperage.
4. **Connects refrigerant gauges** — measures pressures and superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct. This requires EPA certification.
5. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, uses electronic detectors or dye to find the leak before recharging.
6. **Communicating systems (XV series)** — pulls fault history from the thermostat or control board for compressor, sensor, or communication faults.

Be cautious of any tech who "just adds refrigerant" without a leak search — that's a temporary fix that ignores the real problem.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower, ductwork |
| No cold air, fan blows | Wrong thermostat setting | Set to Cool/Auto, replace batteries | Test thermostat wiring/board |
| Ice on lines or coil | Frozen evaporator | Off + fan-only to thaw, new filter | Find airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Outdoor fan hums, won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — high voltage | Test and replace capacitor |
| Warm air, hissing/oily spots | Refrigerant leak | None — sealed system | Locate leak, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Bad contactor or breaker | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Breaker keeps tripping | Shorted compressor/wiring | Leave breaker off | Diagnose electrical fault |
| Dirty, hot outdoor coil | Blocked condenser | Rinse fins, clear debris | Deep coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Prices vary by region and system size.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$450
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (higher if the leak is in the coil)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Blower motor:** $400–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

A basic diagnostic service call typically runs **$75–$200**, often credited toward the repair.

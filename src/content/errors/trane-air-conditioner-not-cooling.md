## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific Trane error code — it's the symptom homeowners describe when the air conditioner runs (or tries to run) but the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On modern Trane systems with communicating controls (ComfortLink II) you may see an actual fault or informational message on the thermostat; on older single- and two-stage units there's no display at all, just warm air from the vents.

Because "not cooling" covers a wide range of failures — from a $15 filter to a failed compressor — this page walks you through the most likely causes in order, what you can safely check yourself, and what a technician should actually be doing so you can sanity-check any quote.

If your Trane furnace/air handler is throwing a blink code instead, that points to the indoor control board — see **Related codes** below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Dirty condenser or evaporator coil** — a coated outdoor coil can't reject heat; a dirty indoor coil chokes airflow.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant. Ice blocks cooling entirely; vents may blow slightly cool then warm.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bulged or dead capacitor stops the compressor or fan from starting. Very common on units 8+ years old.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, long run times, and often ice. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up"; low charge means a leak.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown outdoor disconnect** — the outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor fan still runs.
7. **Contactor failure** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
8. **Thermostat misconfiguration or wiring fault** — set wrong, dead batteries, or a bad wire preventing the cool call.
9. **Clogged condensate drain / safety float switch** — a full drain pan trips the float and shuts cooling off to prevent water damage.
10. **Failed compressor** — least common but most expensive; the unit hums, trips the breaker, or won't start even with a good capacitor.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If none of them resolve it, call a licensed HVAC tech.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. Try lowering it 3–4°F to force a call.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (both the indoor air handler and the outdoor unit may have separate breakers). Reset once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals a real fault.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** The pull-out box near the condenser should be fully seated.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow. Keep most vents open.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from the condenser fins. Make sure the fan is spinning when cooling is called.
- **Condensate:** If you see standing water near the indoor unit or a full drain pan, a clogged drain may have tripped the float switch. Clearing the line can restore cooling.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** but leave the **FAN ON** to thaw it (can take a few hours). Running it while frozen makes things worse.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass the float or safety switches, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work methodically, not just "top off the refrigerant." Expect them to:

1. Verify the thermostat call and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor unit's contactor.
2. Check the air filter and inspect both coils for dirt or ice.
3. Test the run/start capacitor with a meter (a bad cap is a top cause and a quick find).
4. Measure amp draw on the compressor and fan motor.
5. Connect gauges to read suction/discharge pressures and calculate **superheat and subcooling** to judge refrigerant charge — not just "it's a little low."
6. If charge is low, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or bubble test) rather than just adding refrigerant.
7. On communicating (ComfortLink II) systems, pull fault history from the thermostat.

A quote that jumps straight to adding refrigerant without measuring pressures or looking for a leak is a red flag. Ask for the superheat/subcooling numbers.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower, coil |
| Ice on lines/indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn system off, fan on to thaw | Find airflow issue or leak, recharge |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker, disconnect, contactor | Reset breaker once, seat disconnect | Test/replace contactor, check wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Long run times, never reaches setpoint | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Clean debris off outdoor unit | Coil cleaning, charge check, leak search |
| System off, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear drain line, use tablets | Clear line, test float switch |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor or electrical fault | Stop resetting, call pro | Diagnose compressor/wiring |
| Blows cool then warm | Freezing evaporator | Thaw with fan on, replace filter | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $0–$40 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Coil cleaning (condenser or evaporator):** $150–$500
- **Fan motor (condenser or blower):** $300–$700
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs older R-22 is much pricier)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth weighing against a new system on older units)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200, frequently applied toward the repair

Two-stage and variable-speed Trane systems (XV series) can cost more to diagnose because of communicating controls and specialty parts.

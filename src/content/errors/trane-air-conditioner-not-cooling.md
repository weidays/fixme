## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your Trane air conditioner runs (or tries to run) but the house never reaches the set temperature. On modern Trane systems the indoor control board or a communicating thermostat may log a related fault, but many cooling failures show no code at all because the problem is mechanical or refrigerant-related rather than electronic.

Because "not cooling" can come from a dozen root causes ranging from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you through what's safe to check yourself and how a pro narrows it down. Exact diagnostics and any logged codes vary by model (XR, XL, XV series) and by whether you have a single-stage, two-stage, or variable-speed system.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — Restricts airflow, drops coil temperature, and can freeze the evaporator coil. The single most common and cheapest cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil (outdoor unit)** — Grass, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection, so the system runs but can't cool effectively.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — Caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, and warm air from vents.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — A weak capacitor keeps the compressor or condenser fan from starting. Common failure part, often affordable.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — The system undercools and may freeze. Refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge always means a leak.
6. **Failed contactor** — The relay that powers the outdoor unit burns or pits, so the condenser never energizes.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — Outdoor unit gets no power; indoor blower may still run and blow room-temperature air.
8. **Thermostat problem or dead batteries** — Wrong mode, bad wiring, or a dead battery in a battery-powered thermostat.
9. **Condensate safety switch tripped** — A full drain pan opens a float switch and disables cooling to prevent water damage.
10. **Failed compressor** — The most expensive outcome; the unit hums, trips, or does nothing while the fan may still spin.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting off.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter causes a large share of cooling complaints.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the temperature is below room temperature. Set the fan to AUTO. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking airflow.
- **Outdoor unit:** Gently rinse visible grass and debris off the condenser fins with a garden hose (power off first). Clear a 2-foot space around the unit.
- **Condensate drain:** If there's a full drain pan or a clogged line, cooling may be locked out. You can clear a visible clog or empty a full pan.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully — this can take several hours before you troubleshoot further.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — Measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — Inspects filter, blower operation, and coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Inspects electrical** — Tests the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, verifies incoming voltage, and looks at the control board or thermostat wiring.
4. **Reads refrigerant pressures** — Connects gauges to check high/low side pressures and superheat/subcooling. Abnormal readings point to a leak, overcharge, or restriction.
5. **Leak search** — If charge is low, uses electronic detectors, UV dye, or nitrogen to find the leak (a proper repair, not just a "top-off").
6. **Compressor test** — Checks amp draw, windings, and start behavior if all else is ruled out.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to add refrigerant without searching for a leak, or who quotes a compressor without showing you electrical or pressure readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air from vents, blower runs | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter; thaw coil (fan ON) | Verify airflow, check charge and coil |
| Ice on refrigerant lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Thaw fully; replace filter | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor or contactor | Confirm breaker on | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Outdoor unit dead, no hum | Tripped breaker or blown fuse | Reset breaker once | Trace power fault, replace fuse |
| Unit hums but won't start | Weak capacitor or seized compressor | None — call pro | Test capacitor, then compressor |
| Cooling stops after a while | Overheating, dirty condenser | Rinse condenser coil | Deep clean, check amps and charge |
| No cooling, water near unit | Tripped condensate float switch | Clear drain, empty pan | Clear line, verify switch |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Check low-voltage wiring, board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges. Actual pricing varies by region, system size, and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far more expensive).

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250 professionally, or DIY with a coil cleaner
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$450 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Low-voltage fuse / minor wiring:** $75–$200
- **Condensate switch / drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,500 (higher for R-22 or evaporator coil leaks)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$3,500+ (often worth weighing against full system replacement on older units)

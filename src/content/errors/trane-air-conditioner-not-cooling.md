## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint homeowners report about a Trane air conditioner. Your system may run continuously, blow warm or room-temperature air from the vents, or short-cycle without ever bringing the house down to the thermostat setpoint.

Unlike a Trane furnace, most single- and two-stage Trane condensers (XR and XL series) don't flash diagnostic codes at the outdoor unit. Communicating variable-speed models (XV18, XV20i with ComfortLink II or a Trane XL/S-series thermostat) will store fault data you can view on the thermostat screen — so if you have one of those, check the alerts menu first.

Because cooling problems span airflow, electrical, and sealed refrigerant issues, this is rated **pro**: the safe checks below are worth doing, but most genuine "not cooling" repairs require a licensed HVAC technician.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the indoor evaporator coil, cutting capacity and eventually freezing the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer and warm air comes out the vents.
3. **Dirty condenser coil** (outdoor unit) — leaves, grass, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or outdoor fan hums or won't start; one of the most common electrical failures.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; often accompanied by ice on the copper lines.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the compressor never engages.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse / disconnect** — the outdoor unit gets no power at all.
8. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/config fault.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor fan doesn't spin, causing high-pressure lockout.
10. **Failing compressor** — the least common but most expensive; unit runs but produces little or no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter** — pull it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of poor cooling.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breaker/disconnect** — check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Vents and registers** — make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit clearance** — clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (power off) if they're visibly dirty.
- **Condensate drain** — a clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut the system off. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear a visible clog if you can reach it.
- **Frozen coil** — if you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper lines outside, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** to thaw it (can take several hours). Then replace the filter before restarting.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass safety switches, or spin the outdoor fan by hand with power on.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — this helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspects the filter, blower, and evaporator coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Test electrical components** — measures the run capacitor's microfarads against its rating, checks the contactor, and confirms voltage at the disconnect.
4. **Inspect the condenser coil** — cleans it if fouled, since a dirty coil mimics many other faults.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — reads suction and liquid pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge the refrigerant charge. Low readings prompt a leak search (electronic detector or dye).
6. **Check the compressor** — measures amp draw and winding resistance if a hard-start or failure is suspected.
7. **Read stored faults** — on communicating XV models, pulls alert history from the thermostat.

If a tech proposes adding refrigerant without discussing a leak search, ask why — refrigerant doesn't get "used up," so low charge means it's leaking somewhere.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vents blow warm, unit running | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace filter; check for ice | Measure charge, find/fix leak |
| Ice on indoor coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Diagnose airflow vs. charge |
| Outdoor fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — power off | Replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit completely dead | Tripped breaker, bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, wiring |
| Weak cooling, warm outdoor unit | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep-clean coil, check charge |
| Short-cycling, water in pan | Clogged condensate drain | Clear visible clog | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Runs constantly, little cooling | Failing compressor | None | Test compressor, quote options |
| Wrong/no response at vents | Thermostat setting or fault | Set to COOL, replace batteries | Check wiring and configuration |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included where noted:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / reset:** $0–$10 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22 is far pricier)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often the point where replacing the whole condenser makes more sense)

Prices vary by region, unit age, and whether your system is still under Trane's parts warranty — check your registration, as many Trane units carry a 10-year parts limited warranty.

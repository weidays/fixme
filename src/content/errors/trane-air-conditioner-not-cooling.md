## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single blink code — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner may be running (fan spinning, thermostat calling for cool) but the air coming out of your vents is warm or barely cool, or the house never reaches the setpoint.

On most Trane systems, a true fault will show up either as no cooling with normal behavior (usually airflow or refrigerant issues) or as the outdoor unit locking out and displaying a diagnostic code on the control board. Higher-end variable-speed platforms (XV series with communicating controls) can display specific fault codes at the thermostat, while single-stage units (XR series) give you far less on-board feedback. **Because behavior varies by board and model, use your unit's specific service label — don't assume every "not cooling" event is the same fault.**

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — dirt, grass, or cottonwood fluff blocks heat rejection, so the system can't cool.
3. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks cooling entirely.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling capacity, longer run times, ice, or hissing.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — compressor or condenser fan won't start; often a low-cost fix.
6. **Contactor failure** — worn or pitted contactor won't energize the outdoor unit.
7. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a wiring/setting fault.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor disconnect or panel breaker cut power.
9. **Clogged condensate drain / full pan** — a float safety switch shuts cooling off to prevent overflow.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most serious and expensive; unit runs but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no panels, refrigerant, or safety-switch bypassing.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 cause of weak cooling and frozen coils.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers/disconnect. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside coil fins with a garden hose (no pressure washer) when the unit is off.
- **Condensate drain:** If your indoor unit has a float switch and cooling stopped, a full drain pan may have shut it down. Check for standing water; drain tablets can help prevent clogs.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to OFF (fan can stay on) and let it thaw for a few hours before restarting.

If cooling doesn't return after these steps, it's a pro job.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. **Verify the thermostat call** and confirm 24V control signals reach the outdoor unit.
2. **Check the outdoor unit** for a running compressor and condenser fan; measure the capacitor and inspect the contactor.
3. **Read any board fault codes** (especially on XV/communicating systems) to narrow the cause.
4. **Measure airflow and temperature split** across the indoor coil — a normal split is roughly 16–22°F.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and check pressures, superheat, and subcooling to identify low charge or a restriction.
6. **Leak-search** if refrigerant is low — using electronic detectors, dye, or nitrogen — because topping off without a repair is a temporary and non-compliant fix.
7. **Inspect the compressor** electrically (windings, amp draw) if all else checks out.

A trustworthy quote names the specific part or fault and explains the finding — be cautious of any tech who just adds refrigerant without discussing a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Check coil, blower, ductwork |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find airflow restriction or leak |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cooling | Failed capacitor or contactor | None (electrical) | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Long run times, house won't cool | Low refrigerant / dirty coil | Rinse outdoor coil | Leak search, recharge, coil clean |
| Nothing runs outside | Tripped breaker/disconnect | Reset breaker once | Diagnose electrical fault/short |
| Cooling stopped, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain (float switch) | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, verify float switch |
| Unit hums but won't start | Compressor or capacitor fault | None | Test compressor, capacitor, hard-start |
| No cooling, no warm air, thermostat blank | Dead thermostat batteries/power | Replace batteries | Check control wiring/transformer |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run/dual capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$500 (more for smart/communicating)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing to full-system replacement on older units)

Prices vary by region, unit size, and refrigerant type (R-410A vs older R-22 systems cost more to service).

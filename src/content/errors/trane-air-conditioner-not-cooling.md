## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific Trane fault code — most Trane air conditioners are analog at the outdoor unit and don't flash error codes the way furnaces do. If your unit is communicating (ComfortLink II with an XL or XV thermostat), you may see a fault message on the thermostat screen instead of a blink code.

The complaint covers several distinct situations that homeowners lump together:

- The system runs constantly but the air feels lukewarm.
- The indoor blower runs but the outdoor unit never starts.
- The outdoor fan spins but the house temperature climbs.
- The system short-cycles (starts and stops quickly) without cooling.

Each of these points to a different root cause, so identifying **which** behavior you have is the first step.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, freezes the evaporator coil, and kills cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — dirt, grass clippings, or cottonwood on the coil traps heat and prevents the system from rejecting it.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — caused by low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil ices over and blocks air completely.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure; the compressor and/or outdoor fan can't start. Often the outdoor fan runs but the compressor doesn't.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling gradually; requires leak repair, not just a "top off."
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit fails, so nothing outside turns on.
7. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a broken low-voltage wire.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit runs but produces no cooling.
10. **Control board fault (communicating models)** — on ComfortLink II systems, a board or communication error can stop the compressor from staging up.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If none of them fixes the problem, call a licensed technician.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below the current room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter causes more no-cooling calls than any other single issue.
- **Return and supply vents:** Make sure supply registers are open and returns aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker (both the indoor air handler and outdoor unit may have separate breakers). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the coil, and gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (power off). Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF and the fan to ON for a few hours to thaw it, then run cooling again with a clean filter.
- **Condensate:** Check that the drain line isn't backed up and hasn't tripped a float safety switch, which can shut the system down.

Do **not** open the electrical compartment, add refrigerant, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping. Those steps require training and tools.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Trane tech follows a logical sequence. Knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint and airflow** — checks filter, blower operation, and temperature split (return vs. supply air; a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Inspect for a frozen coil** — if frozen, they diagnose the root cause (airflow vs. charge) rather than just thawing it.
3. **Check power and controls** — measures line voltage, 24V control voltage, and tests the contactor and capacitor with a meter.
4. **Read the capacitor's microfarad rating** — a weak capacitor is a cheap, common fix.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — reads suction and liquid pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge the charge. This is where a **refrigerant leak** or **restriction** shows up.
6. **Test the compressor** — checks amp draw and windings if pressures suggest a compressor fault.
7. **On communicating (ComfortLink II) systems** — reads fault history from the thermostat/board to confirm whether it's a compressor, sensor, or communication issue.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, or "replace the compressor" without electrical and pressure readings to back it up.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Clogged filter | Replace the filter | Confirm airflow, check blower |
| Ice on lines/indoor coil | Low airflow or low charge | Thaw with fan ON, new filter | Diagnose charge vs. airflow, find leak |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cool | Failed capacitor or compressor | None (electrical) | Test/replace capacitor, check compressor |
| Nothing runs outside | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Cools poorly on hot days | Low refrigerant / dirty coil | Rinse outdoor coil | Leak search, recover/recharge |
| Short-cycling | Capacitor, control, or charge | Check filter | Electrical and pressure testing |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries / wiring | Replace batteries | Trace low-voltage fault |
| Gradual loss over weeks | Refrigerant leak | None | Locate and repair leak, recharge |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US prices including labor. Your area, unit age, and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. older R-22) affect the total significantly.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Thermostat batteries:** under $10 (DIY).
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250.
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$250.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location; R-22 units cost more due to refrigerant scarcity.
- **TXV (metering device) replacement:** $500–$900.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+; often the point where replacing the whole outdoor unit makes more sense.
- **ComfortLink II control board:** $400–$900 on communicating systems.

If your unit is 12+ years old and facing a compressor or major refrigerant repair, ask your tech to compare the cost against replacement.

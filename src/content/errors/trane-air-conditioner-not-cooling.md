## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint Trane homeowners bring to a tech. Your system may run continuously, blow warm or weak air, short-cycle, or not turn on at all. Trane air conditioners rely on a matched outdoor condenser and indoor coil moving refrigerant in a sealed loop; when any part of that loop, its airflow, or its electrical controls fails, cooling drops off.

Some Trane systems (variable-speed XV models with ComfortLink II controls) will display fault codes or communicate errors to a compatible thermostat. Basic single-stage XR and XL units have no display — you diagnose them by symptoms and measurements. Because it can range from a $15 filter to a dead compressor, "not cooling" is rated **pro**: safe homeowner checks first, then a licensed HVAC tech.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked airflow** — the single most frequent cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, dust, or cottonwood packing the coil chokes heat rejection so the system can't cool.
3. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks airflow entirely and blows warm.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure; the fan may spin but the compressor won't start (you'll often hear a hum or click).
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak, gradually worsening cooling. Requires an EPA-certified tech; never a top-off-and-forget.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the outdoor unit; pitted or stuck contacts stop the compressor.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown control/low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
8. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a broken wire prevents a cooling call.
9. **Clogged condensate drain / float switch tripped** — a safety float shuts cooling off to prevent water damage.
10. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — least common but most expensive; the unit hums, trips the breaker, or does nothing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything is beyond them.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 no-cool cause.
- **Breakers:** Check the panel for a tripped breaker for the air handler and the outdoor unit. Reset **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a tech — do not keep resetting.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (power off). Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see standing water near the indoor unit or the system stopped after getting wet, the float switch may have tripped. Clearing the drain line may reset it.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON is okay) and let it thaw fully — several hours — before running again.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass the float or safety switches, or repeatedly reset a tripping breaker.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Airflow check** — inspects filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Electrical check** — tests capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, confirms 24V control signal and line voltage at the outdoor unit.
4. **Refrigerant check** — attaches gauges and measures pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge charge. Low readings point to a leak, which they'll locate with a detector or dye.
5. **Component test** — verifies compressor and condenser fan motor draw the correct amperage.
6. **Controls** — on ComfortLink II / variable-speed systems, reads stored fault codes from the board or thermostat.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to "add a pound of Freon" without looking for the leak — that's a temporary fix on a sealed system.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runs constantly, weak/warm air | Dirty filter or condenser coil | Replace filter; rinse outdoor coil | Deep coil cleaning, airflow check |
| Ice on lines/indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw fully, replace filter | Find restriction or refrigerant leak |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cooling | Failed capacitor or compressor | None — call a tech | Test/replace capacitor; check compressor |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker, contactor, fuse | Reset breaker once; check thermostat | Replace contactor/fuse, trace wiring |
| Cooling fades over weeks | Slow refrigerant leak | None — call a tech | Leak search and sealed-system repair |
| Humming, breaker trips | Failed compressor or motor | Turn off, stop resetting | Amp-test and replace compressor/motor |
| Stopped after water near unit | Tripped condensate float switch | Clear the drain line | Clear/repair drain, service pan |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Repair low-voltage wiring/transformer |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Regional and model differences apply.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Low-voltage fuse / thermostat wiring repair:** $100–$300
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth weighing against full-system replacement on older units)

If your Trane is under 10 years old, check whether the parts (or compressor) are still under the manufacturer's limited warranty — you may pay only labor.

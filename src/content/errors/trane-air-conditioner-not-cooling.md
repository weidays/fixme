## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Your Trane air conditioner may be running (outdoor fan spinning, indoor blower moving air) but the air coming from your vents is warm, lukewarm, or not cold enough to hold your thermostat setpoint. On some Trane systems paired with a communicating thermostat (like the ComfortLink II XL series), you may also see a comfort-related alert or a fault stored in the thermostat's diagnostics menu — but a basic thermostat will show nothing at all.

Because "not cooling" covers everything from a $20 filter to a dead compressor, the goal of this page is to help you rule out the easy, safe stuff yourself and understand what a technician should be checking so you can sanity-check any quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, cutting cooling capacity and often causing the coil to ice up.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" or "heat"), or a setpoint that isn't actually calling for cooling.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser has its own breaker; if it trips, the indoor blower runs but no cold air is produced.
4. **Dirty condenser or evaporator coil** — caked dirt and debris insulate the coils and kill heat transfer.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan motor hums or won't start, so no cooling.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks the coil until it thaws.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak or no cooling, sometimes with hissing or ice. Requires a licensed pro.
8. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay in the outdoor unit that won't pull in, so the compressor never energizes.
9. **Failed compressor or condenser fan motor** — the most expensive outcome, sometimes preceded by a bad capacitor.
10. **Blocked condensate drain / float switch tripped** — a full drain pan can trip a safety switch that shuts down cooling to prevent water damage.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, remove electrical panels on the outdoor unit, or handle wiring.

- **Filter:** Pull the filter. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. Run the system 30 minutes and recheck cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the fan is on **Auto**, and the setpoint is at least a few degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker — the AC often has its own labeled breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Closing too many rooms can freeze the coil.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** Clear grass, leaves, and debris from around the condenser and gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (power off). Keep 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water pooling near the indoor unit or the system shut off suddenly, the drain may be clogged and the float switch tripped. Clearing the drain line is DIY-friendly; a badly overflowing pan is a call for help.
- **Check for ice:** If you see frost or ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to **Fan Only** for a few hours to thaw it, then run cooling again. If it re-freezes, you have an airflow or refrigerant problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent Trane tech should work through a logical sequence — this is what you're paying for:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor contactor.
2. **Measure electrical draw** on the compressor and fan motor, and **test the capacitor** with a multimeter for correct microfarad rating.
3. **Check the contactor** for pitted or welded contacts.
4. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower operation, and both coils for dirt or ice.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and take temperature readings to calculate **superheat and subcooling**. This tells them if the charge is correct without just "topping off."
6. **Leak-search** if the charge is low — with electronic detectors or dye — because adding refrigerant without fixing the leak is a temporary and non-compliant fix.
7. **Test the compressor** windings and amperage if it won't start after the capacitor and contactor check out.

Be wary of any tech who adds refrigerant without checking for a leak, or who recommends a full system replacement before diagnosing a capacitor or contactor.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor blower runs | Dirty filter or thermostat set wrong | Replace filter, verify Cool/Auto | Check airflow, blower speed |
| Outdoor unit silent, no cold air | Tripped breaker or dead contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, check wiring |
| Compressor hums but won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on coil or refrigerant lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set to Fan Only to thaw, replace filter | Diagnose charge, leak-search |
| Weak cooling, longer run times | Low refrigerant or dirty coils | Rinse outdoor coil, clear debris | Clean coils, check charge |
| System shut off, water near unit | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear drain line, add tablets | Clear blockage, test float switch |
| Nothing runs at all | Breaker, control board, or transformer | Reset breaker once | Test 24V transformer, board, wiring |
| Outdoor fan off, compressor hot | Failed fan motor or capacitor | None — call a pro | Replace fan motor / capacitor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included unless noted. Prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain cleaning:** $0 DIY – $150 pro
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Coil cleaning (evaporator or condenser):** $150–$500
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A costs more)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ (often makes replacing an older unit more cost-effective)
- **Full system replacement:** $5,000–$12,000+ for older units past their service life

A good rule of thumb: if a major repair (compressor, coil) costs more than a third of a new system and your unit is over 10–12 years old, get a replacement quote too.

## What this code means

**If the bang comes from the burner area, shut the furnace down before you do anything else.**

1. Set the thermostat to **Off** (not just a lower temperature).
2. Turn off the **furnace switch** — the light-switch-style toggle on or beside the cabinet.
3. **Do not restart it** to "see if it does it again." Each repeat is another pressure pulse inside the heat exchanger.
4. If you notice **any gas odor, or a scorched/burning smell**, leave the home first, then call your gas utility's emergency line or 911 from outside. Do not flip switches or use anything that could spark on your way out.

Leave the unit off until a qualified technician has inspected it.

A loud bang on startup is a **symptom, not a stored Lennox error code** — your control board typically will not log a specific fault for it unless the event also trips a limit or ignition sequence. There are two very different sources, and telling them apart matters:

1. **Delayed ignition** — gas flows into the burners but doesn't light immediately. It pools for a second or two, then ignites all at once with a "whump" or bang. This is a genuine safety concern: the pressure pulse can crack the heat exchanger, and it can push flame outside the burner box, a condition called flame rollout.
2. **Duct oil-canning / expansion bang** — the metal supply plenum or ductwork "pops" as the blower starts and air pressure changes, or as metal heats and expands. This is a nuisance noise, not a combustion problem.

If the bang comes from the **burner area** (near the front where the flames are), treat it as delayed ignition, keep the furnace off, and call a pro. If it comes from a **duct run, plenum, or register** a moment after the blower kicks on, it's more likely oil-canning. Because this behavior varies by burner design and duct layout across the ML, EL, SL, Merit, Elite, and Signature series, a technician should confirm the source before any repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty, misaligned, or corroded burners plus a weak or slow hot-surface igniter (delayed ignition).** Carbon buildup, rust, or an igniter that takes too long to reach temperature delays the flame, letting gas accumulate before it lights — the most common source of a true bang.
2. **Duct oil-canning from an undersized return or high static pressure.** A clogged filter or closed registers raise blower pressure, causing sheet metal to snap. Often the easiest to address.
3. **Low or fluctuating gas pressure / partially clogged burner orifices.** Uneven gas delivery makes ignition inconsistent and delayed.
4. **Blocked or fouled flame-crossover ports.** Flame doesn't carry cleanly from burner to burner, so some light late with a pop.
5. **Slightly out-of-spec gas valve or manifold pressure.** Too much or too little gas at the burner changes how it ignites.
6. **Heat exchanger already cracked.** A crack can change airflow and combustion dynamics, producing noise — and is itself a reason to stop using the furnace.

Delayed ignition (#1, #3–#6) is technician work involving the burner box and gas. Only #2's homeowner-level contributors (filter, registers) are DIY.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

**Only do these if the noise is clearly coming from the ductwork, plenum, or a register — and there is no gas odor.** If the bang originates at the burners, or you smell gas or burning, skip this section entirely: shut the furnace off as described above and call for help.

- **Replace the air filter.** A clogged filter raises blower static pressure and is a leading cause of duct-bang. Swap in a clean filter of the size printed on the edge of the one you remove.
- **Open all supply and return registers.** Closed or blocked vents force blower pressure up and make ducts pop. Make sure furniture or rugs aren't covering them.
- **Check the thermostat.** Set it to Heat, a few degrees above room temperature, and replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Confirm the breaker and furnace switch are on.** A tripped breaker or the wall switch beside the furnace being off can cause odd start behavior.
- **Look at visible vents/flue outside** for obvious blockage (nests, snow, debris) — from the ground only.
- **Check the condensate line** on high-efficiency models isn't clogged or overflowing.

Do **not** open the furnace cabinet, adjust gas, clean burners, or inspect the igniter yourself. Anything inside the cabinet is technician work.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will:

- **Observe a complete ignition cycle with combustion instruments already in place.** This is a technician-only step — it requires a combustion analyzer, manometers, and the training to work at an open burner compartment. Homeowners should never open the cabinet or stand at the burners to watch a startup.
- **Inspect and clean the burners and crossover ports**, checking for carbon, rust, and scale that delay ignition.
- **Test the hot-surface igniter** for proper resistance and timing.
- **Measure manifold gas pressure** and inlet pressure against Lennox spec for your model, adjusting the valve if needed.
- **Check flame sensor current** and ignition sequence timing on the control board.
- **Measure system static pressure** to confirm whether duct oil-canning is the real culprit — high static points to filter, duct, or blower issues rather than combustion.
- **Inspect the heat exchanger** for cracks, since repeated delayed ignition can damage it.

If a quote jumps straight to "replace the furnace" without a combustion analysis and static-pressure reading, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bang/whump from burner area at ignition | Delayed ignition, dirty burners | Shut furnace off at thermostat and furnace switch, leave it off, call pro | Clean burners, test igniter, check gas pressure |
| Pop from duct/plenum after blower starts, no gas odor | Oil-canning, high static pressure | Replace filter, open registers | Measure static pressure, add duct reinforcement/return |
| Bang plus faint gas smell before ignition | Gas pooling before flame lights | Leave the home, call gas utility/911 from outside | Diagnose ignition timing, valve, orifices |
| Bang gets louder or more frequent over weeks | Worsening igniter or burner fouling | Stop using, call pro | Replace igniter, clean/adjust burners |
| Only one burner lights late with a pop | Blocked flame crossover ports | Shut off, call pro | Clean crossover ports, inspect manifold |
| Strong gas odor or scorched smell anytime | Possible gas leak or flame rollout | Leave home, call gas utility/911 | Leak test and repair |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **Filter replacement / open registers:** $0–$40 DIY.
- **Diagnostic / combustion analysis visit:** $90–$200.
- **Burner cleaning and adjustment:** $150–$350.
- **Hot-surface igniter replacement:** $150–$400.
- **Gas valve replacement or manifold pressure adjustment:** $300–$700.
- **Duct modification to fix oil-canning (added return, reinforcement, damper):** $250–$900+.
- **Heat exchanger inspection:** often included in diagnostic.

**Outside the $0–$900 range quoted above:** heat exchanger **replacement** runs **$1,500–$3,500+**, which frequently makes full furnace replacement the more economical choice. Treat that as a separate scenario, not part of the typical repair range.

A true delayed-ignition bang is a safety issue — don't defer it to save on a service call.

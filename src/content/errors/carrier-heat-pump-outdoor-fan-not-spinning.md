## What this code means

"Outdoor fan not spinning" is a symptom, not a numbered fault code. The large fan on top of your Carrier heat pump stays still while the unit should be running. You might hear the compressor humming or running, or the whole outdoor unit may be silent.

Two situations look like this but are normal:

- **Defrost mode (heating season only).** The defrost board stops the fan on purpose while the compressor runs and the reversing valve shifts. This usually lasts a few minutes, often with steam rising from the coil. If the unit seems to stay in defrost, see the related page on being stuck in defrost mode.
- **Start-up and shutdown delays.** Many Carrier controls add a short time delay before the compressor and fan start. Infinity and Greenspeed variable-speed units also ramp the fan speed up and down. Brief pauses are normal.

Anything beyond those cases means the fan motor isn't getting power or can't turn. The problem doesn't clear itself. A failed capacitor or motor stays failed. The compressor's internal overload may cut out and reset on its own as it cools, so the unit can seem to work on and off.

On communicating Infinity and Greenspeed systems, the thermostat or outdoor board may log a fan-related fault. The exact code and wording vary by model, so check the installation manual for your model number.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor.** This is the most common cause on Carrier units with standard PSC fan motors. Many models use a dual capacitor that serves both the fan and the compressor. A weak capacitor often makes the fan hum without turning, or turn only after a push.
2. **Failed outdoor fan motor.** Common causes are worn bearings, a burned winding, or the motor's internal thermal overload tripping. Signs include a seized shaft, a burnt smell, or a motor that runs briefly and then stops.
3. **Contactor problem.** On single-speed units, the contactor feeds power to both the compressor and the fan. Pitted contacts or a failed coil can stop the whole outdoor unit. A burned contact on the fan's side can leave only the fan dead.
4. **Defrost control board or fan relay fault.** In heat pumps, the outdoor fan is usually routed through a relay on the defrost board. A failed relay, or a board stuck in defrost, keeps the fan off while the compressor runs.
5. **Wiring problems.** Loose, burned, or rodent-chewed wires at the motor, capacitor, contactor, or board can cut power to the fan.
6. **Physical obstruction.** Ice buildup, debris, or a bent or loose fan blade hitting the grille can jam the fan.
7. **Variable-speed motor or inverter control fault (Infinity and Greenspeed only).** The ECM fan motor, its module, or a communication fault with the outdoor control can stop the fan. Diagnosis depends on the model.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Check the thermostat.** Make sure it's set to Cool or Heat, with a setpoint that should call for the system to run. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank.
- **Rule out defrost.** In heating mode, if the fan stopped but steam is rising and it restarts within about 10 minutes, the system is working normally.
- **Check the breakers.** Look at the outdoor unit's breaker in your panel and the disconnect switch beside the unit. If a breaker has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, leave it off and call a technician.
- **Replace a dirty air filter.** A clogged filter won't stop the outdoor fan directly. It does strain the whole system, though, and can lead to icing in heating mode.
- **Look, but don't touch.** From outside the unit, check for sticks, leaves, or heavy ice on top of the grille. Clear loose debris from around the unit's base and sides only, never through the grille. Keep 2 feet of open space around the unit.
- **Confirm the top grille and service panels are seated.** Do not open the unit.
- **Shut the system off if the fan stays stopped while the compressor runs.** This protects the compressor. In winter, switch to emergency heat.

Don't push the blade, and don't touch the capacitor. Even with the power off, a capacitor can hold a dangerous charge.

## How a technician will diagnose it

1. **Confirm the call for operation.** The tech checks the thermostat and control voltage (24V) at the outdoor unit. On communicating systems, they review the fault history.
2. **Watch the unit operate.** They note whether the fan hums, stays silent, or tries to start, and whether the compressor is running.
3. **Kill power, then discharge and test the capacitor** with a capacitance meter. They compare the reading to the microfarad (µF) rating printed on the label, typically within ±6%.
4. **Check the motor.** They spin the shaft by hand to feel for seized bearings, test the windings for resistance and shorts to ground, and look for a tripped thermal overload.
5. **Inspect the contactor** for pitting, check the coil, and confirm voltage across the load side.
6. **Test the defrost board.** They check the fan relay output and whether the board is calling for defrost, using the board's test pins where the model provides them.
7. **Trace the wiring** for burns, loose connections, or rodent damage.
8. **On ECM or inverter models,** they check the motor's high-voltage and communication inputs and follow Carrier's service manual for that model.
9. **After the repair,** they verify amp draw and refrigerant pressures. They may also check whether the compressor overheated while the fan was down.

A good quote names the specific failed part and says how it was tested. Be wary of a quote that jumps straight to a new compressor or a whole new system without these steps.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fan hums but doesn't turn; compressor runs | Weak or failed run capacitor | Turn the system off; don't push the blade | Test and replace the capacitor |
| Fan runs only after a push | Failed capacitor or failing motor | Turn the system off | Test the capacitor and motor; replace the failed part |
| Fan silent, compressor running, heating mode, steam visible | Normal defrost | Wait about 10 minutes | None, unless defrost never ends |
| Fan silent, compressor running, no defrost | Failed motor, fan relay on the defrost board, or wiring | Turn the system off; use emergency heat in winter | Test the motor, board relay, and wiring; replace the failed part |
| Entire outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, disconnect off, contactor, or control voltage | Check the breaker and disconnect; reset once | Diagnose the contactor, transformer, and controls |
| Fan starts, then stops after a few minutes | Motor overheating on its thermal overload; bad bearings | Turn the system off | Replace the fan motor |
| Grinding or scraping, blade won't turn | Ice, debris, or a bent blade | Clear debris around the outside of the unit only | Remove the obstruction, straighten or replace the blade, check the motor |
| Communicating thermostat shows an outdoor fault | ECM motor or inverter control fault | Note the code; cycle power once | Run model-specific ECM or inverter diagnostics |

## Repair costs

Typical installed US prices, including labor:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic or service call | $90 – $200 |
| Run capacitor (single or dual) | $120 – $350 |
| Contactor | $150 – $350 |
| PSC outdoor fan motor | $350 – $750 |
| Fan blade | $150 – $350 |
| Defrost control board | $300 – $650 |
| ECM or variable-speed fan motor (Infinity, Greenspeed) | $700 – $1,200+ |
| Wiring repair (including rodent damage) | $100 – $400 |

Carrier's parts warranty, often 10 years on registered units, may cover the part itself, but you'll still pay for labor. Running the unit with a dead fan can damage the compressor, which can turn a $200 repair into a $2,000+ one.

## What a CO alarm means

**If your CO alarm is going off, do this first:**

1. Get everyone, including pets, outside into fresh air right now.
2. Call 911 or your gas utility's emergency line from outside.
3. If you can do it on the way out without delay, turn the furnace off at the thermostat or the service switch. Do not stay inside to troubleshoot.
4. Anyone with headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion or drowsiness needs medical care immediately. Tell responders it may be carbon monoxide.
5. Do not go back inside until responders say the air is safe.

A CO alarm is not a Goodman error code. It means the alarm detected carbon monoxide in the home.

A gas furnace is one of the most common sources of CO. It can produce CO in two ways:
- It burns fuel incompletely and produces excess CO.
- Its exhaust escapes into the living space instead of going outside.

Goodman furnaces have no CO sensor. The control board cannot detect or report CO.

A blocked vent may cause a pressure switch fault, such as 3 flashes. It may also trip a rollout switch. Rollout switches typically require a manual reset that only a technician should perform. How a rollout trip shows up varies by control board. Some boards report it under a different flash code than others, and the same flash count can mean different faults on different boards. Check the code label on your furnace door to see what a flash count means on your unit.

A cracked heat exchanger or backdrafting chimney can leak CO while the furnace runs normally with no code.

The furnace must stay off until it is professionally inspected.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

Goodman does not publish a CO-specific troubleshooting order. The ranking below reflects how often each cause turns up in field service.

1. **Blocked or disconnected venting.** On 80% models, this includes a blocked chimney, a bird nest, a liner failure, or a separated flue pipe joint. On 90%+ models, this includes a PVC exhaust or intake blocked by snow, ice, leaves or nests, or a pipe separated inside the house.
2. **Backdrafting on 80% furnaces.** Exhaust spills from the draft area into the home. Causes include an undersized or cold chimney, a shared flue issue after water heater changes, or strong exhaust fans depressurizing the house.
3. **Poor combustion.** Typical problems are dirty or misaligned burners, incorrect gas pressure, wrong orifices (for example after a propane or natural gas conversion), or insufficient combustion air in a closed-off closet.
4. **Cracked or failed heat exchanger.** This lets flue gas mix with circulating air or disturbs the flame. It is often linked to age, overheating from restricted airflow, or short cycling.
5. **Inducer motor or pressure switch problems** that allow poor draft, such as a weak inducer or a cracked pressure switch hose.
6. **Not the furnace at all.** Other sources include a gas water heater, range or oven, fireplace, attached garage, or generator. A failing or expired alarm is also possible.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

Do these only **after** emergency responders have cleared the home. Leave the furnace off.

- **Check the alarm itself.** Look at the manufacture date. Most alarms should be replaced 7 to 10 years after that date. Some alarms give an end-of-life chirp that differs from a CO alarm, so check the label or manual. Replace the batteries. Do not silence or remove the alarm.
- **Look at the outdoor vents (90%+ models).** Check the PVC intake and exhaust terminations outside. You may clear snow, ice or leaves you can reach from the ground by hand. Do not take apart or reconnect pipes.
- **Look for visibly separated flue pipe (80% models).** Do not touch or repair it. Note what you see for the technician.
- **Check the air filter.** A clogged filter overheats the heat exchanger over time. Replace it if it is dirty.
- **Check the furnace door.** Confirm the burner and blower doors are fully seated. Do not open the cabinet.
- **Do not restart the furnace** to see whether the alarm repeats. Wait for a technician's inspection.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A thorough technician will:

- Measure ambient CO in the home with a calibrated meter.
- Test flue gas with a combustion analyzer, checking CO (air-free), O2 and draft.
- Check venting from end to end:
  - Pipe slope and joints
  - Chimney and liner condition
  - PVC terminations
  - Condensate trap
- Run a worst-case depressurization or spillage test on 80% furnaces and shared flues, with exhaust fans running.
- Inspect and clean the burners.
- Verify manifold gas pressure and orifice sizing against the rating plate.
- Check inducer performance and the pressure switch hose.
- Inspect the heat exchanger visually or with a camera. They will also watch flame behavior when the blower starts.
- Measure temperature rise against the rating plate.
- Rule out other appliances.

Be skeptical of a heat exchanger diagnosis that comes with no photos, camera footage or combustion readings. Ask for the numbers. Getting a second opinion before replacing a furnace is reasonable.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alarm sounds, people feel ill | Any CO source | Evacuate, call 911 | Full combustion and venting inspection |
| Alarm after snowstorm, 90%+ model | Blocked PVC termination | After clearance, clear snow from vents by hand | Verify venting and combustion before restart |
| Alarm plus 3 flashes on board | Blocked vent or draft problem | Leave off. Confirm the flash count against the code label on your furnace door, since counts are easy to misread. Check outdoor vents | Clear or repair venting, test pressure switch and inducer |
| Soot or rust near draft hood (80% model) | Backdrafting or blocked chimney | Leave off | Chimney or liner inspection, spillage test |
| Yellow, lazy or lifting flames | Poor combustion or cracked heat exchanger | Leave off | Combustion analysis, burner service, heat exchanger inspection |
| Alarm only when blower runs | Possible heat exchanger breach | Leave off | Camera inspection, heat exchanger or furnace replacement |
| Alarm chirps, no CO reading | Low battery or end-of-life alarm | Replace batteries or alarm | None if responders confirmed no CO |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Emergency fire or utility response | Usually $0 |
| New CO alarm (DIY) | $25–$60 |
| Combustion and safety inspection | $100–$300 |
| Clearing a blocked vent or termination | $100–$300 |
| Repairing or re-sealing flue pipe | $150–$600 |
| Chimney liner (usually installed by a chimney contractor, not an HVAC technician) | $1,000–$3,500 |
| Burner cleaning and gas pressure adjustment | $150–$400 |
| Inducer motor | $400–$1,000 |
| Pressure switch or hose | $150–$350 |
| Heat exchanger replacement | $1,500–$3,000 with labor, often part-covered by warranty |
| Full furnace replacement | $3,000–$7,000+ |

## What this code means

"Compressor not running" is a symptom, not a single numbered fault code. You'll typically notice one of these:

- The outdoor unit is silent.
- The outdoor fan spins but the compressor doesn't hum.
- The compressor hums or clicks but never starts.

Indoors, the blower usually still moves air, but the air is warm.

How your system reports the problem depends on the model:

- **Standard single-stage and two-stage Trane condensers (XR and many XL models)** use a simple contactor and capacitor. They have no fault display, so you diagnose by what you see and hear.
- **Communicating XV-series and Link systems** may show a compressor or pressure fault on the thermostat. They can hold a lockout after repeated trips.

The compressor's internal overload resets automatically once the compressor cools. A control-board lockout on a communicating unit may need a power cycle. Check your model's documentation.

If the fan runs but you only get warm air, also see our "fan runs but no cold air" page. This page focuses on the compressor itself failing to start or run.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Failed run capacitor (dual capacitor).** This is the most common no-start cause. The compressor hums, then trips on overload. Often the fan also slows or stops.
2. **Bad or pitted contactor.** The 24-volt call arrives, but worn contacts don't pass power to the compressor.
3. **Compressor internal overload tripped.** It trips from overheating, which is often caused by a dirty condenser coil, low refrigerant, or a failed fan. It resets after cooling, sometimes only after an hour or more.
4. **No 24-volt call reaching the outdoor unit.** Possible reasons include a thermostat setting, a broken low-voltage wire, a blown control fuse, or an open pressure switch on equipped models.
5. **High- or low-pressure switch open or control lockout.** This applies to models with pressure switches or communicating controls. The switch opens because of the underlying problem, such as low charge, a blocked coil, or a restriction.
6. **Power loss to the condenser.** This means a tripped breaker or a pulled or blown outdoor disconnect.
7. **Seized, grounded, or open-winding compressor.** This is the least common cause and the most expensive. It is confirmed only by electrical testing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Thermostat.** Set it to Cool and lower the setpoint at least 3°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is blank or dim. Some thermostats have a built-in 5-minute compressor delay, so wait it out.
2. **Air filter.** Replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter can lead to icing and pressure trips.
3. **Breakers.** Check both the indoor and the outdoor AC breaker. If one has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and see our breaker-tripping page.
4. **Outdoor disconnect.** Make sure the pull-out block or switch near the condenser is fully seated and in the ON position. Don't open the box beyond that.
5. **One reset.** Turn the thermostat off and switch off the breaker for 5 minutes. Then restore power and call for cooling again. If the compressor still won't run, or it runs briefly and quits, don't keep resetting it. Repeated restarts can damage the compressor.
6. **Clear around the unit.** Remove leaves, grass clippings, or covers from the outside of the condenser. Keep 2 feet of clearance. Don't open the cabinet or spray inside it while it's powered.
7. **Condensate.** If your indoor unit has a float or condensate safety switch and the drain pan is full, clear the visible drain line. A tripped float switch can cut the cooling call.
8. **Check the vents.** Make sure the supply and return registers are open and unblocked.

Shut the system off if you hear the compressor humming without starting, or the unit trips again after your one reset. Then call a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will typically go through these steps:

- **Confirm 24 volts at the contactor coil** to check that the call from the thermostat is reaching the outdoor unit.
- **Measure line voltage** at the disconnect and across the contactor, and inspect the contacts for pitting.
- **Discharge and test the capacitor** with a meter that measures µF. A reading outside about ±6% of the rating means the capacitor fails the test.
- **Measure compressor winding resistance** (C–S, C–R, S–R) and test for a ground. This tells apart an open overload, which resets once cooled, from failed windings.
- **Check amp draw on startup.** Locked-rotor amps that never drop point to a stuck compressor. The tech may try a hard-start kit.
- **Check pressures and the condenser coil and fan** to find out why an overload or pressure switch tripped.
- **On communicating XV/Link systems, pull the stored fault history** from the thermostat or service tool.

Before you approve a compressor replacement, ask for the winding and ground readings. Also ask whether the unit is under Trane's limited warranty. Many Trane compressors carry a 10–12 year registered warranty.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor unit completely silent | Breaker, disconnect, thermostat, or no 24-volt call | Check the thermostat and batteries, reset the breaker once, seat the disconnect | Trace the low-voltage circuit, check the control fuse and contactor coil |
| Fan runs, compressor silent | Failed capacitor (compressor side) or tripped overload | Shut off; let the unit cool for an hour, then try once | Test and replace the capacitor; find the overheating cause |
| Compressor hums, clicks off | Weak capacitor or stuck compressor | Shut the system off | Replace the capacitor; try a hard-start kit; test the windings |
| Runs a few minutes, then stops | Overload or pressure trip from a dirty coil, low charge, or a fan problem | Replace the filter; clear debris from outside the unit | Clean the coil, check the charge, find the leak, check the fan motor |
| Thermostat shows a compressor fault (XV/Link) | Pressure switch or control lockout | Do one power cycle | Read the fault history; repair the root cause |
| Contactor buzzing, nothing starts | Pitted contactor | None | Replace the contactor |
| Breaker trips when the compressor tries to start | Grounded compressor or shorted wiring | Don't reset again | Ground and winding tests; repair the wiring or replace the compressor |

## Repair costs

These are typical US costs, including the service call:

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnosis | $80 – $200 |
| Run (dual) capacitor | $150 – $350 |
| Contactor | $150 – $350 |
| Hard-start kit | $200 – $450 |
| Low-voltage wire repair or control fuse | $100 – $300 |
| Pressure switch | $200 – $500 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100 – $250 |
| Leak repair plus refrigerant recharge | $400 – $1,500+ (R-410A/R-454B prices vary) |
| Compressor replacement, under parts warranty (labor and refrigerant) | $1,200 – $2,500 |
| Compressor replacement, out of warranty | $1,800 – $3,500+ |
| Full condenser replacement (often better on units over 12–15 years old) | $4,000 – $8,000+ |

## What this code means

**If your CO alarm is sounding right now, do this first:**

1. **Get everyone out of the house now,** pets included. Don't stop to find the source.
2. **Turn the furnace off at the thermostat on your way out, but only if it is right beside your exit.** Don't go down to the basement or furnace closet to do it.
3. **Call 911 or your local fire department from outside.** If you also smell gas, tell the dispatcher, and call your gas utility's emergency line too. Our "Carrier Furnace Gas Smell" page covers gas leaks.
4. **Get medical help** for anyone with a headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion or drowsiness.
5. **Stay outside** until responders have checked CO levels and told you the home is safe.

Opening windows on the way out is fine, but it does not make it safe to stay inside.

A sounding CO alarm is not a Carrier error code. It means your detector has measured dangerous carbon monoxide in the air. Carrier furnace control boards have no CO sensor, so the furnace may show a normal status light while it produces CO.

A gas furnace makes carbon monoxide as a normal by-product of burning fuel. The heat exchanger and vent system are supposed to keep those gases sealed off and carry them outside. An alarm near the furnace means one of three things:

- Combustion gases are escaping into the house.
- The burners are producing far more CO than normal.
- Another source, such as a water heater, range, car or generator, is responsible.

There is nothing to auto-reset. The alarm may go quiet once the air clears, but that does not mean the problem is fixed. The furnace should stay off until a technician clears it.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

Carrier does not publish a CO-specific troubleshooting sequence. This ranking reflects what fire departments and HVAC technicians most often find.

1. **A source other than the furnace.** Common culprits are a gas water heater sharing a flue, a gas range or oven, a car warming up in an attached garage, or a generator near a window. Fire departments trace many calls to these.
2. **Blocked vent termination on condensing (90%+) models.** Snow, ice, leaves, nests or insects can block the PVC intake or exhaust pipe outside. A blockage usually trips the pressure switch first, but a partial blockage or a sagging or disconnected pipe can let exhaust recirculate.
3. **Disconnected, cracked or poorly sloped vent pipe.** This includes separated PVC joints, loose metal flue sections, or a vent connector pulled away from the chimney.
4. **Backdrafting on 80% models with metal flues.** Causes include a blocked or deteriorated chimney, an unlined masonry chimney, or strong negative pressure in the house. Exhaust fans, dryers and a tight house can all pull flue gases back down the chimney.
5. **Poor combustion.** Dirty or misaligned burners, wrong gas pressure (overfiring), or not enough combustion air can all raise CO. A furnace closet with no air openings is a common example.
6. **Cracked or failed heat exchanger.** This is less common than the causes above, but it is the most serious and often ends with furnace replacement. It should be confirmed by camera inspection or combustion testing.
7. **A faulty or expired alarm.** Most CO alarms last 5–10 years. Only consider this after responders have measured the air and found no CO.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

**Do these only after the fire department has cleared the home.** Never do them while an alarm is sounding.

- **Keep the furnace off** at the thermostat or furnace service switch until a technician has inspected it. Do not reset or restart it to see if the alarm returns.
- **Look at the outdoor vent pipes** on condensing models from outside. If snow, ice, leaves or a nest is visibly blocking them, note it and tell the technician. You can gently clear loose snow by hand from the outside. Do not cut, reconnect or modify the pipes.
- **Check the alarm itself.** Note its manufacture date on the back and the beep pattern you heard. Replace an alarm older than its rated life or showing an end-of-life signal. Fit fresh batteries in battery models.
- **Add alarms if needed.** You should have at least one CO alarm on every level and outside each sleeping area.
- **Write down what was running** when the alarm sounded, such as a dryer, exhaust fans, fireplace, water heater or car. This helps the technician pin down backdrafting.
- **Check the furnace filter and visible supply registers.** A clogged filter does not create CO, but it is worth replacing before the technician visits.

Do not remove furnace panels, inspect the burners or heat exchanger, or touch the gas valve.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified technician should do most of the following. A quote that skips testing and goes straight to "you need a new furnace" deserves a second opinion.

- **Ambient CO readings** throughout the house and near each gas appliance, to rule other sources in or out.
- **Combustion analysis** with a calibrated analyzer in the flue. This measures CO, oxygen and efficiency against Carrier's installation specifications.
- **Draft and spillage testing** on 80% models. The technician runs the furnace and water heater together with exhaust fans and the dryer on, a worst-case depressurization test.
- **Full vent inspection**, covering pipe slope, joints, supports, termination clearances and blockages. A chimney inspection or camera check is done where needed.
- **Burner and gas-pressure check.** The technician measures manifold pressure and compares it with the rating plate, and checks the flame pattern and orifices.
- **Heat exchanger inspection** by camera scope and visual check. They may also watch whether the flame changes when the blower starts, which suggests a breach. Ask to see photos or video of any crack.
- **Combustion air check** to confirm the furnace space meets the air-opening requirements for its installation.

Many gas utilities and fire departments will also "red tag" the furnace, which means shutting off its gas until it is repaired. Only a licensed technician or the utility should restore it.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alarm sounds and people feel ill | Active CO leak, source unknown | Evacuate, call 911, get medical care | Locate source, repair, test before restart |
| Alarm during or after snowstorm, condensing furnace | Blocked PVC intake or exhaust | After clearance, clear visible snow outside, keep furnace off | Inspect vent, repair, combustion test |
| Alarm when dryer or exhaust fans run, 80% furnace | Backdrafting or negative pressure | Note which appliances were on | Draft and spillage test, add makeup air, repair chimney or liner |
| Soot or rust near the furnace vent or draft hood | Spillage or poor combustion | Keep furnace off, take photos | Clean and adjust burners, check gas pressure, inspect vent |
| CO level rises after the blower starts | Possible cracked heat exchanger | Keep furnace off | Camera inspection, confirm crack, replace exchanger or furnace |
| Alarm only with car running in the garage | Vehicle exhaust | Never idle vehicles in the garage | Not needed if testing clears the furnace |
| Single chirp every 30–60 seconds | Low battery or end-of-life alarm | Replace batteries or the alarm | None |

## Repair costs

Typical US ranges. Prices vary by region, access and model.

- **Fire department CO response:** usually free.
- **Technician CO and combustion safety inspection:** $100 – $300.
- **New CO alarm:** $25 – $60 each, DIY.
- **Clearing a blocked vent termination:** $150 – $350.
- **Repairing or replacing PVC or metal vent sections:** $200 – $900.
- **Burner cleaning, gas pressure adjustment, tune-up:** $120 – $350.
- **Adding combustion or makeup air:** $200 – $1,000.
- **Chimney liner for an 80% furnace:** $1,500 – $4,000.
- **Heat exchanger replacement:** $1,500 – $4,000 installed. The part is often covered by Carrier's limited heat exchanger warranty if the furnace was registered. Labor is usually extra.
- **Furnace replacement:** $4,000 – $10,000+, when the exchanger is not covered or the unit is older.

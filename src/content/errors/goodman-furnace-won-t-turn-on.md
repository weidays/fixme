## What this code means

"Won't turn on" isn't a flash-code — it's the situation where your Goodman furnace shows **no response at all** to a call for heat: no blower, no inducer hum, no igniter glow, and often no LED activity on the control board. This is different from a furnace that tries to start and then locks out (which produces a diagnostic flash code you can read through the sight glass).

A true no-start almost always points to a **break in power or the control circuit** before ignition even begins. Because most causes are inside the cabinet — fuses, door switches, the transformer, or the board itself — this is rated **pro-level**. A homeowner can safely rule out a handful of external causes, but the interior work belongs to a licensed technician.

If your furnace *does* light briefly and then quits, or you *do* see a blinking LED, read the matching flash-code page instead (see Related codes).

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Tripped breaker or furnace disconnect switch off.** The furnace has both a breaker at the panel and a wall switch (often looks like a light switch) near the unit. Either being off kills all power.
2. **Dead thermostat or drained batteries.** A blank or unresponsive thermostat never sends the call for heat. Battery-powered models simply go dark when cells die.
3. **Blown low-voltage fuse on the control board.** A short in the thermostat wiring or an accessory commonly pops the small automotive-style fuse on the board (the amperage is printed next to the fuse holder), cutting the 24V control circuit while the board itself may look dead.
4. **Open blower-door safety switch.** Goodman furnaces have a door interlock switch that cuts power when the blower panel is off or seated poorly. A loose or misaligned panel leaves the furnace completely dead.
5. **Failed 24V transformer.** If the transformer that steps 120V down to 24V fails, the control board and thermostat lose power even though line voltage reaches the furnace.
6. **Failed integrated control board.** A board with corroded traces, failed relays, or storm/surge damage may not respond to a heat call at all.
7. **Loss of line voltage.** A tripped upstream device or a bad splice — and in some installations a GFCI — can leave the furnace with no incoming 120V.
8. **Failed blower motor.** Least likely for a *total* no-start, since a dead motor usually leaves you with a unit that shows signs of life but never delivers heat. It's listed here because it's the most expensive of the common no-heat repairs, so a technician should confirm the motor is actually the fault before quoting one.

Note: On two-stage models (GMVC/GMEC) the board and its diagnostics differ slightly from single-stage GMES/GCES units, but a total no-start behaves the same way across the lineup.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should perform. If none of them restores power, stop and call a technician.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **HEAT** and the setpoint is several degrees above room temperature. If the screen is blank or faint, **replace the batteries** with fresh ones.
- **Furnace switch:** Find the switch on or near the furnace (it looks like a standard light switch) and make sure it's **ON**.
- **Breaker:** At your electrical panel, check the furnace breaker. If it's tripped, flip it fully **OFF then ON** once.
- **One reset:** Cycle the furnace switch or breaker **off for 30 seconds, then back on — once only.** Do not repeat resets if it stays dead.
- **Blower panel:** Make sure the lower/blower access panel is **fully seated**. A panel that isn't snugly in place keeps the door interlock switch open and the furnace off. Seat it flush by hand — do **not** open the cabinet or touch the switch itself.
- **Air filter:** Replace a visibly clogged filter. Unlikely to cause a full no-start, but free to rule out.
- **Vents/registers:** Confirm supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate line (high-efficiency models):** A full condensate pan can trigger a safety float switch that cuts operation. If your unit drains to a floor drain or pump, look for standing water around the unit or an overflowing pump reservoir — but leave any wiring or switch inside the cabinet to a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A tech follows the power path from the wall to the board, so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirms 120V** reaches the furnace at the disconnect switch and terminal.
2. **Checks the low-voltage fuse** on the control board with a meter and inspects thermostat wiring for shorts before replacing it — a fuse that blows again means the real fault is still there.
3. **Tests the 24V transformer** output. No 24V means a dead transformer or an upstream open.
4. **Verifies the door interlock switch** closes properly when the panel is on, and checks its wiring.
5. **Inspects the control board** for burnt relays, corrosion, or surge damage, and confirms it energizes on a heat call.
6. **Checks the thermostat and its wiring** end to end, since an open or shorted wire can mimic a board failure.
7. **Tests the blower motor** and its connections if the board is calling correctly but the motor never runs.

A good technician replaces the **cheapest, most-likely** part first (fuse, door switch) and only condemns the board or the blower motor after confirming everything upstream is good.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Totally dead, breaker tripped | Tripped breaker | Reset breaker once | Find cause of the trip; test for shorts |
| Blank thermostat screen | Dead batteries or lost power | Replace batteries | Trace 24V loss, test transformer |
| Furnace dead, panel loose | Open door interlock switch | Seat blower panel firmly | Test/replace door switch and wiring |
| No power, panel seated, breaker on | Blown board fuse or dead transformer | None — call pro | Test fuse & transformer, find the short |
| Board unresponsive on heat call | Failed control board | None — call pro | Confirm & replace integrated board |
| Standing water at unit (HE models) | Full condensate / float switch | Look only at the visible drain line and pump reservoir for standing water | Service float switch & drain |
| Signs of life but blower never runs | Failed blower motor | None — call pro | Test motor, capacitor and board output |
| Thermostat on, nothing happens | Broken thermostat or wiring | Replace batteries; verify HEAT mode | Test/replace thermostat & wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts plus labor:

- **Breaker reset / battery swap:** **$0** DIY.
- **New thermostat batteries:** **$5–$15** DIY.
- **Air filter:** **$10–$40** DIY.
- **Thermostat replacement:** **$150–$400** installed (basic to smart).
- **Low-voltage fuse replacement:** **$75–$175** (part is cheap; the value is finding the short).
- **Door interlock switch:** **$100–$250**.
- **24V transformer:** **$150–$350**.
- **Integrated control board:** **$400–$700**, depending on model and whether it's a single- or two-stage board.
- **Blower motor:** **$450–$1,800** installed. ECM and variable-speed motors on GMVC/GMEC units routinely land above $1,000.
- **Diagnostic/service call:** **$90–$180**, often credited toward the repair.

Prices vary by region and by whether your Goodman is still under its parts warranty — registered units often carry a 10-year parts warranty, so ask the tech to check before you pay for a board or a motor.

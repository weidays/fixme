## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code — American Standard air conditioners don't display a numeric error like a furnace does. Instead, your system is running (or trying to run) but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

On systems with an American Standard **AccuLink communicating control** (Platinum series thermostats paired with communicating equipment), you may see a text alert or fault flagged at the thermostat. On older or non-communicating **Gold and Silver series** units, there's no display — you diagnose by symptom and by reading the outdoor unit's behavior. Because this covers many failure points, some checks are safe DIY and others require a licensed HVAC technician with gauges and refrigerant certification.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — the single most common cause. A clogged filter starves the system of airflow, causing weak cooling and eventually a frozen coil.
2. **Dirty condenser or evaporator coil** — restricts heat transfer so the system runs constantly but can't cool.
3. **Thermostat setting or wiring issue** — set to "fan on" instead of "auto," wrong mode, dead batteries, or a communication fault on AccuLink systems.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs, so you get warm air.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start. Very common on units 8+ years old.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from restricted airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks airflow entirely.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system runs long, cools poorly, and may ice up. Requires leak repair, not just a recharge.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the condenser never energizes.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — compressor runs hot and trips out because heat isn't being rejected.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit won't produce cooling at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here and call a pro for anything beyond this list.

- **Check the air filter.** Pull it out and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to **Cool** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Set the fan to **Auto**, not On. Replace batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Check the breaker.** Look for a tripped breaker at your main panel — the AC typically uses a double-pole breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a technician; do not keep resetting it.
- **Check the outdoor disconnect.** There's a pull-out switch near the outdoor unit; make sure it's fully seated.
- **Check your vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return vents aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Check the condensate drain.** If the indoor unit has a float switch and the drain is clogged, it can shut down cooling. Look for standing water in the drain pan and pour a cup of the correct drain cleaner if you can safely reach it.
- **Look for ice** on the copper refrigerant line or indoor coil. If you see ice, turn the system to **Off** and set the fan to **On** for a few hours to thaw, then run again — but recurring ice means you need a pro.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician follows a logical sequence, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint and check thermostat/control** — including reading any AccuLink fault history on communicating systems.
2. **Confirm airflow** — inspect filter, blower operation, and both coils.
3. **Check power and electrical** — measure voltage at the disconnect and contactor, test the capacitor with a meter, and inspect the contactor for pitting.
4. **Measure refrigerant charge** — connect gauges, check superheat and subcooling. Low readings point to a leak, so a proper diagnosis includes a **leak search** (electronic detector or dye), not just adding refrigerant.
5. **Test motors and compressor** — measure amp draw on the fan and compressor; check compressor windings if it won't start.
6. **Confirm the fix and re-measure** performance after repair.

Expect a diagnostic fee of **$90–$180**, often credited toward the repair. Be cautious of any tech who wants to "just add a couple pounds of refrigerant" without finding the leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower, ductwork |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker / disconnect | Reset once, check disconnect | Trace electrical fault, test contactor |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines or coil | Restricted airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter, thaw with fan On | Check charge, find leak, clean coil |
| Runs constantly, cools poorly | Dirty coils or low charge | Rinse outdoor coil gently, check filter | Measure charge, clean coils, leak search |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed condenser fan motor | Turn system off to avoid overheating | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| No cooling at all, breaker trips | Compressor or short | Stop resetting breaker | Test compressor windings, diagnose fault |
| Standing water, unit shut off | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, use tablets | Clear line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Diagnostic fee:** $90–$180
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$350
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $100–$250
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$450
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$1,800+ (often worth weighing against a full unit replacement on older systems)

Costs vary by region, unit age, and whether your system is still under American Standard's parts warranty — check your registration, as many carry a 10-year limited parts warranty.

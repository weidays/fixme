## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a numeric fault code — it's the symptom homeowners describe when an American Standard air conditioner runs but the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents feels warm or only slightly cool.

American Standard split systems and heat pumps use a matched outdoor condenser and indoor coil to move heat out of your home. Cooling fails when any part of that loop breaks down: airflow is restricted, the refrigerant charge is low, an electrical component won't start the compressor or fan, or the coil has frozen.

Higher-end units with a communicating control (AccuLink / AccuComfort variable-speed systems) may store an actual fault in the thermostat's diagnostic menu. Single-stage systems typically give no code at all — you diagnose by symptom. Check your thermostat's service or fault screen before assuming there's no stored information.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — blocked fins can't reject heat, so the system runs but barely cools.
4. **Low refrigerant charge / leak** — the most common "runs but doesn't cool" cause on older units. Requires an EPA-certified tech.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or outdoor fan hums or won't start. Common and relatively affordable.
6. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or miswiring.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power, only the indoor blower runs.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit won't close.
9. **Clogged condensate drain float switch** — a full pan trips a safety switch that stops cooling.
10. **Failed compressor** — least common but most expensive; the unit hums, trips the breaker, or does nothing.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for any homeowner:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker** — check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting it.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain** — if you see standing water in the pan or near the indoor unit, the float switch may have shut cooling off. Clearing a visible clog can restore operation.
- **Frozen coil** — if you see ice on the indoor coil or the refrigerant lines, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw for a few hours before running it again.

Do not open the sealed refrigerant system, remove electrical panel covers on live components, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified American Standard tech will typically:

1. Verify the thermostat call and check for stored faults on communicating (AccuLink) systems.
2. Confirm 24V control power and that the contactor pulls in at the outdoor unit.
3. Measure capacitor microfarad values against the rating printed on the cap.
4. Check compressor and fan motor amp draw against nameplate specs.
5. Attach gauges to read suction/liquid pressures and calculate superheat and subcooling to judge the refrigerant charge.
6. Inspect the coils for restriction and the drain system for blockage.
7. Perform a leak search (electronic detector or dye) if the charge is low.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, ask why — adding refrigerant to a leaking system is a temporary fix, not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, dirty filter | Clogged filter restricting airflow | Replace filter | None needed if resolved |
| Ice on coil or lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor unit runs, house stays warm | Dirty condenser or low charge | Rinse fins gently, clear debris | Coil clean, charge/leak check |
| Outdoor fan or compressor hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Only indoor blower runs | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, trace fault |
| Water near indoor unit, no cooling | Clogged drain, tripped float switch | Clear visible clog, add tablets | Clear drain line, test switch |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Compressor or wiring fault | Stop resetting, call pro | Diagnose short/compressor |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries or control fault | Replace batteries | Check control wiring/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10 – $30 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 – $10 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $0 DIY – $150 professional
- **Run/dual capacitor replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300 – $650
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $150 – $400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500 – $1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900 – $2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200 – $1,800+ (often worth comparing to full replacement on older units)

Diagnostic/service call fees typically run $75 – $200 and are often credited toward the repair.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code. American Standard air conditioners don't display a single "not cooling" error — instead you notice warm air from the vents, a house that won't reach setpoint, or an outdoor unit that runs constantly without dropping the temperature.

On communicating **AccuLink** systems, the thermostat may log a specific fault (such as a compressor lockout or communication error) that you can read in the service menu. On older non-communicating units, there's no display — you diagnose by observing what the indoor and outdoor equipment are actually doing.

Because "not cooling" can stem from anything from a dirty filter to a dead compressor, this page ranks the likely causes and shows you which ones are safe to check yourself and which require a licensed pro.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, causes the indoor coil to ice over, and kills cooling capacity. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat setting or wiring issue** — set to "fan" instead of "cool," wrong programming, or dead batteries on a battery-powered stat.
3. **Tripped breaker to the outdoor unit** — a separate breaker feeds the condenser; if it's off, the indoor fan blows uncooled air.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — extremely common failure. A weak or dead capacitor stops the compressor and/or condenser fan from starting.
5. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with dirt, grass, or cottonwood, so the system can't reject heat.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit can pit or stick, cutting power to the compressor.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces capacity; the coil may freeze. Always caused by a leak, never by "using it up."
8. **Frozen indoor evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer entirely.
9. **Clogged condensate drain / float switch tripped** — a safety float can shut the system down when the drain backs up.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the outdoor unit hums, trips the breaker, or won't run at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here if anything requires opening the electrical panel of the unit.

- **Air filter** — Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat** — Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breakers** — Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers** — Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside fins with a garden hose (power off) if they look dirty.
- **Condensate drain** — If you see standing water in the drain pan or near the indoor unit, the drain may be clogged and a float switch may have shut the system down.
- **Check for ice** — If you see frost or ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and run the fan on **ON** for a few hours to thaw it before calling anyone.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will typically:

1. Verify the thermostat is calling for cooling and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor unit.
2. Measure the **run capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads — a weak cap is one of the fastest finds.
3. Check the **contactor** for pitting and confirm it pulls in when energized.
4. Inspect the **condenser coil and indoor coil** for dirt and ice.
5. Connect refrigerant gauges to read pressures and calculate **superheat and subcooling** — this tells them if the charge is correct without automatically adding refrigerant.
6. If charge is low, they'll perform a **leak search** (dye, electronic detector, or nitrogen pressure test) before recharging.
7. On AccuLink systems, they'll read stored fault codes and check communication between the thermostat, air handler, and condenser.
8. Test the **compressor** windings and amp draw if it won't start or trips the breaker.

Sanity check: a good tech diagnoses *why* refrigerant is low before adding any. Anyone who just "tops off the Freon" without a leak search is masking the problem.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, filter dark/clogged | Dirty air filter | Replace filter | Check for coil icing from restricted airflow |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker / bad contactor / capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test capacitor, contactor, 24V signal |
| Outdoor fan spins, compressor hums then stops | Failed run capacitor | None | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Leak search, recharge, or fix airflow |
| House won't reach setpoint, long runtimes | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Rinse outdoor coil | Measure pressures, clean coil |
| System off, water in drain pan | Clogged condensate drain, float tripped | Clear visible water | Clear drain line, test float switch |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted compressor or wiring fault | Stop resetting | Test compressor windings, wiring |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or blown low-voltage fuse | Replace batteries | Replace 24V fuse, find the short |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and unit access):

- **Air filter** — $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries** — $5 (DIY)
- **Run capacitor replacement** — $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement** — $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning** — $100–$250
- **Condensate drain clearing** — $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge** — $600–$1,500 (higher if the coil must be replaced)
- **Evaporator coil replacement** — $900–$2,000+
- **Compressor replacement** — $2,000–$2,800+ (often near the cost of a new outdoor unit)

If your unit is 12+ years old and the compressor fails, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison — replacing the whole system is often the better value.

## Related codes

- **Compressor lockout / high-pressure trip** — check for a dirty condenser or overcharge
- **Low-pressure lockout** — usually a refrigerant leak or restricted airflow
- **AccuLink communication error** — thermostat can't talk to the outdoor unit
- **Frozen coil / ice fault** — low airflow or low charge
- **Condensate overflow / float switch open** — clogged drain shutting the system down

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific error code on American Standard air conditioners — it's a symptom. Your system is either running without producing cold air, short-cycling, or failing to start the outdoor unit entirely.

On older single-stage American Standard units (many Silver and Gold models), there's no fault display at all — you diagnose by observing behavior. On newer variable-speed AccuComfort systems paired with a communicating control (like the AccuLink platform), the thermostat may display an actual fault or alert code that points to the real cause. If your control shows a numbered code, note it — that narrows the problem considerably.

In every case, "not cooling" means heat is not being removed from your home. The cause can be as trivial as a clogged filter or as serious as a failed compressor.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat issue** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setpoint, or loss of communication on AccuLink systems.
3. **Frozen indoor (evaporator) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks cooling entirely.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling capacity collapses.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the fan or compressor hums but won't start, or trips off.
6. **Failed contactor** — the outdoor unit won't energize at all.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — no power to the condenser or control board.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — weak cooling, ice on the lines, longer run times.
9. **Failed compressor** — outdoor fan runs but no cooling; the most expensive outcome.
10. **Control/communication fault** (AccuLink systems) — indoor and outdoor units lose handshake.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop here if a check requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or electrical panels beyond the breaker.

- **Filter:** Pull and inspect the air filter. If it's gray or clogged, replace it. This is the single most common cause.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model. On communicating controls, check for an alert message.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the indoor and outdoor units. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** A full drain pan can trigger a safety float switch that shuts down cooling. Check for standing water and clear a clogged drain line if you can access it.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass and debris from around the condenser so it can breathe. If you see ice on the refrigerant lines, turn the system to FAN ONLY for a few hours to let it thaw before calling.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work through a logical sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply and return air temperatures (a healthy split is roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Confirm power and controls** — test 24V control voltage, contactor, and capacitor with a multimeter.
4. **Read any fault codes** — on AccuLink/communicating systems, pull codes from the control.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — check pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge charge (requires EPA certification).
6. **Locate leaks** — if refrigerant is low, use electronic detectors or dye rather than just "topping off."
7. **Test the compressor** — check windings, amp draw, and start components.

Be wary of any tech who wants to "add a couple pounds of refrigerant" without finding the leak — that's a temporary, repeat-charge patch, not a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak or warm airflow | Dirty filter or coil | Replace filter; clear condenser debris | Deep-clean coils, check charge |
| No air at all | Blower or thermostat failure | Check thermostat mode/batteries, breaker | Test blower motor and control board |
| Ice on lines/indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set to FAN ONLY to thaw; replace filter | Find airflow restriction or leak |
| Outdoor unit silent | Contactor, capacitor, or breaker | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor/capacitor |
| Humming, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None | Test and replace capacitor |
| Fan runs, no cooling | Failed compressor or low charge | None | Test compressor, check refrigerant |
| Thermostat shows alert | Communication fault (AccuLink) | Note the code, power-cycle once | Diagnose wiring/board handshake |
| Water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain line/pan | Clear drain, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $130–$350
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Low-voltage fuse/wiring repair:** $100–$300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $300–$1,000+ depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $800–$2,000+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,800+ (often makes replacing the unit worth considering)

If your unit is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison — the repair can approach the cost of a new condenser.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code. On most American Standard split systems — including the Silver, Gold, and Platinum series (4A7 and 4A6 condenser families) — the outdoor unit and indoor coil work together to remove heat from your home. When cooling drops off or stops entirely, it means the system either isn't moving enough air, isn't circulating enough refrigerant, or the compressor isn't running.

On communicating systems with an AccuLink control (Platinum models paired with a compatible thermostat), the thermostat may display a specific alert or fault message when a component fails. Non-communicating single- and two-stage systems usually give you no code at all — you just notice warm air. What the display shows, and whether it shows anything, **varies by model and thermostat**, so don't assume a blank display means everything is fine.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor coil and stop cooling.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a failed thermostat.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower runs.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — caked with grass, dust, or debris, the unit can't reject heat.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common condenser failure; compressor or fan won't start.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the compressor is pitted or stuck.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, ice on lines, longer run times. Always indicates a leak, never normal "top off."
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; blows warm air until it thaws.
9. **Blocked condensate drain triggering a safety float switch** — shuts down cooling to prevent water damage.
10. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive cause; often the last suspect after others are ruled out.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for a homeowner to do:

- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray or clogged, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL and the temperature is below room temperature. If it's battery-powered and blank or dim, replace the batteries.
- **Check the breaker.** Look at your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal an electrical fault.
- **Check supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow.
- **Look at the outdoor unit.** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around it (power off at the disconnect first). If the coil fins are packed with dirt, that reduces cooling.
- **Check the condensate drain.** If your indoor unit has a drain pan that's full or overflowing, a float safety switch may have shut the system off. Clearing a visible clog and pan can restore operation.

Do **not** open sealed refrigerant lines, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed technician will typically:

1. Confirm the thermostat is calling for cooling and check the 24V signal reaches the outdoor unit.
2. Verify voltage at the disconnect and inspect the contactor and capacitor with a meter.
3. Check the indoor coil and blower for airflow problems and ice.
4. Attach gauges to measure refrigerant pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling — this tells them if the charge is correct or if there's a leak.
5. Perform a leak search (electronic detector or dye) if refrigerant is low.
6. Test the compressor's electrical windings and amp draw if the unit won't start or trips.
7. On AccuLink communicating systems, read stored fault data from the control board.

If a quote jumps straight to "compressor" or "recharge" without checking airflow, capacitor, and doing a proper pressure test, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs | Dirty filter or frozen coil | Replace filter; turn off AC and let coil thaw | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker, bad capacitor or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test and replace capacitor/contactor |
| Ice on refrigerant lines | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Replace filter; shut off to thaw | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| System off, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain, float switch tripped | Clear visible drain clog | Clear line, test float switch |
| Unit hums but won't start, may trip breaker | Failed capacitor or compressor | Stop resetting; call pro | Test compressor windings and amps |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Diagnose control wiring |
| Weak cooling, long run times | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Clean debris around outdoor unit | Coil cleaning, pressure test |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional and model variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 (more for communicating AccuLink thermostats)
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R‑410A units cost more than older systems)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$1,800+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement if out of warranty)

Many American Standard units carry a 10-year registered limited warranty on the compressor and internal parts — check your registration, as it can cut part costs dramatically (you'll still pay labor).

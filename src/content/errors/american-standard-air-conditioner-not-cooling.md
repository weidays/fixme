## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's a symptom. American Standard air conditioners don't display a text error for this; instead you notice the house won't reach setpoint, the air from the vents feels warm or weak, or the system runs constantly without satisfying the thermostat.

On single-stage **Silver** and two-stage **Gold** units there's no onboard display, so diagnosis is done with meters at the equipment. On variable-speed **Platinum** units with an AccuLink communicating control, the thermostat or control board can log fault data (comfort call failures, coil sensor faults) that a technician reads through the system menu. Exactly what data is available varies by model and thermostat generation, so don't assume your unit shows a code.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the coil.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** (the outdoor unit) — dust, grass clippings, and cottonwood block heat rejection.
3. **Failed run/dual capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan won't start or hums.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, ice on the lines, longer run times.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; blocks cooling entirely.
6. **Condenser fan motor failure** — unit overheats and trips on high-pressure or thermal limit.
7. **Contactor failure** — the outdoor unit won't energize at all.
8. **Thermostat or wiring fault** — no cooling call reaches the equipment.
9. **Compressor failure** — least common but most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. If it's battery-powered, install fresh batteries.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping means a fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure vents aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs. Closing too many rooms starves the system.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (power off) if it looks dirty.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trigger a float switch that shuts down cooling. Check for a full pan or standing water at the indoor unit.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON is fine) and let it thaw completely for a few hours before running again.

Do not open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

- Verify a cooling call is reaching the outdoor unit with a meter at the contactor.
- Test the dual run capacitor's microfarad rating against the nameplate.
- Check the condenser fan motor and compressor for proper amp draw.
- Attach gauges to read suction and head pressures, and measure superheat/subcooling to judge the refrigerant charge — this is how they confirm a leak versus a mechanical issue.
- Inspect the evaporator coil and measure the temperature split across it (return vs supply air).
- On Platinum/AccuLink systems, read stored fault history and sensor data through the communicating control.
- Leak-search with electronic detectors or dye if the charge is low.

A quote for "low refrigerant, added freon" with no leak search should raise a flag — refrigerant doesn't get consumed, so a low charge means a leak that should be located.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm vents | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Verify airflow, check blower |
| Outdoor unit runs, warm air | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil exterior, clear debris | Deep coil clean, verify charge |
| Unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None — power off, call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low charge or low airflow | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Long run times, poor cooling | Low refrigerant (leak) | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Compressor runs, outdoor fan dead | Fan motor failure | Power off | Replace condenser fan motor |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Contactor or breaker | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, wiring |
| No cooling call at all | Thermostat/wiring | Replace batteries, check settings | Trace low-voltage wiring |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Compressor failure | None | Test compressor, options review |

## Repair costs

Ranges are typical US installed prices; your area and unit age will vary.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Dual run capacitor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often near the cost of a new condenser — weigh replacement)

If the unit is over 12–15 years old and needs a compressor or coil, ask for a replacement quote for comparison, especially given the phase-out of older refrigerants.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a fault code in the traditional sense — American Standard air conditioners don't display an alphanumeric error for it the way a furnace board flashes a number. Instead, it's a symptom: the system is calling for cooling but indoor temperatures aren't dropping, air from the vents isn't cold, or the outdoor unit isn't behaving as it should.

On systems with an AccuLink (ComfortLink II) communicating thermostat, you may actually see a text alert or a fault reference. On standard 24-volt thermostats, there's no message at all — just a warm house. Because "not cooling" spans everything from a $15 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you from the cheapest, safest checks up to the professional diagnoses.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, cutting cooling and often causing the coil to freeze.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a miscalibrated or failing communicating thermostat.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer and the unit blows warm.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, dust, and debris block heat rejection, so the system can't cool effectively.
5. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler has lost power.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor won't start; you may hear humming or clicking outside.
7. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; requires a licensed tech.
9. **Failed condenser fan or blower motor** — no airflow over the coils.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the heart of the system has died.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. If any of them don't resolve it, stop and call a pro.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 cause of weak or no cooling.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is below room temperature. If it uses batteries, replace them. Make sure the fan is set to AUTO, not just ON (ON alone circulates warm air).
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the AC and air handler. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop — do not keep resetting it.
- **Vents and registers:** Walk the house and confirm supply vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Check that return grilles aren't blocked.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or leaves packed against the condenser. Clear anything blocking airflow, with the power off.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water pooling near the indoor air handler or a full drip pan, the drain may be clogged. Some units have a float switch that shuts cooling off when the pan fills.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (leave the fan ON) and let it thaw fully — this can take several hours — before running it again.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or add refrigerant yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech follows a logical path, and knowing it helps you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint and thermostat call** — confirm the system is actually receiving a cooling call and 24V is present.
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower operation, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Measure electrical values** — test the capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and confirm voltage to the compressor and fan.
4. **Gauge the refrigerant** — connect manifold gauges, read pressures, and calculate superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct.
5. **Leak-search if low** — use electronic detectors or dye if refrigerant is undercharged. A good tech won't just "top off" without finding the leak.
6. **Check the compressor and fan motors** — measure amp draw and windings.
7. **On communicating (AccuLink) systems** — pull fault history from the thermostat or control board.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without measuring pressures and electricals first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm airflow, dirty filter | Clogged filter restricting airflow | Replace filter | None if that fixes it |
| Ice on coil or refrigerant lines | Frozen evaporator coil | Turn to OFF, fan ON, let thaw | Find airflow or charge root cause |
| Indoor unit runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker, capacitor, or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace capacitor or contactor |
| Humming outside, fan not spinning | Failed run capacitor or fan motor | None | Replace capacitor or condenser fan motor |
| Cooling weak in hot weather only | Low refrigerant / dirty condenser | Rinse condenser (power off) | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Water near air handler, no cooling | Clogged condensate drain, float switch tripped | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, verify float switch |
| No cold air, no thermostat response | Dead thermostat batteries or wiring | Replace batteries | Diagnose thermostat/control wiring |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Electrical short or failing compressor | Stop, don't reset again | Diagnose fault; possible compressor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Regional rates and system size affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10 – $40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75 – $250 professionally, or ~$10 DIY with tablets
- **Run capacitor:** $150 – $400
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300 – $650
- **Blower motor:** $400 – $900 (more for variable-speed ECM motors)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500 – $1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV / metering device:** $500 – $900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200 – $2,500 (often makes replacing an older system worth considering)
- **Communicating control board / AccuLink issues:** $400 – $900

If your system is more than 12–15 years old and facing a compressor or coil failure, ask your tech to compare repair cost against a full replacement — and whether older R-22 refrigerant applies, which raises recharge costs significantly.

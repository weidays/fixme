## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your American Standard air conditioner runs but the house never reaches the setpoint, or the air from the vents isn't cold. On systems with an **AccuLink communicating control (Platinum series)**, your thermostat may display a specific alert or fault number; on standard 24-volt (non-communicating) systems, there's usually no code at all, just warm air.

Because "not cooling" can stem from anything from a $15 filter to a $3,000 compressor, this page walks you through the safe checks you can do yourself and how a technician narrows it down — so you can sanity-check any quote you're given.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked airflow** — restricts return air, drops capacity, and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — ice on the coil blocks airflow entirely; often caused by dirty filter or low refrigerant.
3. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection, so the system can't cool efficiently.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure; the compressor or fan motor hums or won't start.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — American Standard units are sealed; low charge always means a leak, not "normal use."
6. **Bad contactor or tripped breaker** — the outdoor unit gets no power, so only the indoor fan runs.
7. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a miscommunication on AccuLink systems.
8. **Failing compressor** — the least common but most expensive cause; the unit draws power but doesn't build pressure.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are genuinely safe for a homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or electrical panels.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Set the fan to AUTO, not ON (ON alone can blow room-temperature air). Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it out and look at it against a light. If it's gray or clogged, replace it — this is the single most common fix.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't starving the system of airflow.
- **Outdoor unit:** Turn the unit off, then clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around and against the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance. You can gently rinse the exterior fins with a garden hose (no pressure washer).
- **Condensate drain:** If you see standing water or a full drain pan, a clogged condensate line may have triggered a safety float switch. Clearing a visible clog is fine; anything more involved is a pro job.
- **Check for ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system OFF (fan can stay on) and let it thaw for several hours before running again. Persistent freezing needs a technician.

If cooling doesn't return after a fresh filter, correct thermostat settings, and a clean outdoor unit, it's time to call a licensed HVAC tech.

## How a technician will diagnose it

Knowing the sequence helps you tell a thorough diagnosis from a quick upsell:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower operation, and coil condition; confirm the coil isn't iced or clogged.
3. **Confirm power and controls** — verify 24V signal to the contactor, thermostat call, and (on Platinum/AccuLink units) read fault data from the communicating control.
4. **Test electrical components** — measure the run capacitor with a meter, check the contactor, and read compressor and fan motor amp draw.
5. **Check refrigerant pressures** — connect gauges to read suction and liquid pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling. Low charge points to a leak, which they'll locate with a leak detector or dye.
6. **Assess the compressor** — if pressures don't build and electricals are good, they'll test the compressor windings.

A good tech explains *what* they found and *why* it caused the symptom before quoting a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, bad contactor, or capacitor | Reset breaker once; check thermostat | Test/replace contactor or capacitor, trace fault |
| Ice on indoor coil or refrigerant lines | Low refrigerant or airflow restriction | Turn off, replace filter, let ice thaw | Find/repair leak, recharge, check airflow |
| Weak cooling, house never reaches setpoint | Dirty filter, dirty condenser, low charge | Replace filter, clean outdoor unit | Measure pressures, clean coils, check charge |
| Outdoor fan spins, compressor hums then stops | Failed run/start capacitor | None — leave electrical to a pro | Replace capacitor, verify amp draw |
| Nothing runs at all | Dead thermostat, blown fuse, breaker | Replace batteries, reset breaker once | Check control board, 24V transformer, wiring |
| Unit runs constantly, barely cools | Refrigerant leak or failing compressor | Confirm filter/coil are clean | Pressure test, leak repair or compressor eval |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor included. Prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Air filter:** $0–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries or reset:** $0–$15 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $300–$1,500 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$3,000+ (often worth weighing against a new system if the unit is 10+ years old)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180, frequently credited toward the repair

Note: American Standard uses **R-410A** in most modern units; older units may use R-22, which is expensive and increasingly scarce — a factor if you're weighing repair vs. replacement.

## Related codes

- **AccuLink / Platinum communicating faults** — specific alert codes shown on Nexia/AccuLink thermostats when a communicating fault occurs.
- **Frozen evaporator coil** — related airflow/refrigerant symptom often mistaken for a separate fault.
- **Outdoor unit won't start** — overlaps with capacitor and contactor causes above.
- **Short cycling** — compressor turning on and off rapidly, sometimes tied to low charge or a control fault.

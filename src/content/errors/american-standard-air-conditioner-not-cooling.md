## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on most American Standard air conditioners — it's a symptom. Your system may be running, blowing air, and drawing power, but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

On variable-speed and **communicating (AccuLink) systems**, the thermostat *may* display a specific fault or error code that points to the cause (for example, a communication fault, a low-pressure lockout, or a compressor fault). On older single-stage units there is usually no display — you diagnose by observing behavior. Because the exact codes and lockout behavior vary by model and control board, check the label on your air handler or outdoor unit and match it to your owner's manual before acting on any code.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops efficiency, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the single most common reason for warm air with the system running. Requires a certified tech.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start; a very common failure in summer heat.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling capacity collapses.
5. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; the coil ices over and blocks cooling.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit fails to close, so the compressor never runs.
7. **Condenser fan motor failure** — the outdoor fan doesn't spin, causing overheating and lockout.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — power to the outdoor unit is cut.
9. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a wiring/communication fault.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive; often preceded by breaker trips or hard-start symptoms.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes many "not cooling" complaints.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Breaker** — check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker (usually a double-pole breaker for the AC). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers** — make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain** — if you see water pooling near the indoor unit or the pan is full, the drain may be clogged. A full pan can trip a safety float switch and shut the system down.

Do **not** open the outdoor disconnect and touch internal components, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch. Capacitors hold a lethal charge even with power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech will work methodically. Use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply and return air temperatures (a healthy split is usually 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, blower, and coil for icing or restriction.
3. **Read any fault codes** — on communicating systems, they'll pull codes from the thermostat or control board.
4. **Test electrical components** — measure the capacitor's microfarads, check the contactor, and confirm voltage at the compressor and fan.
5. **Gauge the refrigerant** — connect manifold gauges, read pressures, and check superheat/subcooling. Low pressure means a leak they must locate.
6. **Inspect the coils** — clean or note a dirty condenser or frozen evaporator.
7. **Confirm the diagnosis** before recommending parts. Be wary of anyone who jumps straight to "you need a new compressor" without measuring anything.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Check coil for icing |
| Indoor coil iced over | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Turn AC off, fan on to thaw; replace filter | Find leak, recharge, verify airflow |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Fan motor or capacitor | None — call pro | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Warm air, unit runs fine | Low refrigerant leak | None | Leak search, repair, EPA-certified recharge |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker/contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, wiring, disconnect |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Electrical fault/compressor | Stop resetting | Diagnose short or failing compressor |
| Water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain if visible | Flush line, test float switch, add pump |
| No cold air, thermostat blank | Dead batteries/thermostat fault | Replace batteries | Diagnose control wiring/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Condensate pump / drain clearing:** $100–$450
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$2,000+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (older R-22 systems cost far more)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often the point where replacing the whole unit makes more sense)

Diagnostic/service call fees typically run $75–$200 and are sometimes applied toward the repair.

## Related codes

- **AC freezing up / iced coil** — low airflow or low refrigerant
- **AC blowing warm air** — refrigerant, capacitor, or contactor issues
- **Outdoor unit not turning on** — contactor, capacitor, or breaker
- **Communicating system fault codes** — check your AccuLink thermostat display and owner's manual for the specific code
- **AC short cycling** — often related to airflow, refrigerant, or a failing capacitor

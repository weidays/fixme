## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code. American Standard air conditioners generally don't display alphanumeric error codes on the equipment itself the way some furnaces do. Instead, a comfort-control thermostat (like an AccuLink / Nexia-enabled control) may show a general "cooling failure" or system alert, or you simply notice the house won't reach the setpoint.

This page covers the case where the system is powered and calling for cooling but the air coming from your vents is not cold, or the house temperature keeps climbing. Because this brand uses several system types — single-stage, two-stage, and variable-speed AccuComfort units — the exact behavior and diagnostics differ, and we note where that matters below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the indoor coil, cutting capacity and eventually freezing the coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, dust, and cottonwood block heat rejection so the system can't cool efficiently.
3. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a failed thermostat not calling for cooling.
4. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler has lost power.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure that stops the compressor or condenser fan from starting.
6. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; often paired with ice on the lines or coil.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; blows warm air until it thaws.
8. **Failed contactor** — the outdoor unit won't energize the compressor.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — compressor may run but overheats and shuts down.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive cause; the unit hums or trips but won't cool.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything is beyond them.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it takes them.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Breakers:** Check the main panel and any disconnect near the indoor unit. If a breaker is tripped, reset it once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting it.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear grass clippings, leaves, and debris from around and off the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water pooling near the indoor unit, the drain may be clogged. A wet-safety float switch will shut the system off to prevent overflow. Clear the visible drain line and pan if you can safely reach it.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON is fine) and let it thaw fully — often several hours — before running it again.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, remove electrical panels on the outdoor unit, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work airflow-first, then electrical, then refrigerant:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** — confirms the thermostat is sending 24V and the system is trying to run.
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and indoor coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Electrical inspection** — tests the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, and confirms voltage to the compressor and condenser fan.
4. **Temperature split** — measures the difference between return and supply air (typically 15–22°F on a healthy system).
5. **Refrigerant readings** — connects gauges to check pressures and calculates superheat/subcooling. Low readings point to a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, a proper repair includes finding and fixing the leak, not just topping off.
7. **Component testing** — for variable-speed (AccuComfort) systems, the tech may pull fault data from the control/inverter board.

A quote that jumps straight to "you need refrigerant" without checking airflow and capacitor first deserves a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Inspect blower and duct restriction |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, let thaw | Diagnose airflow vs. refrigerant charge |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker/contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, confirm power |
| Compressor hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Fan on outside not spinning | Failed condenser fan motor | None — call pro | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| House slowly warms, long runtimes | Low refrigerant / leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| No cooling, thermostat blank | Dead batteries/no power | Replace batteries | Diagnose control wiring/transformer |
| Water near indoor unit, system off | Clogged condensate drain | Clear visible drain line | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Compressor failure | None — call pro | Test compressor, quote replacement |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; regional and by-model variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,800 depending on leak location
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing to full system replacement)
- **AccuComfort inverter/control board:** $600–$1,500 on variable-speed units

If your system is out of warranty and needs a compressor or coil, ask for a repair-vs-replace comparison. American Standard registered systems often carry a 10-year parts warranty — have your model and serial number handy to check coverage.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your American Standard air conditioner runs but the house won't reach the thermostat setpoint, or the air from the vents feels warm or only slightly cool.

American Standard's higher-end variable-speed systems (Platinum series with the AccuLink / inverter control board) may log a specific fault or flash an error at the control board, but most single- and two-stage systems give no code at all. In those cases the "diagnosis" is a process of elimination across airflow, electrical, and refrigerant.

Because refrigerant handling, sealed-system leaks, and compressor faults require EPA certification and specialized tools, this problem is rated **pro** — but there are several safe checks below that may fix it for free.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the evaporator coil.
2. **Thermostat issue** — wrong mode (set to "fan" not "cool"), dead batteries, or a setpoint that isn't calling for cooling.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser or indoor blower loses power while the other keeps running, so you feel airflow but no cold.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from a dirty filter, closed vents, or low refrigerant. Ice blocks heat transfer and airflow.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common wear part. The compressor or condenser fan won't start, so the outdoor unit hums or stays quiet.
6. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, killing cooling capacity.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice, and higher bills. Requires a licensed tech to find and repair.
8. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the fan doesn't spin, the compressor overheats and shuts down on its internal protector.
9. **Contactor failure** — the electrical switch that energizes the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the condenser never starts.
10. **Compressor failure** — least common but most expensive; the outdoor unit won't cool at all.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. If none fix it, call a licensed HVAC tech.

- **Air filter:** Locate and inspect it. If it's gray or clogged, replace it with the correct size (printed on the frame). This alone fixes a large share of no-cooling calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the AC. If tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs. Restricted airflow starves the system.
- **Condensate drain:** If your indoor unit has a float safety switch and the drain is clogged, the system may shut off cooling. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear a visible clog if you can reach it safely.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant lines, switch the system to **Off** (or fan-only) and let it thaw fully — this can take several hours — then replace the filter before restarting.

**Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works methodically — you can sanity-check their quote against these steps:

1. **Verifies the complaint** and checks thermostat operation and the call for cooling.
2. **Measures the temperature split** (return vs. supply air). A healthy system usually shows a 15–20°F drop; a small split points to airflow or refrigerant issues.
3. **Inspects airflow** — filter, coil condition, and blower operation.
4. **Checks electrical components** — capacitor microfarad reading with a meter, contactor condition, and voltage at the condenser.
5. **Reads refrigerant pressures** with gauges and compares to superheat/subcooling targets for your model. This tells them if you're low (leak) or overcharged.
6. **Leak search** if refrigerant is low — using electronic detectors or dye, never just "topping off."
7. **On Platinum/variable-speed units,** pulls fault history from the AccuLink control board or communicating thermostat.

Be cautious of any tech who wants to add refrigerant without first performing a leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak flow | Dirty filter / airflow restriction | Replace filter, open vents | Clean coil, check blower |
| Vents blow warm, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None (electrical) | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on coil or lines | Frozen coil (airflow or low charge) | Turn off, thaw, new filter | Diagnose airflow or refrigerant |
| Weak cooling, high bills | Low refrigerant (leak) | None (requires cert) | Leak search and repair, recharge |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed condenser fan motor | None | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Little/no cooling, unit runs hot | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse debris off gently (power off) | Deep coil cleaning |
| No cooling at all, unit tries to start | Compressor failure | None | Test and replace compressor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, including parts and labor. Your quote varies by region and model.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$1,800+ (often worth comparing against a new condenser or system, especially on older units)

Diagnostic/service-call fees typically run $75–$200 and are often credited toward the repair.

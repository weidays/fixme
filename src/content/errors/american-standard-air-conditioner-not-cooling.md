## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on the display — it's the symptom homeowners report when an American Standard air conditioner runs (or tries to run) but the house stays warm. On communicating **AccuLink** systems paired with a Gold- or Platinum-series thermostat, you may see a specific alert or error number, but on standard single-stage systems there's often no code at all — just warm air from the vents.

The root problem is always the same: the system can't move heat from inside your home to the outside. That happens when airflow is blocked, the refrigerant charge is wrong, or an electrical/mechanical part in the outdoor unit has failed. Because diagnosing charge and compressor faults requires gauges and EPA certification, this is rated a **pro** repair — but there are several safe checks below that can save you a service call.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, freezes the evaporator coil, and stops cooling. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (not set to COOL), or a setpoint above room temperature.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser has lost power while the indoor blower still runs, so you get airflow but no cold.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with dirt, grass, or cottonwood can't reject heat.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure that keeps the compressor or fan motor from starting.
7. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit can pit or stick, cutting power to the compressor.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity and can freeze the coil. Requires leak repair, not just a top-off.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor runs hot and shuts down on overload.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit hums or trips the breaker but never cools.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace or clean it. Then run the system for 30 minutes.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is at least 3–5°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal a fault.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, tall grass, or leaves choking the condenser. Clear anything within about 2 feet. Make sure it's plugged in / the disconnect isn't pulled.
- **Condensate drain:** If a full drain pan or clogged line tripped a float switch, the system may shut off cooling. Look for standing water near the indoor unit; a wet-vac on the drain line can clear a light clog.

Do **not** open the electrical panel of the outdoor unit, touch the capacitor (it holds a dangerous charge), or add refrigerant. Those are pro tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence, and knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is about 16–22°F).
2. **Confirm power and controls** — checks voltage at the contactor, thermostat call, and (on AccuLink units) reads any board fault codes.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter — a weak or bulged cap is a fast, cheap find.
4. **Check the contactor** for pitting or failure to pull in.
5. **Inspect coils and airflow** — dirty condenser, frozen evaporator, failing blower.
6. **Attach gauges** to read pressures and superheat/subcooling — this reveals low charge, a leak, or a restriction.
7. **Amp-test the compressor and fan motor** to spot electrical failure or a locked rotor.
8. **Leak search** if charge is low — with electronic detector or dye.

If a tech wants to "just add refrigerant" without checking for a leak, get a second opinion.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker / no outdoor power | Reset breaker once, check disconnect | Test contactor, wiring, control board |
| Ice on copper lines or coil | Dirty filter or low refrigerant | Replace filter, turn off AC to thaw | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — pro only | Test and replace capacitor |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries / power loss | Replace batteries | Check transformer, wiring, board |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Fan motor or capacitor failure | None — pro only | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Weak cooling, high energy bills | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Rinse coil gently, replace filter | Coil clean, pressure/charge check |
| Water near indoor unit, AC off | Clogged condensate drain / float | Clear drain with wet-vac | Clear line, treat pan, test float |
| Loud hum then breaker trips | Failing compressor | Do not keep resetting | Amp-test, replace compressor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$200 (or DIY)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$300
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,800 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Blower motor:** $450–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth weighing against a new unit if out of warranty)

American Standard offers strong registered-warranty coverage on many parts and compressors — check your registration; you may owe only labor.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a fault code. American Standard air conditioners are cooling-only outdoor units controlled by your thermostat — the unit itself does not display an alphanumeric error. When you call your system "not cooling," it usually means one of three things:

- The system runs but the air from the vents is warm or barely cool.
- The outdoor condenser never starts even though the thermostat calls for cooling.
- The indoor blower runs but the outdoor unit hums, trips a breaker, or shuts off quickly.

On **variable-speed AccuComfort (Platinum) systems**, the communicating control board *can* log fault codes on a compatible thermostat (e.g., an AccuLink display). If you have one of those, note the exact code — it will point a technician straight to the problem. Single-stage and two-stage units give no code, so diagnosis is done by measurement.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, freezes the evaporator coil, and kills cooling. The single most common cause.
2. **Dirty condenser coil** — a coil packed with grass, cottonwood, or dust can't reject heat, so the AC runs constantly without cooling.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or fan hums but won't start, or the outdoor fan spins while the compressor is dead.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the copper lines, and long run times. Requires a licensed tech.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — result of low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks all cooling until thawed.
6. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the condenser fails to pull in, so the outdoor unit never starts.
7. **Failed condenser fan motor** — compressor tries to run but overheats and shuts down on its internal overload.
8. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome, often the end result of ignored low-refrigerant or capacitor problems.
9. **Thermostat or control wiring fault** — no cooling call reaches the outdoor unit.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks you should do yourself. Do not open the electrical panel of the condenser or touch the capacitor — it can hold a lethal charge.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL and lower the setpoint 3–5°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it has them and the screen is dim.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter alone can stop all cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor air handler/furnace breaker and the outdoor AC breaker (and the disconnect box near the condenser). Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — that's an electrical fault for a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** Clear grass, leaves, and debris from the condenser fins and give it 2 feet of clearance. Rinse the outside of the coil gently with a garden hose (power off) if it's visibly dirty.
- **Condensate:** If the indoor unit has a float switch and the drain pan is full, a clogged condensate line can shut the system down. Clearing the drain is a reasonable DIY task.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the copper refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to OFF (fan only, if available) and let it thaw for several hours before calling. Running iced-up damages the compressor.

If cooling doesn't return after a clean filter, clear vents, and a good thermostat call, the problem is almost certainly refrigerant, capacitor, or motor — all pro-level jobs.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech follows a repeatable path. Knowing it helps you judge a quote:

1. **Confirm the call:** Verify 24V is reaching the contactor when the thermostat calls for cooling.
2. **Check the contactor and capacitor:** Test the capacitor's microfarad rating against its rating plate; inspect the contactor for pitted or stuck contacts.
3. **Measure amp draw:** Clamp-meter the compressor and fan motor to see if they're pulling correct, locked-rotor, or no current.
4. **Read refrigerant pressures:** Connect gauges and measure superheat/subcooling against the American Standard charging chart for your model. This reveals undercharge (leak), overcharge, or a restriction.
5. **Leak search:** If low on refrigerant, use electronic detector or dye to find the leak — recharging without repair is a bad practice.
6. **Airflow check:** Verify blower operation and a clean evaporator coil, since low airflow mimics a refrigerant problem.
7. **Communicating units:** On AccuComfort/Platinum systems, pull stored fault codes from the control board to confirm the diagnosis.

A good quote names a *specific* failed part with a *measured* reason (e.g., "capacitor tested 22µF on a 45µF rating"). Be wary of "you just need refrigerant" with no leak search.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, filter black | Clogged filter / frozen coil | Replace filter, let coil thaw | Verify airflow and coil are clean |
| Outdoor unit silent, no fan | Blown fuse, bad contactor, or no 24V call | Reset breaker once, check thermostat | Test contactor, 24V control circuit, low-voltage fuse |
| Fan spins, compressor hums then quits | Failed run capacitor | Note the symptom | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Shut off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, repair, recharge to spec |
| Runs constantly, weak cooling | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Rinse outdoor coil, clear debris | Check pressures, clean coil, leak search |
| Compressor tries, trips breaker | Failed compressor or hard-start fault | Stop resetting, call pro | Amp draw test, compressor/hard-start diagnosis |
| Outdoor fan dead, compressor hot | Condenser fan motor failure | Shut unit off | Replace fan motor and capacitor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250.
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$350.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700.
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location; evaporator coil leaks trend higher.
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$400 (more for AccuLink communicating stats).
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500; on an older R-22 unit this often makes full replacement the smarter choice.
- **Full outdoor unit / system replacement:** $4,500–$9,000+ when repair isn't worth it.

Note: American Standard registered systems often carry a **10-year parts warranty**. If your unit is within it, the failed part may be covered — you'd pay labor only. Confirm your registration and dates.

## Related codes

- **American Standard AC freezing / iced coil** — same low-airflow and low-refrigerant roots as no cooling.
- **AccuComfort / AccuLink communicating fault codes** — the coded errors on Platinum variable-speed systems.
- **Outdoor unit not turning on** — overlaps with contactor, capacitor, and breaker causes above.
- **AC short cycling** — often an early warning of the capacitor or refrigerant issues that later show up as "not cooling."

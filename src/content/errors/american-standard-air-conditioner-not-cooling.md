## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code. On most American Standard split-system air conditioners it means the system is powered and calling for cooling but the air coming from your vents is warm, room temperature, or not cold enough to reach the thermostat setpoint.

On systems with a communicating **AccuLink / S-series thermostat**, the control may actually display a numbered fault or a text message (for example a compressor lockout or communication fault) instead of just failing quietly. Behavior varies by control board and thermostat generation, so check your thermostat screen for any specific code before assuming it's a generic problem.

The core issue is almost always one of three things: **airflow is restricted**, **the refrigerant circuit isn't working** (low charge or a compressor/electrical fault), or **the system never got the call to cool** (thermostat or power problem).

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil into a block of ice.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "heat" or "fan" instead of "cool"), or a setpoint that's too high.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit (condenser) has its own breaker. If it's tripped, the indoor fan may still blow warm air.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The outdoor fan or compressor won't start, often with a humming sound.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil and weak airflow.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — refrigerant isn't consumed, so low charge means a leak. Cooling drops off and the coil may freeze.
7. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil packed with dirt, grass, or debris can't reject heat.
8. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit fails to close, so the condenser never starts.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor may run but overheat and shut down without airflow over the coil.
10. **Failed compressor** — the least common but most expensive; the "heart" of the system no longer pumps refrigerant.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. If none of them resolves it, stop and call a pro.

- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This is the #1 fix.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the **batteries** if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Check the breakers.** Look for a tripped breaker for both the indoor air handler/furnace and the outdoor condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop — do not keep resetting, as that indicates an electrical fault.
- **Check the vents and registers.** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Check for ice.** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **OFF** and set the fan to **ON** to help it thaw (this can take several hours). Running a frozen system can damage the compressor.
- **Check the condensate drain.** A full drain pan or clogged condensate line can trip a safety float switch that shuts the system down. If the pan is overflowing, note it for the technician.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, touch the capacitor (it stores a dangerous charge even when power is off), or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified technician will typically:

1. **Confirm the call for cooling** and check thermostat wiring and 24V control voltage.
2. **Verify power** to the indoor and outdoor units and inspect the contactor for pitting or failure to pull in.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter — a weak or bulging capacitor is a frequent, cheap find.
4. **Check the condenser fan and compressor** startup, measuring amp draw against the nameplate.
5. **Attach gauges and measure refrigerant pressures and superheat/subcooling** to determine if the charge is correct. Low readings point to a leak, which they'll trace with an electronic detector or dye.
6. **Inspect coils** (indoor and outdoor) for dirt and ice.
7. **On communicating systems**, read fault history from the AccuLink thermostat or control board.

A quote should name a specific failed part and a measured reason — "capacitor tested at 30 µF, rated 45 µF" is a good sign. Be cautious of a quote to "add refrigerant" with no mention of finding the leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check for coil freeze-up |
| Indoor fan runs, warm air, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or bad contactor/capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor or capacitor |
| Ice on refrigerant lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw, replace filter | Find leak, recharge, or clear airflow issue |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run/start capacitor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor |
| Compressor runs, outdoor fan not spinning | Failed condenser fan motor | None — power off unit | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| No air at all, no thermostat display | Dead batteries or no power | Replace batteries, check breaker | Diagnose control/transformer |
| Cools poorly on hot days only | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Gently rinse outdoor coil (power off) | Clean coil, check refrigerant |
| System off, pan full of water | Clogged condensate line / float switch | Note it, call pro | Clear drain line, reset switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, including parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$500+ (communicating AccuLink stats cost more)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis + repair + recharge:** $500–$2,500 depending on leak location; evaporator coil replacement can push this higher
- **Compressor replacement:** $2,000–$4,500+ (often worth weighing against replacing the whole unit if it's 10+ years old)

Prices vary by region, unit age, and refrigerant type. Older R-22 systems are especially expensive to recharge.

## Related codes

- **AC frozen / iced-over coil** — closely related; usually airflow or refrigerant.
- **AC outdoor unit not turning on** — contactor, capacitor, or breaker.
- **AccuLink communication fault** — control-board or wiring issue on communicating systems.
- **Compressor lockout / hard-start fault** — capacitor or compressor problem flagged on the thermostat.

If your thermostat shows a specific numbered code, look that up directly rather than treating it as a generic "not cooling" issue.

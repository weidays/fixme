## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it is the symptom you notice when your American Standard air conditioner is running (or trying to run) but your home isn't getting cold. On systems with a communicating **AccuLink** thermostat, you may see a related fault or comfort alert on the display; on conventional systems there's no code at all, just warm air.

The underlying problem falls into one of a few buckets: airflow is restricted, the outdoor unit isn't running, refrigerant charge is low, or a control component (capacitor, contactor, thermostat) has failed. This page walks you through the safe checks, then explains how a pro narrows it down so you can sanity-check any quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, causing weak cooling and, in bad cases, a frozen indoor coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — leaves, grass and dust block heat rejection, so the system runs but barely cools.
3. **Thermostat issue** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" or "heat"), or a bad program.
4. **Tripped breaker or disconnect at the outdoor unit** — the indoor blower runs but the condenser doesn't.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start; a very common failure.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, ice on the copper lines, longer run times.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — usually a downstream result of low airflow or low refrigerant.
9. **Failing compressor** — the most expensive cause; the unit tries but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no panels off the outdoor unit, no refrigerant, no electrical work.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to a light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the single most common fix.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **AUTO**. Replace the batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker for the indoor air handler and the outdoor condenser. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the pull-out disconnect box next to the outdoor unit is fully seated.
- **Supply and return vents:** Confirm they're open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water pooling or an overflow switch has cut the system off, clear the drain line and empty the pan. Drain tablets help prevent clogs.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the indoor coil or the copper lines outside, turn the system **OFF** (leave the fan on) and let it thaw fully before running again. Persistent icing means you need a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap-and-common to expensive, so you can follow along on the invoice:

1. **Confirms the symptom** — measures the temperature split between return and supply air (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Checks airflow** — inspects the filter and blower, and looks for a frozen coil.
3. **Verifies power and controls** — confirms 24V call to the outdoor unit, tests the contactor and the capacitor with a meter.
4. **Reads the charge** — connects gauges to measure suction/discharge pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge refrigerant level.
5. **Leak search** — if the charge is low, they use an electronic detector or dye to find the leak (topping off without repair is a red flag).
6. **Compressor test** — measures amp draw and checks windings if the compressor won't start.

On communicating AccuLink systems, the tech can also pull fault history from the thermostat, which speeds this up.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak cooling, low airflow | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Verify blower, check for frozen coil |
| Outdoor unit runs but poor cooling | Dirty condenser coil | Gently rinse fins with a hose (power off) | Professional coil cleaning |
| Warm air, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / wrong mode | Replace batteries, set to COOL/AUTO | Replace faulty thermostat |
| Indoor blower runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor |
| Compressor or fan hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines/coil, long run times | Low refrigerant (leak) | Turn off, let thaw | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Unit tries but no cooling at all | Failing compressor | None | Diagnose and replace compressor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges, parts and labor. Your quote varies by region and model:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$500 (communicating AccuLink models cost more)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often near the cost of a new outdoor unit)

If your system uses R-22 refrigerant (older units), recharge costs are significantly higher and replacement may be more economical.

## Related codes

- **AccuLink / communicating fault alerts** — check your thermostat display for specific comfort alerts on Platinum-series systems.
- **Frozen evaporator coil** — closely related; usually caused by the low-airflow or low-refrigerant issues above.
- **Outdoor unit not running** — a subset of "not cooling," often a capacitor, contactor, or breaker fault.
- **Short cycling** — the unit starts and stops rapidly; can overlap with capacitor and refrigerant problems.

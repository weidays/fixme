## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific American Standard fault code. Your air conditioner is either running without lowering the indoor temperature, blowing warm air, or not turning on at all. On single-stage units (Silver and most Gold series) there is no display to read — you diagnose by behavior. On communicating Platinum-series systems paired with an AccuLink/Link thermostat, the thermostat may show a specific fault or comfort alert that points to the real problem.

The core question is *which part of the system has stopped working*: the indoor blower, the outdoor condenser, the refrigerant circuit, or the controls tying them together. This page walks through the most likely culprits in order.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the most common and cheapest cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor coil into a block of ice, which then stops cooling entirely.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode, dead batteries, wrong programming, or a failed communicating thermostat on Platinum systems.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser often runs on its own breaker. If it trips, the indoor blower still runs and blows warm air.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common outdoor failure. A bad capacitor stops the compressor and/or condenser fan from starting; you may hear humming with no fan spin.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low refrigerant or low airflow. Ice on the copper lines at the indoor unit is the tell.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak or no cooling and often coil icing. Requires a licensed tech; refrigerant is never a DIY top-off.
7. **Dirty condenser coil** — a coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so cooling fades on hot days.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit can pit or stick, leaving the condenser dead.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — compressor runs but overheats and shuts down without airflow over the coil.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case. Expensive enough that replacement is often on the table.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if a step doesn't apply.

- **Thermostat:** Set it to COOL, temperature several degrees below room temp, and fan on AUTO. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers in your panel. Flip a tripped one fully off, then on. If it trips again immediately, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting it.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit clearance:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around and on top of the condenser. Keep at least two feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** If the indoor unit has a float safety switch and the drain is clogged, cooling shuts off. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear a visible clog. Drain tablets help prevent recurrence.
- **Check for ice:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON is fine) and let it thaw for a few hours before calling. Running it iced up can damage the compressor.

Do **not** open the outdoor electrical panel, discharge the capacitor, touch refrigerant lines, or bypass any safety switch. Those are pro tasks.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical path — use this to sanity-check the quote:

1. **Confirm the complaint** and check thermostat call, mode, and settings.
2. **Verify airflow** — inspect filter, blower operation, and check the evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Check power** at the outdoor unit and test the **contactor** and **capacitor** with a multimeter.
4. **Measure electrical draw** on the compressor and condenser fan motor.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and take temperature readings (superheat/subcooling) to judge the charge. Low readings point to a leak.
6. **Leak search** if charge is low — dye, electronic sniffer, or soap bubbles. A reputable tech finds and repairs the leak, not just recharges.
7. **On Platinum/communicating systems**, read AccuLink/Link fault history at the thermostat or control board.

Be wary of any tech who wants to add refrigerant without looking for a leak, or who condemns a compressor without electrical readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower runs, warm air from vents | Outdoor breaker tripped, capacitor, or low refrigerant | Check outdoor breaker; reset once | Test capacitor/contactor, check charge, find leak |
| Nothing runs at all | Thermostat, breaker, or transformer/control fault | Check thermostat batteries and both breakers | Test transformer, control board, wiring |
| Ice on indoor coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter, turn to fan-only to thaw | Diagnose airflow vs. charge; leak repair |
| Outdoor unit hums but fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — do not open the panel | Test and replace capacitor |
| Cooling weak on very hot days | Dirty condenser coil or slightly low charge | Rinse debris off coil exterior, clear clearance | Deep coil clean, verify charge |
| Compressor starts then shuts off | Failed fan motor, hard-start issue, or overload | Verify fan clearance | Test fan motor, compressor amps, controls |
| System shuts off, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain / float switch | Clear visible drain clog, add tablets | Clear drain line, test float switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Regional and unit variation applies.

- **Air filter:** $15 – $40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat battery / reset:** $0 – $10 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75 – $250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350 – $700
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150 – $400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500 – $1,500 (varies by leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000 – $2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,800 – $2,500+ — at this cost, weigh it against full system replacement, especially on older units using R-22.

Note: American Standard systems built through the mid-2020s may use R-410A, while newer units use A2L refrigerants like R-454B. This affects recharge cost and which parts are compatible — confirm your unit's refrigerant with the tech.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code. American Standard air conditioners don't typically flash a numeric code for "no cooling" the way furnaces flash diagnostic LEDs. Instead, you'll notice the house won't reach the setpoint, the air from the vents feels warm or only mildly cool, or the outdoor unit runs constantly without making the home comfortable.

Because this is a general symptom, the underlying cause can be anything from a clogged filter to a failed compressor. On systems with a communicating control board (some Platinum-series setups with a variable-speed compressor), you may see a fault indication at the thermostat — the exact wording varies by thermostat model, so read what your display says rather than assuming.

This page helps you rule out the simple, free causes before you pay for a service call, and helps you sanity-check any quote you're given.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil. The single most common cause of weak cooling.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or dead batteries** — wrong mode, wrong setpoint, or a battery-powered thermostat that has gone dark.
3. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer and vents blow warm.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — grass, cottonwood, and dust insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — a cheap part whose failure stops the compressor or condenser fan; a very common pro repair.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit pits or sticks over time.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces cooling capacity; requires a licensed tech to find, fix, and recharge.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
9. **Compressor failure** — least common but most expensive; the outdoor unit hums or trips but won't cool.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Filter:** Pull the filter. If light doesn't pass through it, replace it and let the system run 30–60 minutes.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL**, the fan is on **AUTO**, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor breakers. If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop — do not keep resetting it; call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't choking airflow. Open them.
- **Ice on the lines or indoor coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or coil, turn the system to **OFF** but leave the **fan ON** to help it thaw. Running a frozen unit makes it worse.
- **Condensate drain:** If there's a full drain pan or a tripped float switch shutting the system down, a clogged condensate line is likely. Clearing a visible clog is fine; wiring changes are not.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or touch capacitors — they hold a dangerous charge even with power off.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work through it in roughly this order:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply/return temperature split (a healthy system typically drops air ~16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition.
3. **Check line voltage and low-voltage control** — confirm 24V signal reaches the outdoor unit; test the fuse and thermostat call.
4. **Test capacitor and contactor** — with a meter; these are quick, cheap failures they should rule out early.
5. **Read refrigerant pressures** — connect gauges to check high/low side, then calculate superheat and subcooling to judge charge and coil condition.
6. **Inspect for leaks** if charge is low — electronic sniffer or dye, not just "top it off."
7. **Test the compressor** — amperage draw and windings if the fan runs but the compressor won't.

If a tech wants to add refrigerant without looking for a leak, be skeptical — refrigerant doesn't get "used up."

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vents blow warm, unit runs | Dirty filter or dirty condenser coil | Replace filter; rinse debris off outdoor unit | Deep-clean coils, check charge |
| Ice on indoor coil or copper lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set to OFF, fan ON to thaw | Find airflow issue or leak; recharge |
| Outdoor unit silent, no power | Tripped breaker or blown 24V fuse | Reset breaker once | Replace fuse, find why it blew |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cool air | Failed run capacitor or compressor | None — call a pro | Test/replace capacitor or compressor |
| Loud hum then trips breaker | Failed contactor or compressor | Stop resetting; call a pro | Replace contactor; test compressor |
| Weak cooling everywhere | Low refrigerant charge / leak | None | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries or no power | Replace batteries | Trace control power if still dead |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional pricing varies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 — DIY.
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 — DIY.
- **Condensate drain clear:** $0 DIY – $150–$250 pro.
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$350 installed.
- **Contactor:** $150–$300 installed.
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250.
- **Refrigerant leak find + repair + recharge:** $300–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650.
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,800+ — often the point where replacing the unit makes more sense, especially on older R-22 systems.

Get a written diagnosis before authorizing anything over a capacitor or contactor.

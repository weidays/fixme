## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom you're seeing: the system runs (or tries to) but your home doesn't reach the thermostat setpoint. On American Standard units it can show up several ways:

- The indoor blower runs but only room-temperature or warm air comes out.
- The outdoor condenser never starts even though the thermostat is calling for cool.
- The system short-cycles (starts and stops quickly) and never satisfies the setpoint.

On **AccuLink/AccuComfort communicating systems** (with a Gold 824 or Platinum 1050 control), the thermostat may display a specific communication or system fault instead of just "not cooling." If your thermostat shows a numbered alert, note it — it narrows the diagnosis considerably. On older single-stage 4A7A units there is no on-board diagnostic display, so the symptom is all you get.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, can freeze the indoor coil, and drops cooling output. Most common and fully DIY.
2. **Thermostat issue** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setpoint, or a loose wire. Communicating thermostats may lose the link to the outdoor unit.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser has its own breaker/disconnect. If it's off, the indoor fan blows warm air.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — very common. The compressor and/or fan motor won't start (you may hear a hum or clicking outside).
5. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — heavy dirt/grass clippings choke heat rejection, so the system runs but barely cools.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes warm air, ice on the copper lines, or short cycling. Requires a certified tech.
7. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't pull in; condenser stays silent.
8. **Frozen indoor evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks cooling entirely.
9. **Failed compressor or fan motor** — least common but most expensive; usually the end of a chain caused by other issues.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or the outdoor electrical panel.

- **Check the thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is at least 3°F below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Replace the air filter:** A clogged filter is the #1 cause. Swap in a clean one and give the system 30–60 minutes.
- **Check both breakers:** There's usually one for the indoor air handler/furnace and one for the outdoor condenser, plus an outdoor disconnect box near the unit. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated trips signal a real fault.
- **Look at the vents:** Make sure supply and return registers aren't blocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Check the condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trip a float safety switch that stops cooling. Look for standing water in the drain pan. A wet/dry vac on the drain line outlet can clear a simple clog.
- **Look at the outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the coil so it can breathe. Do this with the disconnect pulled.
- **Check for ice:** If you see frost or ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully before running again. Persistent icing means a pro visit.

Do **not** add refrigerant, open the sealed system, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech will typically work through this order — use it to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the call for cooling** at the thermostat and confirm 24V control voltage reaches the outdoor unit.
2. **Check the capacitor** with a meter (microfarad reading) — a cheap, common fix.
3. **Test the contactor** for proper pull-in and clean contacts.
4. **Measure amp draw** on the compressor and fan motor to spot a failing motor.
5. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and read pressures and temperatures (superheat/subcooling) to judge the charge and look for a leak — this requires certification.
6. **Inspect the coils** (indoor and outdoor) for dirt or ice.
7. **On communicating systems,** read fault history through the AccuLink/AccuComfort control board.

A legitimate quote names the failed part and the measured reason. Be cautious of a tech who says "just needs refrigerant" without discussing a leak — refrigerant doesn't get consumed, so a low charge means a leak that must be located.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor fan runs, warm air | Dirty filter / low refrigerant | Replace filter, check for ice | Test charge, find/repair leak |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker / bad capacitor / contactor | Reset breaker once | Test & replace capacitor or contactor |
| Humming outside, no start | Failed capacitor | None | Replace run/start capacitor |
| Ice on copper lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Filter swap, thaw with fan ON | Diagnose airflow or charge issue |
| Short cycling | Low refrigerant / clogged drain float | Check drain pan/filter | Measure charge, clear/replace float |
| Water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, vacuum line | Flush drain, replace float switch |
| Barely cools on hot days | Dirty outdoor coil | Clear debris, rinse gently | Professional coil cleaning |
| Thermostat blank/unresponsive | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries | Check 24V transformer & wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including labor. Diagnostic/service call is usually **$80–$150** and often credited toward the repair.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Breaker reset / debris clearing:** $0 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condensate drain clearing / float switch:** $100–$300
- **Outdoor condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,800 (often close to replacement cost — check warranty first)
- **Fan motor:** $400–$700

American Standard compressors and coils often carry a **10-year parts warranty if registered.** Ask the tech to check your model and serial before you approve a big-ticket part.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Most American Standard air conditioners don't display a plain-English "not cooling" message. Instead, the system either runs continuously without lowering the temperature, short-cycles, or the outdoor unit fails to start.

If you have an American Standard AccuLink (Gold or Platinum) communicating thermostat, you may see a numbered fault or alert on the display instead. Basic single-stage systems with a standard thermostat give no code at all — you just notice warm air. Because behavior varies by model and control type, use the symptoms below rather than expecting one universal readout.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (fan "on" vs "auto"), or a miscalibrated/failed thermostat.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat, so cooling drops off, especially on hot days.
5. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer.
6. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor hums but won't start. Common and relatively inexpensive.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — warm air, longer run times, hissing, or ice on the line. Requires a pro.
8. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit fails to close; unit stays off.
9. **Failing compressor or fan motor** — the most expensive cause; usually last on the list.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do:

- **Air filter:** Pull it and inspect. If it's gray or clogged, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Set it to **Cool** and lower the setpoint several degrees below room temp. Set the fan to **Auto**. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breaker:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water near the indoor unit, the drain may be clogged. You can clear a visible clog with a wet/dry vac at the outdoor drain exit or add drain tablets.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around it, and gently rinse the exterior coil fins with a garden hose (power off first).

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting down.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. Confirm the symptom and check thermostat calls and control voltage (24V).
2. Verify power at the disconnect, contactor, and capacitor with a meter.
3. Measure the temperature split across the indoor coil (return vs supply air).
4. Attach gauges to read suction and head pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to judge refrigerant charge.
5. Inspect the coils for dirt and ice, and check the blower and condenser fan operation.
6. If low on charge, perform a **leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen) — a proper repair finds the leak, not just tops off.
7. On AccuLink systems, read stored fault history from the thermostat or board.

If a quote jumps straight to "add refrigerant" without a leak search, ask why — that's a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, indoor fan runs, outdoor unit off | Tripped breaker or bad contactor/capacitor | Reset breaker once; check filter | Test/replace contactor or capacitor |
| Ice on indoor coil or refrigerant line | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter; turn off AC, run fan to thaw | Find leak, repair, recharge |
| Runs constantly, never reaches setpoint | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Rinse outdoor coil; replace filter | Measure charge, clean coils, leak search |
| Hissing or bubbling near lines | Refrigerant leak | None — call a pro | Locate and seal leak, recharge |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call a pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| No cooling, thermostat blank | Dead batteries or thermostat failure | Replace batteries | Replace or recalibrate thermostat |
| Water pooling indoors | Clogged condensate drain | Clear line with wet/dry vac | Clear drain, check safety switch |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,800 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

Note: R-22 systems (older American Standard units) cost far more to recharge than R-410A models due to refrigerant scarcity — this often tips the decision toward replacement.

- **Outdoor unit not turning on** — power, contactor, or capacitor

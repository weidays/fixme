## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the symptom homeowners report when an American Standard air conditioner runs (or tries to run) but the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On systems with AccuLink communicating controls, you may actually see an alert or fault number on the thermostat; on traditional 24V systems there's no display, just warm air.

The root problem is almost always one of three things: the system can't move enough air, it can't move enough heat (low refrigerant or a fouled coil), or an electrical component in the outdoor unit isn't energizing the compressor or fan. This page covers all three so you can sanity-check a quote before paying for a repair.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow drops output and can freeze the indoor coil into a block of ice.
2. **Dirty or blocked outdoor condenser coil** — leaves, grass clippings, or a bent fin mat traps heat and kills cooling.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common outdoor-unit failure. The compressor and/or fan hums, buzzes, or won't start.
4. **Tripped breaker or failed contactor** — no power to the outdoor unit means the fan spins inside but no cold air arrives.
5. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak** — gradual loss of cooling, ice on the copper lines, longer run times. Requires a pro.
6. **Frozen indoor evaporator coil** — often a downstream symptom of low airflow or low charge.
7. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor runs but the fan doesn't, causing a high-pressure shutdown.
8. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome, usually last on the list.
9. **Thermostat, wiring, or communicating-board fault** — miswired, dead thermostat, or a control fault on AccuLink systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Turn the thermostat to **Off** before inspecting anything.

- **Air filter:** Pull it. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. If it's a battery model, install fresh batteries.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets are dangerous.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply registers are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs, and that return grilles aren't covered.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the condenser coil. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance on all sides. Do not remove panels.
- **Condensate:** If the drain line is clogged, a safety float switch can shut cooling off. Check for standing water in the drain pan and clear a visible clog; drain tablets help prevent recurrence.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn cooling **Off** and run the fan only to thaw it before a tech visits. Never chip ice off.

If none of these restores cooling, it's a pro repair.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good technician follows a logical sequence — use this to check that a quote matches the actual work:

1. **Verify the complaint** — confirms setpoint, airflow, and measures supply vs. return temperature (a healthy split is usually about 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Confirm power and controls** — tests the contactor, 24V signal, and thermostat/communicating board for a call for cooling.
4. **Test the capacitor** — measures microfarads against the rating printed on the cap. A weak cap is a quick, cheap fix.
5. **Check the condenser fan and compressor** — verifies both energize and draw correct amperage.
6. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — measures pressures and superheat/subcooling to judge charge. Low charge means a **leak search**, not just a top-off.
7. **Read faults** — on AccuLink systems, pulls stored alerts from the control board.

Be cautious of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without airflow, capacitor, and leak testing first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Dirty filter / blocked returns | Replace filter, open vents | Inspect blower, coil, ductwork |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor fan runs | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low charge | Off, run fan to thaw | Diagnose airflow/charge, leak search |
| Gradual loss of cooling, longer runs | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Compressor runs, top fan dead | Failed condenser fan motor | Off, call pro | Replace fan motor/capacitor |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted motor/compressor | Stop resetting, call pro | Diagnose electrical fault |
| No response, thermostat blank/error | Thermostat or board fault | Replace batteries | Test thermostat/AccuLink board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included. Actual prices vary by region and by whether your system uses communicating (AccuLink) controls.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY).
- **Condensate drain clear:** $0 DIY – $150 pro.
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400.
- **Contactor:** $150–$350.
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700.
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600, depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A vs. legacy R-22 is far pricier).
- **Thermostat / control-board replacement:** $200–$800 (communicating boards cost more).
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+. On an older unit, replacing the whole system is often the smarter spend.

Ask for the specific failed part and its measured reading (e.g., capacitor microfarads, refrigerant pressures) so you can verify the diagnosis.

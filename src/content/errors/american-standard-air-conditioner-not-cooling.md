## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code. American Standard air conditioners are largely mechanical outdoor units controlled by your thermostat and indoor air handler or furnace — they don't display alphanumeric error codes the way a furnace board does. Some newer setups paired with an American Standard communicating thermostat (like the AccuLink / Platinum controls) will show a message such as a communication fault or a system lockout, but the classic split-system AC just runs, short-cycles, or blows warm air.

When your American Standard AC is "not cooling," it generally falls into one of three buckets: **airflow is restricted**, the **refrigerant charge is wrong (usually a leak)**, or an **electrical/mechanical component has failed** (capacitor, contactor, fan motor, or compressor).

Because diagnosing charge and electrical faults requires gauges, meters, and EPA certification, this problem is rated **pro** once you've ruled out the simple stuff below.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked return** — restricts airflow, freezes the indoor coil, and kills cooling output. Most common and cheapest.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — matted with grass, dust, or cottonwood; the unit can't reject heat.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs, so you feel room-temperature air.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — very common; the compressor or fan motor hums or won't start.
5. **Bad contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — gradual loss of cooling, ice on the lines, hissing. Requires leak repair, not just a "top-off."
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — result of low airflow or low charge; the coil turns to ice and blocks air.
8. **Failed condenser fan or blower motor** — no airflow across a coil means no heat transfer.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit runs but produces no cold, or trips on overload.
10. **Thermostat or control fault** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a communication error on AccuLink systems.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. If any require opening the sealed system or the electrical panel beyond the breaker, stop and call a pro.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling and freeze the coil.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor and outdoor breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — do not keep resetting it.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for the condenser coil clogged with grass, leaves, or debris. Gently rinse it with a garden hose (power off) from the inside out if you can safely reach it.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a float switch and shut the system down. Check for standing water and clear a visible clog.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to OFF but leave the fan ON to thaw it — then call a pro, because ice means airflow or charge problems.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, touch capacitors, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed HVAC tech should work systematically — this is how you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint and airflow** — measure supply/return temperature split (should be roughly 15–20°F). A low split points to charge or airflow.
2. **Inspect filter, coils, and blower** — confirm airflow isn't the root cause.
3. **Check electrical** — test the run capacitor with a meter, inspect the contactor, and confirm the compressor and fan motor draw correct amps.
4. **Attach refrigerant gauges** — read suction and liquid pressures and calculate superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct. Low charge means a **leak search**, not just adding gas.
5. **Leak detection** — electronic sniffer, dye, or nitrogen pressure test if refrigerant is low.
6. **Compressor test** — check windings and start behavior if the unit runs but won't cool.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without measuring the temperature split, capacitor, and pressures first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blower runs, air is room temp | Outdoor unit has no power | Reset breaker once; check outdoor disconnect | Test contactor, capacitor, wiring |
| Weak cooling, high energy bills | Dirty filter or coil | Replace filter, rinse condenser | Clean coils, verify airflow and charge |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn to fan-only to thaw, replace filter | Find/repair leak, recharge, check blower |
| Outdoor fan not spinning, humming | Failed capacitor or fan motor | Turn off, don't force fan | Replace capacitor or condenser fan motor |
| Unit runs constantly, never cools | Low charge or failing compressor | Nothing safe DIY | Pressure test, leak search, compressor test |
| System won't start at all | Tripped breaker, blown fuse, thermostat | Check batteries, breaker, mode | Test low-voltage circuit and control board |
| AccuLink shows comm/lockout message | Communication or control fault | Note the message, cycle power once | Diagnose communicating bus and controls |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Regional pricing and unit age vary widely.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Run capacitor replacement:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Blower motor:** $450–$900
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (higher for R-410A systems with large charge)
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often worth comparing to full-system replacement)
- **Full outdoor unit / system replacement:** $5,000–$12,000+ depending on capacity and efficiency

If the system is out of warranty and needs a compressor, get a second quote comparing repair vs. replacement — a compressor repair on an old R-22 or aging R-410A unit often isn't worth it.

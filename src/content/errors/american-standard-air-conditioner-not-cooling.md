## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code on the display — it's the symptom you notice: the American Standard condenser is running (or trying to), but the air from your vents is warm, room temperature, or not cold enough to satisfy the thermostat. On systems with a communicating thermostat (like the AccuLink / Nexia platform), you may see a system message or a fault stored on the control board, but the cause still has to be diagnosed at the equipment.

This page covers whole-system cooling failures across American Standard split systems. Because behavior varies by model and by the matched indoor unit (air handler or furnace with a matching coil), the exact diagnostic messages and lockout behavior differ. Where that's the case, we say so rather than guess.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops cooling capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" not "cool"), or a schedule/setpoint issue.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit or air handler loses power.
4. **Frozen indoor evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; blocks heat exchange entirely.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — very common; the compressor or fan motor hums but won't run properly.
6. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil can't reject heat, so cooling drops off, especially in extreme heat.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces capacity; the system may still run but never satisfies.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that sends power to the compressor and fan pits or sticks.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor or blower motor** — no airflow across a coil kills cooling.
10. **Compressor failure or control board fault** — least common but most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do — no panels off the live electrical, no refrigerant, no bypassing safeties:

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 cause and can freeze your coil.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool** and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery model.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water near the indoor unit or a full drain pan, the safety switch may have shut the system off. You can gently clear a visible clog at the pan; don't disassemble the unit.
- **Frozen coil / ice:** If you see ice on the refrigerant line or indoor coil, **turn the system to "off" and the fan to "on"** for a few hours to let it thaw, then run cool again. If it refreezes, you have a deeper problem.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work top-down, not just "add refrigerant." Expect them to:

1. **Confirm the complaint** — measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition.
3. **Verify power** — voltage at the disconnect, contactor, and control board; test the capacitor with a meter.
4. **Read the board/thermostat** — on communicating American Standard systems, pull any stored faults from the control or AccuLink thermostat.
5. **Take refrigerant readings** — connect gauges, check superheat/subcooling against the charging chart for that model.
6. **Leak-test if charge is low** — a proper diagnosis finds and fixes the leak, not just tops off refrigerant.
7. **Test motors and compressor** — amp draw, windings, and start behavior.

A quote that jumps straight to "you need refrigerant" without checking airflow, the capacitor, and the coils is a red flag.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, unit running | Low refrigerant / leak | Check & replace filter | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| No air at all indoors | Blower motor / breaker | Reset breaker once, check filter | Test blower, board, wiring |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Failed capacitor / fan motor | Turn off, don't force fan | Replace capacitor or fan motor |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low charge | Thaw with fan on, replace filter | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant |
| Unit hums, won't start | Bad capacitor / contactor | None — call pro | Replace capacitor/contactor |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Short / compressor fault | Do not keep resetting | Electrical/compressor diagnosis |
| Water near air handler | Clogged condensate drain | Clear visible pan clog | Clear drain, test float switch |
| Weak cooling in heat | Dirty condenser coil | Gently rinse outdoor coil off | Deep coil clean, pressure check |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor, varies by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$450
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$700
- **Blower motor:** $450–$1,200 (variable-speed ECM motors cost more)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,600 (depends on leak location and refrigerant type)
- **Control board:** $400–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often worth comparing to full system replacement on older units)

If your system uses R-22 (older units), refrigerant costs are much higher, and replacement may be more economical than repair.

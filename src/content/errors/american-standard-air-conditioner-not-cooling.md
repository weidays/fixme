## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Your American Standard air conditioner may be running, blowing air, and drawing power, but the air coming from your vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

On American Standard units with communicating controls (AccuLink / Platinum series with a Nexia-compatible thermostat), an actual fault may show as a numbered error on the thermostat display. On older Gold and Silver series with conventional controls, there's usually no code at all — you just feel warm air. Because the cause can be anything from a $20 filter to a failed compressor, "not cooling" is rated **pro**: the safe DIY checks are limited, and the most common real fixes involve electrical or refrigerant work.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, can freeze the indoor coil, and kills cooling capacity.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode (fan vs. cool), dead batteries, or a setpoint above room temperature.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower still runs.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — very common; the compressor or condenser fan can't start, so no cold air.
5. **Dirty condenser coil / blocked outdoor unit** — restricted heat rejection sharply cuts cooling.
6. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat exchange.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduces or eliminates cooling; requires a pro to find and repair.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't close.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — compressor overheats and shuts off on internal protection.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause; unit hums or trips but won't cool.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. If it's a battery model, replace the batteries.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter is the #1 easy fix.
- **Breaker:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the AC. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals a real fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from the condenser. Keep at least two feet of clearance. Do not remove panels or spray water into electrical components.
- **Condensate drain:** If your unit has a safety float switch and the drain is clogged, the system may shut off cooling. Check for standing water at the indoor unit's drain pan.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (leave the fan ON to help it thaw), and call a technician — running a frozen unit risks compressor damage.

Do **not** open sealed electrical compartments, discharge capacitors, add refrigerant, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work through a logical sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat call for cooling and low-voltage signal.
2. **Measure voltage** at the disconnect and contactor to confirm the outdoor unit is getting power.
3. **Test the capacitor** with a meter — a weak or bulged cap is a quick, cheap find.
4. **Check the contactor** for pitting and confirm it pulls in when there's a cooling call.
5. **Inspect and clean the condenser coil**, and confirm the condenser fan spins freely.
6. **Take temperature-split readings** (return vs. supply air) to gauge cooling performance.
7. **Attach gauges** to check refrigerant pressures and superheat/subcooling — this identifies low charge or a leak.
8. **Leak search** if the charge is low (electronic detector or dye) rather than just "topping off."
9. On communicating systems, **pull fault history** from the AccuLink control board.

Be wary of any tech who adds refrigerant without searching for the leak — that's a temporary, non-compliant fix.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check for frozen coil, duct issues |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker / contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, find trip cause |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed capacitor | None (electrical) | Test and replace run/start capacitor |
| Ice on coil or refrigerant lines | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn to OFF, fan ON to thaw | Find airflow/charge problem, leak search |
| House won't reach setpoint | Dirty condenser coil | Clear debris outside | Deep coil clean, verify charge |
| Nothing cold, no fault | Low refrigerant leak | None | Leak detection, repair, recharge |
| Compressor runs, no cooling | Failing compressor | None | Compressor test/replacement |
| Blank thermostat | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries | Check transformer, wiring |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor (vary by region and model):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Capacitor replacement:** $150–$450
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$700
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$2,000+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type (R-410A varies with market pricing)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often near the cost of a new outdoor unit — get a replacement-vs-repair opinion)

Note: On older R-22 systems, refrigerant costs and parts availability may make full replacement more economical.

## Related codes

- **American Standard high-pressure lockout** — often tied to dirty condenser coils or a failing fan.
- **American Standard low-pressure / loss of charge fault** — indicates a refrigerant leak.
- **AccuLink communication error (open/short)** — thermostat-to-board wiring fault on communicating systems.
- **Compressor short-cycling** — capacitor, contactor, or overload issues that also cause poor cooling.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a numeric fault code — it's the symptom you notice when your American Standard air conditioner runs (or tries to run) but your home never reaches the thermostat setpoint. On communicating **AccuLink** systems, the thermostat may show an actual alert or fault message; on standard 24V systems there's no code, just warm air.

The underlying problem falls into one of a few buckets: airflow restriction, a failed electrical component (capacitor, contactor, fan motor), low refrigerant from a leak, or a control/thermostat issue. Because several of the likely causes involve refrigerant or line-voltage electrical work, this is rated a **pro-level** repair once the basic homeowner checks are done.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow across the evaporator coil, reducing cooling and eventually causing the coil to ice over.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or (on AccuLink) a communication fault between thermostat and equipment.
3. **Dirty condenser or evaporator coil** — a caked outdoor coil can't reject heat; a dirty indoor coil chokes airflow.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor and/or condenser fan won't start or start weakly. Often the culprit when the outdoor fan hums but won't spin.
5. **Failed condenser fan motor or contactor** — the outdoor unit won't energize properly.
6. **Low refrigerant due to a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the lines, or long run times. Requires EPA-certified diagnosis and repair.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — a result of low airflow or low refrigerant; blows warm air until it thaws.
8. **Failed compressor** — least common but most expensive; the outdoor unit runs but produces no cold refrigerant.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only steps a homeowner should attempt. If none resolve it, stop and call a licensed HVAC tech.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and inspect. If it's gray, matted, or you can't see light through it, replace it with the correct size. Restart the system.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **COOL** and the setpoint is well below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. On AccuLink thermostats, note any error message on screen.
- **Breaker:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop — do not keep resetting.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture, rugs, or closed dampers.
- **Condensate drain / safety switch:** A clogged condensate line can trip a float switch and shut cooling down. If you see standing water in the drain pan, that's a sign to call a pro.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for obvious debris, leaves, or tall grass blocking the coil. Clear the area and gently rinse loose dirt off the fins with a garden hose (power off). Do **not** bend fins or open the electrical panel.

**Do not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or handle any high-voltage components.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measures supply and return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Electrical checks** — tests the capacitor with a meter, checks the contactor, fan motor, and compressor windings, and confirms 24V control voltage.
4. **AccuLink diagnostics** — on communicating systems, pulls fault history and confirms the thermostat, indoor, and outdoor units are communicating.
5. **Refrigerant measurements** — connects gauges, reads suction/liquid pressures, and calculates superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct or if there's a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if low, uses electronic detector or dye to locate the leak before recharging.

Be wary of any tech who "just adds refrigerant" without finding a leak — that's a temporary fix you'll pay for again.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm air, weak airflow | Dirty filter / iced coil | Replace filter; turn system to fan-only to thaw | Inspect coil, correct airflow or charge |
| Thermostat blank or unresponsive | Dead batteries / power loss | Replace batteries; check breaker | Test 24V transformer & wiring |
| Outdoor fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Contactor / breaker / capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, capacitor, wiring |
| Ice on refrigerant lines | Low refrigerant or low airflow | Replace filter; shut off to thaw | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Long run times, never cools | Low charge / dirty coils | Clean/clear outdoor coil area | Measure charge, clean coils |
| Outdoor unit runs, no cold air at all | Compressor failure | None — call pro | Test compressor, quote replacement |
| AccuLink shows comm/fault error | Control or wiring fault | Note the message | Diagnose communicating system |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor), varying by region and model:

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Coil cleaning (professional):** $150–$400
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,300–$2,800+ (often worth comparing against full system replacement on older units)

Prices for R-410A and older R-22 systems differ significantly — R-22 refrigerant is expensive and phased out, which can push older-unit repairs toward replacement.

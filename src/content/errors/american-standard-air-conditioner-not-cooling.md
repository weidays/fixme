## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code on American Standard equipment — it is the most common complaint homeowners describe when their system runs but the house stays warm. Depending on your model and control platform (AccuLink communicating systems, standard 24V thermostats, or newer touchscreen controls), the underlying problem may or may not display a diagnostic code.

There are two broad versions of this problem:

- **The outdoor unit runs but you get no cold air** — usually refrigerant, compressor, or airflow related.
- **Nothing runs at all** — usually power, thermostat, capacitor, or control-board related.

American Standard's higher-end communicating systems (Platinum series with a Nexia/AccuLink thermostat) can report specific fault information at the thermostat, while older Silver-series units give you no on-board readout. Because the exact reporting varies by model, this page focuses on the physical causes rather than promising a particular code will appear.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked indoor airflow** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor coil and stop cooling.
2. **Tripped breaker or lost power to the outdoor unit** — the condenser needs its own 240V circuit; a tripped breaker leaves the indoor fan blowing warm air.
3. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common outdoor-unit failure. The fan or compressor won't start, often with a humming sound.
4. **Dirty or blocked condenser coil** — the outdoor coil can't reject heat, so cooling capacity drops sharply.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, possible ice on the lines. Always indicates a leak that must be repaired.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit burns or sticks, so the compressor never gets voltage.
7. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — dead batteries, wrong mode, or a bad signal wire to the outdoor unit.
8. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low charge; the system ices over and stops cooling.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive cause. The unit runs but produces no cold air.
10. **Control board fault (communicating models)** — on AccuLink systems, a board or communication error can lock out cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop at anything beyond this list.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's a battery-powered model.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals a real electrical fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs, and that return grilles aren't obstructed.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for leaves, grass clippings, or debris packed against the condenser coil. Clear the area and gently rinse the coil with a garden hose (power off).
- **Condensate line/drain pan:** A full pan can trip a safety float switch and shut down cooling. If you see standing water, clear it or call for service.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully before running again.

Do not open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass any safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps locking out.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A licensed tech should work systematically. Knowing the steps helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — measure supply vs. return air temperature (a healthy split is roughly 15–20°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspect filter, coil, and blower operation.
3. **Confirm power and controls** — verify 240V at the condenser, test the capacitor and contactor, and check the thermostat call.
4. **Inspect the outdoor unit** — coil cleanliness, fan operation, compressor amperage.
5. **Read refrigerant pressures** — connect gauges to check charge and superheat/subcooling. Low pressures point to a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if charge is low, a reputable tech finds and fixes the leak rather than just topping off.
7. **Read fault data** — on Platinum/AccuLink communicating systems, pull codes from the thermostat or diagnostic tool.

Be cautious of any tech who adds refrigerant without discussing a leak, or condemns the compressor without amperage and electrical testing.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak or no cold air, unit runs | Dirty filter / airflow block | Replace filter, open vents | Check blower, static pressure |
| Indoor fan blows warm, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor, wiring |
| Outdoor fan hums but won't spin | Failed run capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, recharge, verify airflow |
| Cooling weak on hot days | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil with hose (power off) | Deep coil clean, check charge |
| Long runtime, never reaches setpoint | Low refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Leak search and repair, recharge |
| Unit runs, zero cooling, high amps | Failed compressor | None — call pro | Test and replace compressor |
| No response, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / control fault | Replace batteries | Check board, wiring, comms |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor. Regional rates and warranty coverage vary widely.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150–$500 (communicating AccuLink thermostats cost more)
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $300–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $900–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often approaching the cost of a new condenser)
- **Control board (communicating models):** $400–$900

If your compressor or coil fails and the system is over 10–12 years old, ask about replacement — a repair may not be worth it. Check whether your American Standard warranty still covers parts.

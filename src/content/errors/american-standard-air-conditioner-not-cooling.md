## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a specific fault code. On most American Standard single-stage and two-stage air conditioners (such as the Silver and Gold 4A7A series), the outdoor unit has no display, so there is no code to read — you just notice warm air or a system that never satisfies the thermostat.

On variable-speed **AccuComfort / Platinum** systems, the outdoor control board and a compatible communicating thermostat (like the AccuLink / Nexia line) **do** log diagnostic fault codes. If you have one of those, check the thermostat's alerts screen — it may point directly to the cause.

Because the term covers everything from a dirty filter to a dead compressor, the goal below is to rank the likely causes and help you tell a fair diagnosis from an overpriced one.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter or blocked airflow** — the most common and cheapest cause. Restricted airflow can freeze the indoor coil and stop cooling entirely.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a failed thermostat not calling for cooling.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit, indoor blower, or a low-voltage fuse on the control board may have tripped.
4. **Failed dual run capacitor** — extremely common on aging units; the fan may spin but the compressor won't start, or vice versa.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked with dirt, grass, or cottonwood can't reject heat, killing capacity.
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit gets pitted or stuck and stops sending power to the compressor.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes poor or no cooling and often a frozen coil. This is a leak repair, not a "recharge."
8. **Frozen indoor evaporator coil** — a downstream symptom of low airflow or low refrigerant.
9. **Failed compressor or condenser fan motor** — the most expensive outcomes, usually diagnosed last.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed refrigerant system or bypassing a safety device.

- **Filter:** Replace a dirty filter. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered and the screen is dim or blank.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside of the coil with a garden hose (power off) if it's visibly dirty.
- **Condensate:** A full drain pan or clogged condensate line can trip a safety switch and shut cooling down. Check for standing water at the indoor unit.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw for a few hours before running it again.

Do **not** repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping, and never add refrigerant or open the sealed system yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech follows a logical order. Use this to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat call, then confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor unit.
2. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition; a frozen coil gets thawed before further testing.
3. **Test electrical components** — measure the capacitor's microfarads against its rating and inspect the contactor for pitting or stuck contacts.
4. **Check the condenser fan and compressor** — confirm the fan spins and the compressor starts and draws proper amperage.
5. **Measure refrigerant** — take superheat/subcooling and pressure readings. Abnormal readings point to a leak or overcharge; a proper diagnosis includes **finding** the leak, not just topping off.
6. **Read fault codes** on variable-speed AccuComfort/Platinum boards via the communicating thermostat.

A fair quote names the specific failed part, the reading that proves it, and the repair cost. Be cautious of anyone who "just adds a couple pounds of Freon" without leak testing.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Confirm airflow, check blower |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test & replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit totally dead | Tripped breaker / bad contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, check wiring |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Cools poorly, long run times | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil exterior | Deep-clean coil, check charge |
| No cooling, higher power bill | Low refrigerant (leak) | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Loud hum, no start | Failing compressor | None — call pro | Amp test, replace compressor |
| Blank thermostat | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries | Check 24V, transformer, fuse |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Dual run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $150–$300
- **Condenser fan motor:** $350–$650 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (on older units, weigh against full replacement)
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $80–$180, often applied to the repair

Costs vary by region, refrigerant type (R‑410A vs. older R‑22), and whether parts are under American Standard's registered warranty. Register your unit and keep records — many parts carry a 10-year limited warranty.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it is a symptom. Your American Standard air conditioner is either not turning on, running without producing cold air, or short-cycling before the house cools down. Because American Standard uses several control platforms (basic single-stage boards on Silver-series units up through communicating variable-speed controls on Platinum-series systems), the way the system reports a problem varies.

Some newer American Standard units with communicating thermostats (like the AccuLink / Nexia-compatible controls) will display a text alert or a fault history in the thermostat menu. Older single-stage units give you no code at all — you diagnose by symptom. This page covers the symptom-based approach that applies across the lineup.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, causing weak cooling and eventually a frozen indoor coil. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat issue** — dead batteries, wrong mode (set to "fan" not "cool"), or a bad setting/schedule.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor condenser or the indoor air handler has lost power.
4. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — a coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat, so the house never cools.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a bulged or dead capacitor stops the compressor or fan motor from starting. Common on units 6+ years old.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes long run times, warm air, ice on the copper lines, or hissing. Requires a licensed tech.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks all cooling.
8. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay in the outdoor unit doesn't pull in, so the compressor never starts.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the outdoor fan doesn't spin, the unit overheats and shuts down on high-pressure limit.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive outcome; the unit hums but won't start, or trips the breaker instantly.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the temperature is set below room temperature, and the fan is on **Auto**. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A dirty filter is the #1 cause of weak cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check your electrical panel for a tripped breaker for the AC (often a double-pole breaker) and the air handler. Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed dampers aren't blocking airflow at the registers.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for grass clippings, leaves, or debris packed against the coil fins. Turn the disconnect off and gently clear obvious debris. Do not bend the fins or spray water into electrical parts.
- **Condensate drain:** If you see water pooling near the indoor unit or a full drain pan, the drain may be clogged and a safety float switch may have shut the system off. Clearing a visible clog is safe; anything deeper is a pro job.
- **Ice on the lines:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, **turn the AC off and set the fan to On** for a few hours to thaw it, then call a technician — running a frozen system causes more damage.

Never open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass a safety switch, or add refrigerant yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work through this so you can sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — confirm the thermostat is calling for cooling and check for any stored faults on communicating models.
2. **Check power and controls** — measure voltage at the disconnect, contactor, and 24V control circuit.
3. **Test the capacitor** — with a meter for microfarad value against the rating printed on it.
4. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower, coil condition, and static pressure.
5. **Read refrigerant pressures** — connect gauges to check suction and liquid pressures and measure superheat/subcooling. Low pressures point to a leak.
6. **Leak search** — if refrigerant is low, a real diagnosis includes finding the leak with electronic detector or dye, not just "topping off."
7. **Check the compressor and contactor** — for amp draw, resistance, and proper pull-in.

A good tech explains *why* the unit isn't cooling before recommending a repair. Be cautious of any quote to "add refrigerant" with no mention of finding the leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, slow cooling | Clogged filter / dirty coil | Replace filter, clear outdoor debris | Deep coil cleaning, blower inspection |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries, check breaker | Trace 24V control wiring, transformer |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor blows warm | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, control circuit |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed capacitor or compressor | None — call a pro | Test/replace capacitor or compressor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Find cause, check refrigerant charge |
| Long run times, never reaches setpoint | Low refrigerant / dirty coil | Check/clean filter and coil | Leak search, pressure test, recharge |
| Water near indoor unit, system off | Clogged condensate drain | Clear visible clog | Clear line, test float switch |
| Breaker trips instantly on start | Shorted compressor or wiring | Do not keep resetting | Electrical/compressor diagnosis |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10 – $30 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75 – $250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150 – $400
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400 – $700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500 – $1,500 (higher on R-410A vs. older systems, and more if the leak is in the coil)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000 – $2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500 – $2,500+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

Prices vary by region, unit tonnage, and refrigerant type. American Standard variable-speed Platinum units cost more to service due to their communicating boards and inverter compressors.

## Related codes

- **American Standard high-pressure lockout** — often tied to a dirty condenser coil or failed condenser fan.
- **American Standard low-pressure / loss-of-charge fault** — points to low refrigerant or a leak.
- **Frozen evaporator coil** — the physical result of low airflow or low charge.
- **Communicating system "loss of communication" alerts** — on Platinum/AccuLink systems, a control fault can present as no cooling; check the thermostat's fault history.

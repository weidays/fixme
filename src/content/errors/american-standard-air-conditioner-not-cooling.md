## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it is a symptom. American Standard air conditioners generally do not display alphanumeric error codes on the outdoor unit itself. Instead, the condenser either runs without producing cold air, short-cycles, or won't start at all. On systems paired with an AccuLink / ComfortLink II communicating thermostat, you may see a diagnostic message or a fault logged in the thermostat menu, but basic single-stage models give no on-screen code.

The core problem is that the system is failing to move heat out of your home. That can happen at the thermostat, the indoor blower, the outdoor condenser, or anywhere in the refrigerant circuit. This page helps you narrow it down and understand what a fair repair should cost.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops indoor coil temperature, and can lead to freezing and weak or no cool air.
2. **Thermostat set wrong or failing** — not in COOL, setpoint too high, dead batteries, or a communication fault on AccuLink systems.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler has no power.
4. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil is caked with dirt, grass, or cottonwood and can't reject heat.
5. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan motor won't start; you may hear humming.
6. **Failed or stuck contactor** — the relay that powers the compressor isn't closing.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — ice on the indoor coil from low airflow or low refrigerant blocks cooling.
8. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling capacity, ice, and eventually no cooling; requires a licensed tech.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — compressor runs hot and cuts out on overload.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the unit hums or trips but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do. Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, bypass any safety switch, or handle wiring under power.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This is the single most common cause.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is at least 3–5°F below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers:** Check the main panel and any outdoor disconnect. If a breaker is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated resets can destroy the compressor.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit airflow:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the condenser. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance on all sides.
- **Condensate drain / pan:** If your air handler has a full drain pan or clogged line, a float switch may have shut the system off. Check for standing water and clear a visible clog if you can reach it safely.
- **Look for ice:** If you see ice on the refrigerant line or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw fully before calling — running it frozen makes things worse.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech should work systematically. Use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Confirm the complaint** and check thermostat mode, setpoint, and call for cooling.
2. **Measure supply and return temperatures** — a healthy split is roughly 14–20°F.
3. **Check power and voltage** at the disconnect, contactor, and low-voltage circuit.
4. **Test the capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads.
5. **Inspect the contactor** for pitting or failure to pull in.
6. **Verify condenser fan and compressor operation** and amp draw against the nameplate.
7. **Attach gauges** to read suction and head pressures, then calculate superheat and subcooling. This is where they determine if refrigerant is low — and if so, **they must find the leak**, not just add refrigerant.
8. **Inspect coils** (indoor and outdoor) for dirt and airflow restrictions.
9. On **communicating AccuLink / ComfortLink systems**, pull stored fault codes from the thermostat.

A good tech explains the actual measured cause before quoting parts. Be cautious of anyone who "just adds a pound of Freon" without discussing a leak.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check ductwork, blower speed |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Failed capacitor or contactor | None | Test and replace capacitor/contactor |
| Unit hums but won't start | Failed capacitor or seized compressor | Kill power, call pro | Diagnose start components / compressor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Filter change, thaw with fan ON | Find leak, restore charge |
| Thermostat blank/unresponsive | Dead batteries or tripped fuse | Replace batteries | Check 24V transformer and fuse |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted motor or compressor fault | Reset once only, then stop | Locate electrical fault |
| Water leaking / system off | Clogged condensate line, float trip | Clear visible clog, treat drain | Clear line, test float switch |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Tripped disconnect or capacitor | Check disconnect | Verify power path to condenser |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Regional rates and system type (single-stage vs. variable-speed) affect the total.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Condensate line clearing:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak diagnosis:** $150–$450
- **Leak repair + recharge:** $600–$1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,900 (often worth comparing to full system replacement on older units)

Note: R-410A refrigerant pricing has climbed, and older R-22 systems are far more expensive to service — a factor worth weighing on any unit over 10–12 years old.

## Related codes

- **AC freezing up / iced coil** — often the precursor to a "not cooling" call.
- **AC won't turn on** — no power vs. a cooling-specific fault.
- **AC short cycling** — related electrical or refrigerant issues.
- **AccuLink / ComfortLink II thermostat fault codes** — check stored codes on communicating systems.
- **Condensate overflow / float switch trip** — a safety shutoff that mimics "not cooling."

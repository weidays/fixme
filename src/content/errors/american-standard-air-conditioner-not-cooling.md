## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single blinking error code — it's the most common complaint American Standard homeowners report, and it can mean several very different things. Your system may be running but blowing warm air, short-cycling on and off, or not turning on at all.

American Standard units with a communicating **AccuLink** control (common on Platinum and some Gold series) may store an actual fault code you can read at the thermostat. Single-stage Silver and older units usually have no code at all — you diagnose by symptom. Because of that, this page walks through causes by behavior rather than by a specific flash pattern.

The bottom line: cooling requires good airflow, a full refrigerant charge, and a working compressor circuit. When any one of those fails, you lose cooling. Refrigerant and compressor work require a licensed tech, which is why this is a **pro-level** problem.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or clogged evaporator coil** — restricts airflow, freezes the coil, and kills cooling. The single most common cause.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a wiring/comm fault (especially on AccuLink systems).
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — the outdoor unit or air handler loses power.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — the compressor or fan motor won't start; you may hear humming from the outdoor unit.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — warm air, ice on the copper lines, longer run times. Always a leak, never "used up."
6. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit is pitted or stuck, so the compressor never energizes.
7. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from airflow or refrigerant issues; the unit ices over and stops cooling.
8. **Condensate overflow safety switch tripped** — a full drain pan shuts the system down to prevent water damage.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the compressor overheats and shuts down on its internal protector.
10. **Failed compressor** — the least common but most expensive; often preceded by other faults.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything requires opening the sealed system or touching high-voltage components.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered. On AccuLink thermostats, note any fault code displayed.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and check it. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling.
- **Breakers:** Check the breaker panel for the air handler and the outdoor condenser (often a separate double-pole breaker). If one is tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure they're open and unblocked by furniture or rugs. Blocked airflow freezes coils.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from the condenser fins. Keep at least two feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** Check for standing water in the drain pan. A clogged drain can trip a float switch and shut the system down. You can gently clear the visible drain line, but don't disable the switch.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the copper refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON) and let it thaw for a few hours before restarting. Persistent icing means a real fault — call a tech.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, bypass a safety switch, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps tripping.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — this lets you sanity-check the quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** — confirms warm air, measures supply/return temperature split (should be roughly 16–22°F).
2. **Check airflow** — inspects filter, blower, and evaporator coil for restriction or ice.
3. **Read stored codes** — on AccuLink/communicating systems, pulls fault history from the control.
4. **Electrical checks** — tests capacitor microfarads, contactor operation, and voltage at the compressor and fan.
5. **Refrigerant measurement** — connects gauges, reads suction/head pressures and superheat/subcooling to determine if the charge is correct.
6. **Leak search** — if low on charge, uses an electronic detector or dye to find the leak *before* adding refrigerant.
7. **Compressor test** — checks amp draw, windings, and start behavior if the compressor won't run.

Be cautious of any tech who "tops off" refrigerant without finding a leak — that's a temporary fix that violates good practice and EPA rules.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blowing warm air, unit runs | Low refrigerant / leak | Check filter and vents | Find and repair leak, recharge |
| Ice on copper lines or coil | Airflow restriction or low charge | Replace filter, thaw with fan on | Diagnose airflow/refrigerant cause |
| Outdoor unit hums, won't start | Failed run capacitor | None — high voltage | Test and replace capacitor |
| Nothing runs at all | Tripped breaker or thermostat | Reset breaker once, check t-stat | Diagnose control/comm fault |
| Unit short-cycles | Low charge, dirty coil, control fault | Clean filter and condenser fins | Measure charge, test controls |
| System shuts off, water in pan | Clogged condensate drain | Clear visible drain line | Clear drain, reset/test float switch |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor dead | Contactor or capacitor failure | Check outdoor breaker | Test contactor and capacitor |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor combined. Warranty status matters — many American Standard units carry a 10-year parts warranty if registered, which can dramatically cut costs.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400–$700
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500 (depends on leak location)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,000
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (often better to weigh against a new system on older units)

If the unit is 12+ years old and facing a compressor or coil replacement, ask your tech to compare repair cost against a full system replacement.

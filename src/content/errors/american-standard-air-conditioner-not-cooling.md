## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a single fault code — it's a symptom. Your American Standard air conditioner may be blowing air that isn't cold, running constantly without lowering the temperature, or not starting the outdoor unit at all.

On systems with a standard thermostat, there is no error code to read — you diagnose by observing behavior. On systems with an **American Standard AccuLink / ComfortLink II communicating thermostat**, the thermostat may display a specific fault or system message that points to the problem. The exact codes and wording vary by thermostat model and firmware, so check your thermostat's on-screen diagnostics if you have one.

This is rated **pro** because the most common non-trivial causes — refrigerant leaks, failed compressors, and electrical faults in the outdoor unit — require licensed tools and EPA certification.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Clogged air filter** — restricts airflow, drops capacity, and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty outdoor condenser coil** — a coil packed with grass, dust, or cottonwood can't reject heat.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** to the outdoor unit — the indoor fan runs but the compressor doesn't.
4. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a wiring/communication fault.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — reduced cooling, hissing, or ice on the lines. Always a leak, never "used up."
6. **Failed run capacitor** — a common, inexpensive part that stops the compressor or fan motor from starting.
7. **Failed contactor** — the electrical relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't engage.
8. **Frozen indoor evaporator coil** — usually caused by #1 or #5; airflow of unconditioned air until it melts.
9. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; the outdoor unit hums but won't start, or trips the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything is beyond them.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool**, the fan to **Auto**, and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it uses them.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and check it against light. If it's gray or clogged, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Breakers:** Check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor unit breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals an electrical fault.
- **Supply and return vents:** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow.
- **Outdoor unit:** Look for blocked airflow — clear leaves, grass clippings, or debris from around the cabinet. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance.
- **Condensate drain:** A clogged drain can trigger a safety float switch that shuts the system down. Check the drain line and pan for standing water or blockage.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** but leave the fan **On** to thaw it (can take several hours). Don't run cooling again until it's fully thawed.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable order — you can use this to sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat settings and, on communicating systems, read any AccuLink/ComfortLink fault codes.
2. **Measure temperature split** across the indoor coil (return vs. supply air) — a healthy split is roughly 15–22°F.
3. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower, coil for ice or dirt.
4. **Check electrical** — voltage at the contactor, capacitor microfarad reading, and compressor amp draw.
5. **Connect refrigerant gauges** to read pressures and superheat/subcooling — this is how they confirm a low charge or a restriction.
6. **Leak search** if the charge is low — electronic detector or dye. A reputable tech won't just top off refrigerant without finding the leak.
7. **Compressor test** if the outdoor unit won't start — checking windings and start components.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace the compressor" without measuring pressures and airflow first.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air blows but isn't cold | Dirty filter or dirty condenser coil | Replace filter; rinse/clear coil area | Deep coil clean, verify charge |
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker, bad contactor, or capacitor | Reset breaker once | Test/replace contactor or capacitor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Replace filter, thaw with fan on | Find/repair leak, recharge |
| Runs constantly, never reaches setpoint | Undersized charge, dirty coil, hot day | Check filter and vents | Measure split, pressures, subcooling |
| Outdoor unit hums but won't start | Failed capacitor or compressor | None — call a pro | Replace capacitor; test compressor |
| System shuts off shortly after starting | Condensate float switch or overheating | Clear drain line | Diagnose safety lockout cause |
| No power to anything | Blown fuse, thermostat wiring | Check breaker and thermostat batteries | Trace low-voltage wiring/board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor. Prices vary by region and contractor.

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Breaker reset:** $0 (DIY, once)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,500+ depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator or condenser coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,200+ (often worth comparing against a full system replacement on older units)

If your unit is over 12–15 years old and needs a compressor or coil, ask for a replacement quote too — repairing an aging system with expensive refrigerant is often poor value.

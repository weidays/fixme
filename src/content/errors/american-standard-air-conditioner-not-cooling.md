## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific numeric fault on most American Standard air conditioners — it's the symptom you notice: the system runs (or tries to) but the air coming from your vents never gets cold, or the house never reaches the thermostat setpoint.

On older single-stage American Standard units there is no diagnostic display, so "not cooling" is what you see and hear. On **communicating systems** using the AccuLink or ComfortLink II thermostat, the control board may store a numeric fault (for example related to compressor, sensor, or communication errors) that points to the underlying cause. Because the exact codes vary by board generation, check your thermostat's fault/alert screen — but treat this page as covering the general "warm air" symptom.

The root cause is almost always one of a handful of things: airflow restriction, low refrigerant, an electrical component that won't energize the compressor or fan, or a frozen coil.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — the most common and cheapest cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Dirty or blocked outdoor condenser coil** — grass clippings, cottonwood, and dirt insulate the coil so it can't reject heat.
3. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common failure. The compressor or condenser fan hums or won't start; you may hear a click but no startup.
4. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes weak cooling, ice on the copper lines, and long run times. This is a pro repair, not a "top-off."
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — result of low airflow or low refrigerant; the unit ices over and stops cooling.
6. **Failed or stuck contactor** — the 24V relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't pull in, so the condenser never starts.
7. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — the outdoor unit gets no power.
8. **Thermostat problem or dead batteries** — wrong mode, wrong wiring, or a call for cooling that never reaches the equipment.
9. **Failed compressor** — the least common but most expensive. The condenser fan may run while the compressor is silent or trips the breaker.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should do without tools or training:

- **Air filter** — pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling.
- **Thermostat** — confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is below room temperature. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Breakers** — check both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor AC breakers in your panel. Flip a tripped breaker fully OFF, then ON, once. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Supply and return vents** — make sure interior vents aren't closed, blocked by furniture, or covered by rugs.
- **Outdoor unit** — look for tall grass, leaves, or debris packed against the condenser and clear it. Gently rinse the coil fins with a garden hose (power off first). Do not bend or force the fins.
- **Condensate line** — if you have a clogged drain safety switch, a full drain pan can shut the system down. Check for standing water and clear obvious clogs with the tablets listed above.
- **Ice check** — if you see ice on the copper line or indoor coil, turn the system to OFF and set the fan to ON to help it thaw. Do not run cooling on a frozen coil.

If cooling doesn't return after a filter change and 24 hours of thaw, it's time for a technician.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a repeatable process — use this to sanity-check any quote:

1. **Verifies the complaint** and checks the thermostat call and any stored fault codes (on communicating boards).
2. **Measures airflow and inspects the filter/coil** for restriction or ice.
3. **Checks line voltage and low-voltage (24V)** at the disconnect, contactor, and control board.
4. **Tests the capacitor** with a meter against its rated microfarads.
5. **Tests the contactor** for pull-in and pitted/welded contacts.
6. **Connects refrigerant gauges** and takes superheat/subcooling readings to judge the charge — this is where a leak shows up.
7. **Leak-searches** with electronic detector or dye if the charge is low.
8. **Checks the compressor** windings and amp draw if it won't start or trips.

Be wary of any quote that jumps straight to "add refrigerant" or "replace compressor" without gauge readings and a leak search — a properly sealed system shouldn't lose refrigerant.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm air, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Replace filter, clear vents | Verify airflow, inspect coil |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cold air | Low refrigerant / leak | None — call a pro | Gauge readings, leak search, seal & recharge |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Low airflow or low charge | Turn off, run fan to thaw | Diagnose airflow vs. refrigerant cause |
| Humming, won't start | Failed capacitor | None — shut off, call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Outdoor unit dead, indoor runs | Contactor / breaker / fuse | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, 24V circuit, fuse |
| Long run times, high bills | Dirty condenser coil | Rinse coil, clear debris | Deep coil clean, check charge |
| Breaker trips on cooling call | Compressor or wiring fault | Do not keep resetting | Amp/winding test, replace if failed |
| Nothing responds | Thermostat/wiring | Check mode, batteries | Verify wiring and control board |

## Repair costs

Honest US ballpark ranges (parts + labor; varies by region and contractor):

- **Air filter:** $10–$30 (DIY)
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $75–$250
- **Run/dual capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Low-voltage fuse / minor wiring:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $400–$1,500 depending on leak location
- **Refrigerant recharge only (with older R-22):** can exceed $600+ due to R-22 cost — often a sign it's time to consider replacement
- **Compressor replacement:** $2,000–$2,800+ (often worth weighing against a new unit)
- **Full system replacement:** $5,000–$12,000+

Capacitors and contactors are the "sweet spot" repairs — common, inexpensive, and quick. Refrigerant and compressor issues are where costs climb.

## Related codes

- **AC frozen / iced coil** — closely tied to airflow and refrigerant problems.
- **Outdoor unit not turning on** — often the contactor, capacitor, or breaker.
- **AccuLink / ComfortLink II communication fault** — on communicating systems, check the thermostat's numeric alert code for the specific subsystem.
- **AC short-cycling** — the unit starts and stops rapidly; can accompany low charge or a failing capacitor.

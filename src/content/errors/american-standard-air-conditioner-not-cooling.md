## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a numeric fault code — it's the symptom you're living with: the American Standard system runs (or tries to) but your home isn't getting cold. On units with the **AccuLink communicating control** (Platinum series with a matching communicating thermostat), the thermostat may actually display a specific alert or fault number, so check it. On conventional 24V setups (most Gold and Silver series with a standard thermostat), there's no code — you diagnose by behavior.

The condenser (outdoor unit) and the air handler or furnace coil (indoor unit) must both work together. Poor cooling can come from either side, from the refrigerant charge between them, or from airflow. Because refrigerant, electrical, and sealed-system work require tools and EPA certification, most true "not cooling" causes are **pro-level repairs**.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or blocked airflow** — restricts airflow, drops output, and can freeze the coil. Most common and fully DIY.
2. **Failed run/start capacitor** — the compressor or condenser fan won't start; you may hear a hum or clicking outside. Common on units 8+ years old.
3. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power while the indoor blower keeps running.
4. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; ice blocks heat transfer. You'll see little or warm airflow, sometimes ice on the outdoor line.
5. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — gradual loss of cooling, longer run times, ice, or hissing. Requires a certified tech.
6. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor unit can't reject heat; caused by grass, cottonwood, or dirt buildup.
7. **Contactor failure** — the outdoor contactor won't pull in, so the compressor and fan never start.
8. **Thermostat problem or wiring fault** — wrong mode, dead batteries, or a bad C-wire, especially after a thermostat swap.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — compressor runs hot and shuts down on internal overload.
10. **Compressor failure** — the worst case; expensive and often means considering replacement on older units.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner. Stop if anything is beyond them.

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to COOL and the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Air filter:** Pull it and check. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. A clogged filter alone can stop cooling and freeze the coil.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped AC breaker (indoor and outdoor circuits). Reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — do not keep resetting.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply and return vents are open and not blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris away from the condenser coil. Keep at least 2 feet of clearance. Do not remove panels.
- **Condensate drain:** If cooling stopped and you have a full drain pan or a tripped float switch, clear the visible drain line opening. Standing water can shut the system off on many setups.
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to OFF (fan ON is okay) and let it thaw for a few hours before running again.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech works from cheap to expensive — a quote that jumps straight to "compressor" without these steps deserves a second opinion:

1. **Verify the complaint and airflow** — checks filter, blower operation, and temperature split across the indoor coil (typically 15–20°F drop).
2. **Read any AccuLink/thermostat alerts** on communicating systems.
3. **Check power and controls** — confirms 24V call, contactor pull-in, and line voltage at the outdoor unit.
4. **Test the capacitor** with a meter for microfarad rating within spec.
5. **Inspect the condenser coil** and fan motor operation.
6. **Attach refrigerant gauges** and read pressures/superheat/subcooling to judge charge — this is where a leak or overcharge shows up.
7. **Leak search** with an electronic detector or dye if the charge is low.
8. **Amp-test the compressor** and check windings if start components are ruled out.

Ask for the measured readings. Pressures, capacitor value, and temperature split are objective and should back up any diagnosis.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Verify blower/coil condition |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Find leak, recharge, check airflow |
| Outdoor unit silent, indoor runs | Tripped breaker or contactor | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, wiring, capacitor |
| Humming, fan won't spin | Failed capacitor | None — call pro | Test and replace capacitor |
| Gradual loss of cooling | Refrigerant leak | None — call pro | Leak search, repair, recharge |
| Outdoor coil caked with dirt | Dirty condenser | Rinse gently with hose (power off) | Deep coil cleaning |
| No response at all | Thermostat / power | Check mode, batteries, breaker | Diagnose control wiring / board |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Electrical fault or bad compressor | Stop resetting; call pro | Electrical + compressor testing |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges including parts and labor:

- **Air filter:** $10 – $40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat replacement:** $150 – $400
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150 – $350
- **Contactor:** $150 – $350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $400 – $700
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100 – $300
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $600 – $1,600 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000 – $2,500
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500 – $2,800+ — on older units, weigh this against full replacement

Diagnostic/service call fees typically run $80 – $200 and are often credited toward the repair.

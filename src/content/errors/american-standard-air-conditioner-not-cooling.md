## What this code means

"Not cooling" is a symptom, not a single fault code. On most American Standard single-stage air conditioners, there is no display to report it. The system runs, or tries to run, but the house does not get cooler.

Higher-end variable-speed and communicating systems are different. Models paired with AccuLink-style controls may log specific alerts at the thermostat or on the outdoor control board. Examples include high/low pressure, compressor, or communication faults. The exact codes and meanings vary by model. Check the service facts sheet for your unit, which is usually found inside the outdoor unit's access panel.

Whether the unit recovers on its own also depends on the model. Protective trips, such as a pressure switch or a compressor's internal overload, may reset automatically after the unit cools down. Some communicating controls hold a lockout until power is cycled.

This issue is rated **pro** because most of the likely causes are electrical or refrigerant problems inside the cabinet.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty air filter or restricted airflow.** This reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat setting or batteries.** Examples: the thermostat is set to Heat or Off, the batteries are dead, or the setpoint is above room temperature.
3. **Tripped breaker or outdoor disconnect switched off.** When this happens, the indoor fan runs but the outdoor unit does not.
4. **Failed run capacitor.** This is one of the most common outdoor-unit failures. The compressor or condenser fan hums but won't start.
5. **Failed or pitted contactor.** The outdoor unit doesn't engage even though the thermostat is calling for cooling.
6. **Dirty or blocked outdoor condenser coil.** This causes high head pressure, poor cooling, and possibly a high-pressure trip.
7. **Low refrigerant from a leak.** Symptoms include an iced coil or lines, long run times, and warm air. Evaporator coil leaks are a known issue across the industry, including some American Standard/Trane coils.
8. **Clogged condensate drain tripping a float switch.** Where a float switch is installed, it shuts off cooling.
9. **Condenser fan motor failure.**
10. **Compressor failure or control board fault.** Communicating and variable-speed models report these through alerts.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Confirm it is set to Cool, the setpoint is several degrees below room temperature, and the fan is set to Auto. Replace the batteries if the display is blank or faint.
- **Air filter:** Pull it out and replace it if it is gray or clogged.
- **Breakers and disconnect:** Check both AC breakers in the panel and the outdoor disconnect.
  - If a breaker has tripped, reset it **once**.
  - If it trips again, leave it off and call a technician.
- **One reset:** If the system appears locked out, turn it off at the thermostat. Wait 5 minutes, then restore cooling. Do this only once.
- **Vents and registers:** Make sure supply registers are open and return grilles are not blocked by furniture.
- **Outdoor unit surroundings:** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around the unit, keeping about 2 feet of clearance. Do not open the unit.
- **Ice:** If you see ice on the lines or coil, set the thermostat to Off and the fan to On until the ice fully melts.
- **Condensate line:** Turn the system off at the thermostat first. Then check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch near the indoor unit.
  - Clear the end of the drain line.
  - If your system has an access port, you can flush it with a cup of distilled vinegar.
  - If your system uses a condensate pump, do not pour anything into the line unless the pump manufacturer allows it.
- **Panels:** Make sure the indoor unit's access panels are fully seated.

## How a technician will diagnose it

- Verify there is a 24V cooling call from the thermostat at the indoor and outdoor units.
- Check line voltage at the disconnect and contactor, and inspect the contactor contacts.
- Test the run capacitor with a meter against its µF rating.
- Measure compressor and fan motor amp draw. If the compressor won't start, check its windings.
- Read stored alerts on communicating or variable-speed controls.
- Connect gauges or probes to measure superheat and subcooling against the charging chart. This confirms whether the charge is correct.
- If the charge is low, leak-search with an electronic detector, bubbles, or nitrogen.
- Measure the temperature difference between return and supply air, which is typically 15–20°F. Check static pressure and blower operation.
- Inspect and clean the outdoor coil, and check the condensate safety switch.

Be skeptical of a quote that recommends "just add refrigerant" without any leak search. Be equally skeptical of a compressor replacement recommended without amp-draw and winding tests.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indoor fan runs, outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker, failed contactor, or no 24V call | Check thermostat mode; reset breaker once | Test voltage and replace contactor or repair wiring |
| Outdoor unit hums, fan won't spin | Failed run capacitor or fan motor | Turn system off to avoid damage | Test and replace capacitor or motor |
| Ice on lines or indoor coil | Dirty filter, low airflow, or low refrigerant | Set to Off/Fan On; replace filter | Check airflow, charge, and leaks |
| Runs constantly, air slightly cool | Dirty condenser coil or low charge | Clear debris around outdoor unit | Clean coil; measure superheat/subcooling |
| AC shuts off, water near indoor unit | Clogged condensate drain or float switch | Turn system off; clear drain line; empty pan | Clear trap; repair pan or switch |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Shorted compressor or motor, or wiring fault | Leave breaker off | Electrical diagnosis; component replacement |
| Alert shown on communicating thermostat | Model-specific fault | Note the code; reset power once | Diagnose per service facts |

A warranty can sharply reduce parts costs. Many American Standard units carry a 10-year registered parts warranty, and some compressors carry longer coverage. Labor is usually not covered.

## Related codes

The published American Standard pages, 2 Flashes through 9 Flashes, cover furnace flash codes. They describe furnace or air handler problems, such as limit circuit, ignition, and control faults. In a split system, the furnace or air handler moves the air during cooling, so a fault there can affect indoor airflow and cooling performance.

If your system uses a gas furnace as the indoor blower, check its LED status light. If the LED shows any fault code, look it up on the wiring/diagnostic label on your furnace door. Flash-code meanings vary between American Standard furnace control boards, so always go by the label on your own unit.

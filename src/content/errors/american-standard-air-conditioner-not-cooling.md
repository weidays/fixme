## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it's a symptom. Your American Standard air conditioner may be running (indoor blower and/or outdoor fan spinning) yet the air coming from your vents is warm, lukewarm, or barely cool. On communicating systems using an AccuLink or ComfortLink thermostat, you may see an actual error code or a "waiting" / "system fault" message; on older single-stage units there's typically no code at all, just poor performance.

Because "not cooling" spans everything from a clogged filter to a dead compressor, the goal below is to help you rule out the cheap, safe causes yourself — then understand what a technician should be checking so you can sanity-check any quote.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricts airflow, freezes the evaporator coil, and kills cooling.
2. **Thermostat problem** — wrong mode/setting, dead batteries, or a miswired/failing communicating stat.
3. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow (dirty filter, closed vents) or low refrigerant.
4. **Failed run capacitor** — a very common outdoor-unit failure; the compressor or fan won't start.
5. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil caked in dirt/grass can't reject heat.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — the system slowly loses cooling capacity. Requires a licensed pro.
7. **Failed condenser fan motor** — outdoor unit hums but fan doesn't spin; unit overheats and shuts down.
8. **Tripped breaker or blown fuse** — outdoor disconnect or panel breaker off.
9. **Contactor failure** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit fails to close.
10. **Failed compressor** — the most expensive outcome; often the last thing confirmed after ruling out the above.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are all homeowner-safe. **Do not** open the outdoor unit's electrical panel, touch the capacitor, or handle refrigerant lines.

- **Filter:** Pull your air filter. If it's gray or you can't see light through it, replace it. This alone fixes a large share of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool** and the target is below room temperature. Replace batteries if it's battery-powered. If the screen is blank, that's your lead.
- **Breaker:** Check both the AC breaker in your main panel and the outdoor disconnect box near the unit. If a breaker tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro — repeated tripping signals a real electrical fault.
- **Vents and returns:** Make sure supply vents are open and return grilles aren't blocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Outdoor unit:** Clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser. Gently rinse the outside coil fins with a garden hose (power off).
- **Frozen coil:** If you see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil, turn the system to **Off** and the fan to **On** to thaw it (can take a few hours). Replace the filter before restarting.
- **Condensate drain:** A full drain pan or clogged line can trip a float switch and shut cooling down. Check for standing water near the indoor unit.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech should work systematically, not jump to "you need a new compressor." Expect them to:

1. Verify thermostat call for cooling and confirm 24V control signal reaches the outdoor unit.
2. Check the air filter and evaporator coil for ice/dirt, and measure airflow.
3. Test the **capacitor** with a meter and inspect the **contactor** for pitting or failure to pull in.
4. Confirm the **condenser fan motor** and **compressor** are drawing correct amperage.
5. Attach gauges to read **refrigerant pressures** and take superheat/subcooling measurements — the key test for a leak or undercharge.
6. On communicating (AccuLink/ComfortLink) systems, pull fault history from the control board.

If a tech quotes a compressor or refrigerant job without showing you gauge readings or amperage measurements, ask for that data before approving.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Dirty filter | Replace filter | Check coil, blower |
| Blank thermostat | Dead batteries / power | Replace batteries | Test control wiring/transformer |
| Ice on lines or coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw, replace filter | Check charge, find leak |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker/disconnect | Reset breaker once | Test contactor, capacitor, wiring |
| Outdoor fan won't spin, humming | Failed capacitor or fan motor | None — do not open panel | Test/replace capacitor or motor |
| Outdoor coil filthy | Dirty condenser | Rinse fins with hose (power off) | Deep coil cleaning |
| Cools poorly over weeks | Refrigerant leak | None | Leak search + EPA-certified recharge |
| Breaker trips repeatedly | Electrical/compressor fault | Stop resetting; call pro | Diagnose short/compressor draw |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges (parts + labor; regional variation applies):

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries / setting fix:** $0–$15 (DIY)
- **Run capacitor:** $150–$400 installed
- **Contactor:** $150–$350 installed
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650 installed
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $500–$1,800 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **TXV / metering device:** $500–$900
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$2,500+ (often makes replacing an older unit more sensible)
- **Full system replacement:** $5,000–$12,000+

Note: newer American Standard units may use R-454B refrigerant, which affects recharge cost and parts availability. Confirm your refrigerant type before approving a recharge quote.

## Related codes

- **Frozen evaporator coil** — often the underlying cause behind "not cooling."
- **Outdoor unit not starting** — overlaps with capacitor and contactor failures above.
- **Short cycling** — a related complaint that can accompany low refrigerant or airflow problems.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" is not a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint homeowners report about American Standard central air conditioners. The system may be running (blower on, thermostat calling) but the air coming from the vents isn't cold, or the house never reaches the setpoint.

On American Standard units with a **communicating (AccuLink / Nexia) thermostat**, an actual fault may be logged that points to a component. On older or non-communicating systems there is no error display, so the diagnosis relies on physical inspection and gauge readings. If your thermostat is showing a specific message or number, note it — that narrows the problem considerably. This page covers the general "no/weak cooling" condition.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause. Restricted airflow reduces cooling and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a wiring/communication fault with the outdoor unit.
3. **Dirty outdoor (condenser) coil** — caked with dust, grass, or cottonwood, the unit can't reject heat.
4. **Frozen evaporator (indoor) coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or coil.
5. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common electrical failure; the compressor or fan hums but won't start.
6. **Tripped breaker or blown low-voltage fuse** — outdoor unit gets no power.
7. **Low refrigerant due to a leak** — causes weak cooling and often coil freezing. Requires a licensed tech.
8. **Failed contactor** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit doesn't close.
9. **Clogged condensate drain / float switch tripped** — a safety switch may cut cooling to prevent water overflow.
10. **Failed compressor** — the worst case; the outdoor unit runs the fan but produces no cooling.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are safe for any homeowner to do:

- **Check the air filter.** If it's gray or clogged, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Check the thermostat.** Confirm it's set to COOL, the setpoint is below room temperature, and the fan is on AUTO. Replace the batteries if it's battery-powered.
- **Check the breakers.** Both the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor (condenser) breakers. Reset a tripped breaker **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Look at the outdoor unit.** Clear leaves, grass, and debris from around it. Make sure the fan on top is spinning when the system calls for cooling.
- **Check all supply and return vents.** Make sure furniture, rugs, or closed registers aren't blocking airflow.
- **Look for ice.** If the copper refrigerant line or indoor coil is frosted over, turn the system to OFF and run the FAN only for a few hours to thaw it, then call a technician.
- **Check the condensate drain.** If there's standing water in the drain pan or near the indoor unit, the float safety switch may have shut cooling off. Clearing the drain is DIY; a chronically clogging drain needs a pro.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, remove electrical panels on live equipment, or repeatedly reset a unit that keeps shutting down.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A qualified tech will typically:

1. **Verify the thermostat call** and confirm 24V is reaching the outdoor contactor.
2. **Measure the capacitor** with a meter (microfarad rating) — a weak cap is a fast, cheap find.
3. **Check the contactor** for pitted or non-closing contacts.
4. **Inspect airflow** — filter, blower operation, and coil condition — since low airflow mimics low refrigerant.
5. **Attach gauges and take temperatures** to measure superheat and subcooling. This tells them if refrigerant charge is correct or if there's a leak.
6. **Perform a leak search** (electronic detector, dye, or nitrogen pressure test) if the charge is low.
7. **Read stored faults** on communicating (AccuLink) systems via the thermostat or a service tool.
8. **Test the compressor** for windings and amp draw if all else checks out.

A quote should identify a *specific* part or condition. "It's low on refrigerant, we topped it off" without a leak search is a red flag — refrigerant doesn't get used up; it leaks.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak/warm air, dirty filter | Restricted airflow | Replace filter | None if that fixes it |
| No cold air, thermostat blank | Dead batteries / no power | Replace batteries, check breaker | Trace low-voltage fault |
| Outdoor fan runs, no cooling | Failed capacitor or compressor | None (electrical) | Test & replace capacitor/compressor |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Set to FAN to thaw, replace filter | Leak search, recharge, airflow fix |
| Outdoor unit dead | Tripped breaker/contactor | Reset breaker once | Replace contactor, find cause |
| Water near indoor unit, no cooling | Clogged drain / float switch | Clear drain line | Clear/repair drain, reset switch |
| Runs constantly, never cools | Low refrigerant or dirty coil | Clean outdoor coil, replace filter | Leak repair, recharge |
| Humming, won't start | Capacitor or contactor | None | Test & replace electrical part |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $15–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor replacement:** $150–$350
- **Condenser coil cleaning:** $100–$250
- **Refrigerant leak search + repair:** $500–$1,500 (higher if the leak is in the coil)
- **Evaporator coil replacement:** $1,000–$2,500+
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,500–$2,500+ (may be covered under American Standard's parts warranty if registered — you still pay labor)
- **Full outdoor condenser replacement:** $3,500–$7,000+

If your unit is under 10 years old, check your **American Standard registered limited warranty** before authorizing a compressor or coil replacement — the part may be free.

## Related codes

- **AC frozen / iced-over coil** — often the underlying cause of weak cooling.
- **AC won't turn on** — no power vs. no cooling are different starting points.
- **AccuLink communication fault** — on communicating systems, a lost link can stop cooling.
- **Condensate float switch tripped** — a common hidden cause of a system that "just quit."
- **Capacitor failure** — a frequent electrical culprit behind a humming, non-starting condenser.

## What this code means

"Not cooling" isn't a specific fault code — it's the most common complaint homeowners report about American Standard air conditioners. Your thermostat calls for cooling, but the air coming from the vents is warm, weak, or the house never reaches the setpoint.

On American Standard communicating systems (AccuLink / Platinum control), the thermostat may display a fault or system message pointing to a specific problem. On conventional 24V systems there's no display code — you diagnose by symptom. Because the underlying causes range from a $20 filter to a failed compressor, this page walks you through what's safe to check and what needs a pro.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged air filter** — the single most common cause of weak cooling. A choked filter starves airflow and can freeze the indoor coil.
2. **Thermostat problem** — dead batteries, wrong mode/setting, or a miscommunicating AccuLink thermostat.
3. **Tripped breaker or disconnect** — the outdoor unit has its own breaker and a disconnect box; either can trip and stop the condenser.
4. **Failed run/start capacitor** — a very common outdoor failure. The compressor or fan won't start, so the unit hums or runs without cooling.
5. **Frozen evaporator coil** — from low airflow or low refrigerant; you'll see ice on the copper lines or indoor coil.
6. **Low refrigerant from a leak** — causes gradual loss of cooling. Requires a certified tech to locate and repair.
7. **Dirty condenser coil** — the outdoor coil packed with dirt, grass, or cottonwood can't reject heat.
8. **Contactor failure** — the relay that powers the outdoor unit gets pitted or stuck, so the condenser won't energize.
9. **Failed condenser fan motor** — the outdoor fan doesn't spin, the unit overheats and shuts down on high-pressure limit.
10. **Compressor failure** — the most expensive outcome; the heart of the outdoor unit no longer pumps refrigerant.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

These are the only checks a homeowner should attempt. Stop if anything is unclear.

- **Air filter:** Pull it and hold it to the light. If you can't see through it, replace it. This alone fixes a surprising number of "not cooling" calls.
- **Thermostat:** Confirm it's set to **Cool** and the setpoint is below room temperature. If it's a battery model, swap in fresh batteries. On AccuLink thermostats, note any on-screen message.
- **Breakers:** Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker to the indoor (air handler/furnace) and outdoor unit. Flip it fully off, then on — **once**. If it trips again, stop and call a pro.
- **Outdoor disconnect:** Make sure the pull-out disconnect box near the condenser is fully seated.
- **Vents and registers:** Confirm supply and return vents are open and unblocked by furniture or rugs.
- **Condensate:** If the unit shut off, check for a full drain pan or a tripped float switch. You can clear a visible clog and pour condensate tablets in the drain line, but don't bypass the float switch.
- **Ice check:** If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or coil, turn the system to **Off** and set the fan to **On** to thaw it (can take several hours), then call a tech — icing means a real problem.

Do **not** open the sealed refrigerant system, add refrigerant, or bypass any safety switch.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A good tech follows a logical sequence — knowing it helps you sanity-check a quote:

1. **Verify the complaint** and check thermostat call, mode, and any communicating fault codes.
2. **Confirm airflow** — inspect filter, blower operation, and indoor coil for ice or dirt.
3. **Check outdoor power** — voltage at the disconnect, contactor operation, and capacitor microfarad reading with a meter.
4. **Test motors** — condenser fan and compressor amp draw against nameplate ratings.
5. **Connect refrigerant gauges** — measure suction/liquid pressures, superheat and subcooling to judge the charge and find restrictions.
6. **Leak-search** if the charge is low — electronic sniffer, UV dye, or bubble test.
7. **Inspect the compressor** — windings, resistance, and start behavior if it won't run.

Ask the tech to show you the capacitor reading, pressure numbers, or amp draw. Reputable pros will explain their findings before quoting a repair.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak airflow, warm air | Clogged filter | Replace filter | Check blower & duct static |
| Ice on lines/coil | Low airflow or low refrigerant | Turn off, thaw with fan | Find leak, recharge, check airflow |
| Outdoor unit silent | Tripped breaker/disconnect | Reset once; reseat disconnect | Trace electrical fault |
| Unit hums, won't start | Failed capacitor | None | Test & replace capacitor |
| Fan spins, no cold air | Low refrigerant / bad compressor | None | Gauge system, leak search |
| Outdoor fan not spinning | Fan motor or capacitor | None | Replace motor/capacitor |
| Thermostat blank | Dead batteries / power | Replace batteries | Check 24V transformer & wiring |
| Water/shut-off cycling | Clogged condensate drain | Clear drain, add tablets | Clear line, test float switch |
| Warm air, dirty outdoor coil | Blocked condenser | Clear debris, gentle rinse | Deep coil cleaning |

## Repair costs

Honest US ranges, parts and labor included:

- **Air filter:** $10–$40 (DIY)
- **Thermostat batteries:** $5–$15 (DIY)
- **Run/start capacitor:** $150–$400
- **Contactor:** $150–$350
- **Condenser fan motor:** $300–$650
- **Condensate drain clearing:** $75–$250
- **Coil cleaning (condenser or evaporator):** $150–$500
- **Refrigerant leak repair + recharge:** $200–$1,500 depending on leak location and refrigerant type
- **Compressor replacement:** $1,200–$1,800+, often close to the cost of a new condenser
- **Diagnostic/service call:** $75–$200, frequently applied toward the repair

Note: older units using R-22 refrigerant cost far more to recharge, since R-22 is phased out. If your unit is 12+ years old and needs a compressor or major refrigerant work, ask about replacement.

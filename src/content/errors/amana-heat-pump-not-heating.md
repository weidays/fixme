## What this code means

"Not heating" is a symptom, not a single fault code. Amana heat pumps don't show one dedicated "no heat" code. The outdoor unit may flash a diagnostic LED code, or it may show nothing at all. Common no-heat situations include:

- The system runs but blows cool air.
- The outdoor unit doesn't start.
- The outdoor coil stays iced over.
- Only the backup heat strips are doing the work.

**How it reports a problem depends on your model and control platform:**

- **Conventional 24V models (such as ASZ13, ASZ14 and ASZ16):** These use a defrost control board. It may flash LED codes for pressure-switch or sensor faults.
- **ComfortNet communicating models (the earlier communicating platform, used on some ASZC and AVZC units):** These store fault codes on the outdoor control. Fault display and diagnostic tools differ from ComfortBridge.
- **ComfortBridge communicating models (the newer platform, used on some later ASZC and AVZC units):** These store detailed fault codes on the outdoor control board.

Which communicating platform you have depends on the unit's vintage. Check the model plate on your outdoor unit and the installation paperwork. If you're unsure, a technician can confirm it.

If a specific code is flashing, note it before you do anything else. A technician will need it.

**Auto-reset or lockout:** This varies by model.

- Many pressure-switch trips auto-retry after a delay.
- Repeated trips can cause a "soft lockout." This holds until power is cycled.

Allow at most one power reset. If heat doesn't return, stop and call a technician.

**Furnace-paired systems:** If your heat pump is paired with an Amana gas furnace (dual fuel) and the furnace LED is flashing, the problem may be the furnace itself. See the related furnace codes listed at the end of this page.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Thermostat set wrong or not calling for heat.** Common culprits are the wrong mode, a schedule override, dead batteries, or the thermostat being configured as a conventional system instead of a heat pump. A conventional setup sends the wrong reversing-valve signal (O/B), so the unit cools when it should heat.
2. **Normal heat pump behavior mistaken for a fault.** This includes defrost cycles, lukewarm supply air, and long run times in cold weather.
3. **Restricted airflow.** A clogged filter, closed registers, or a blocked return reduces heat output. It can also trip the high-pressure safety in heating mode.
4. **Outdoor coil iced over.** This can come from a failed defrost control board, a bad coil or defrost sensor, a defrost thermostat problem, or poor drainage around the unit.
5. **Failed capacitor or contactor.** The outdoor fan or compressor won't start. The indoor blower may still run cool air. In practice this is one of the most frequent no-heat repairs.
6. **Low refrigerant charge (a leak).** The unit runs but produces weak heat. It may trip the low-pressure switch. On some models it also contributes to icing.
7. **Reversing valve stuck or its solenoid coil failed.** The unit runs in cooling mode when heat is called for.
8. **Outdoor fan motor failure.** The compressor trips on high head pressure, or the coil can't absorb heat.
9. **Compressor failure.** This is the least common cause, and the most expensive.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thermostat:** Set it to HEAT, with the setpoint at least 3°F above room temperature. Replace the batteries if the display is dim or blank. If the thermostat was recently replaced, the installer should confirm it's set up as a heat pump.
- **Wait out defrost:** If the outdoor unit is steaming or the fan has stopped while the compressor hums, wait 10–15 minutes before judging.
- **Air filter:** Replace it if it's dirty. Make sure all supply registers and return grilles are open and unblocked.
- **Breakers and disconnects:** Check the breakers for both the indoor air handler and the outdoor unit. Also check the outdoor disconnect switch near the condenser. Reset a tripped breaker only once. If it trips again, leave it off and call a pro.
- **One power reset:** Turn the thermostat off. Switch off both breakers for about 5 minutes, then restore power and call for heat. Do this only once.
- **Outdoor unit, from the outside only:**
  - Clear snow, leaves, and debris to give at least 18–24 inches of clearance.
  - Make sure meltwater can drain away from the base.
  - Don't chip ice off the coil. You can damage the fins and tubing.
- **Condensate:** If your air handler has a float or overflow switch, make sure the condensate drain and pan aren't backed up. A tripped float switch can shut the system off.
- **Exterior panels:** Confirm the air handler access panel is fully seated. If your air handler has a door switch, a loose panel can keep the unit from running.
- **Stay warm while you wait:** If your system has backup heat, switch the thermostat to EM HEAT until service arrives. If you have no heat strips or undersized ones, EM HEAT may give little or no heat. In a dual-fuel system, EM HEAT runs the furnace, which won't help if the furnace itself is faulted.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent technician will typically work through these steps:

1. **Read fault codes.** They check the outdoor control board LED on conventional units. On communicating units they read the fault history, using the method that matches the platform (ComfortNet or ComfortBridge).
2. **Verify thermostat signals.** They confirm the thermostat is sending the correct calls at the air handler and outdoor unit. On conventional systems these are the 24V signals (Y, O/B, W, G).
3. **Test start components.** They check the contactor, the run/dual capacitor (measured against its microfarad rating), and the fan motor amperage.
4. **Check the reversing valve.** They confirm the solenoid coil is getting power. They also measure temperatures across the valve to see if it's stuck.
5. **Measure system pressures and temperatures.** Readings like superheat and subcooling show whether the refrigerant charge is low. If the charge is low, they should leak-search rather than just "top off."
6. **Check defrost controls.** They test the defrost sensor resistance against Amana's chart and may force a test defrost from the board.
7. **Measure airflow.** They check temperature rise and static pressure on the indoor side.
8. **Evaluate the compressor.** They test winding resistance and amp draw only after the cheaper causes are ruled out.

**Be cautious if:** A quote calls for a new compressor or system without documented pressure readings and electrical tests. It's reasonable to ask for those readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nothing runs at all, inside or out | Thermostat, breaker, float switch | Check thermostat mode and batteries, breakers, and condensate | Test 24V transformer, fuse, and wiring |
| Indoor blower runs, outdoor unit silent | Failed capacitor or contactor, tripped pressure switch | One power reset, clear debris around outdoor unit | Replace capacitor or contactor; find the cause of the pressure trip |
| Blows cool air; outdoor unit runs | Reversing valve, thermostat O/B setting, low charge | Confirm thermostat is configured as a heat pump | Test valve solenoid, check charge, repair leak |
| Outdoor coil a solid block of ice | Defrost board or sensor, low charge, failed fan | Clear snow, check drainage, use EM HEAT if you have working backup heat | Test defrost controls, check charge and fan |
| Outdoor fan not spinning, compressor hums | Fan motor or capacitor | Turn power off; call for service | Replace fan motor or capacitor |
| Short heat, then shuts off repeatedly | Airflow restriction, high or low pressure trip | Replace filter, open registers | Measure static pressure and charge, read fault code |
| Steam and cold air for a few minutes | Normal defrost cycle | Wait 10–15 minutes | None needed if it recovers |

**Check your warranty first.** Amana is known for long parts warranties, and some models carry unit-replacement coverage for registered units. These often cover compressors and other major parts, but labor is usually extra. Look up your registration before approving a major repair.

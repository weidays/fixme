## What this code means

"Blowing warm air" is a symptom, not a fault code. On most Rheem central air conditioners, the indoor blower moves air through the house, but that air never gets cooled. The supply air feels about the same as the room, or warmer.

This page covers a specific situation: **the system is running and air is coming out of the vents, but it is warm**. Some related cases have their own pages:

- If only the indoor fan runs and the outdoor unit is completely dead, see "Fan runs but no cold air" or "Compressor not running."
- If the air is just a little cool and the house never reaches the setpoint, see "Not cooling."

Warm air with active airflow usually means the refrigeration cycle has stopped moving heat. The usual reasons are:

- the compressor is not pumping,
- the refrigerant charge is low, or
- the outdoor unit can't reject heat.

**Fault display varies by model:**

- Single-stage Rheem Classic and Classic Plus units have no fault display.
- Some communicating and variable-speed Prestige models report diagnostics on the outdoor control board LED or an EcoNet thermostat. The codes are model-specific, so check the label inside the service panel or your installation manual.

**Reset behavior varies too:**

- A compressor's internal thermal overload resets on its own after it cools, which can take 30–60+ minutes.
- Pressure-switch lockouts on equipped models typically hold until a technician clears them or power is cycled.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** This is the most common cause of warm air with the outdoor unit running. Rheem's evaporator and condenser coils, and the line-set connections, can develop leaks over time. A leak often freezes the indoor coil first, then the air warms as the ice blocks the coil.
2. **Failed or weak run capacitor.** The fan may spin, or try to, while the compressor hums and won't start, or starts and quickly trips. This is very common on older Rheem condensers, especially after a hot summer.
3. **Compressor tripped on its internal thermal overload.** This is often caused by low charge, a dirty condenser coil, or a weak capacitor. The overload resets automatically once the compressor cools, so cooling may come and go.
4. **Dirty or blocked outdoor condenser coil.** Cottonwood, grass clippings and debris restrict heat rejection. Head pressure rises, cooling falls off, and the high-pressure switch or overload can trip.
5. **Pitted or stuck contactor.** The contactor may not pull in fully, so only part of the circuit runs. Some contactors use a single pole, which can leave the fan or compressor with power while the other gets nothing.
6. **Thermostat or control issues.** Examples include the thermostat set to Heat or Fan On, a dual-fuel or heat-pump thermostat configured wrong after a swap, or a low-voltage wiring fault.
7. **Restricted airflow causing a frozen evaporator coil.** A clogged filter or closed registers can freeze the coil, and the air turns warm once ice blocks it. Thawing helps only temporarily if the underlying cause is a leak.
8. **Failed compressor.** This is the least likely cause but the most expensive. The compressor may have a shorted or grounded winding, a locked rotor, or internal valve failure.
9. **Metering device restriction or reversing valve fault (heat-pump systems only).** This is uncommon in straight AC. If you have a heat pump, see the heat-pump pages listed below.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

1. **Thermostat settings.**
   - Confirm the mode is **Cool**, not Heat, Auto or Fan Only.
   - Set the temperature at least 3°F below room temperature.
   - Set the fan to **Auto**. With the fan on "On," the blower moves warm air between cooling cycles.
2. **Thermostat batteries.** Replace them if the display is dim, blank, or shows a low-battery icon.
3. **Breakers and disconnect.**
   - In the main panel, check that the AC breaker (usually a double-pole 240V) and the furnace or air handler breaker are both fully On.
   - Check the outdoor disconnect switch or pull-out next to the condenser.
   - If a breaker has tripped, reset it **once**. If it trips again, stop and see "Breaker keeps tripping."
4. **Air filter.** A clogged filter can freeze the coil. Replace it if it's dirty.
5. **Check for a frozen coil.**
   - Look for ice on the copper line at the outdoor unit, or frost on the indoor unit.
   - If you see ice, set the thermostat to **Off** and the fan to **On** for 2–4 hours to thaw it.
   - Do not chip at the ice.
6. **Vents and registers.** Open supply registers and clear blocked return grilles.
7. **Outdoor unit (outside only, power left on, panels closed).**
   - Clear leaves, weeds and debris within about 2 ft of the condenser.
   - From a distance, listen for the compressor's steady hum and check that the fan is spinning.
   - Do not open the panel.
8. **Condensate line.** A full drain pan can trip a float switch, which shuts the outdoor unit off while the blower keeps running. If your pan has a float switch and water is standing, clear the drain line at its accessible clean-out.
9. **Exterior panels.** Make sure the indoor unit's access panels are fully seated.

**If none of this fixes it, turn the system Off and call a technician.** Running it while it blows warm air can overheat the compressor.

## How a technician will diagnose it

A competent tech will usually:

- **Confirm the complaint.** Measure supply and return air temperatures. A temperature drop of roughly 15–22°F across the coil is normal for many systems.
- **Check the outdoor unit for power.** Verify 240V at the disconnect, 24V control at the contactor coil, and the contactor's contact condition.
- **Test the capacitor** with a meter that reads microfarads, comparing it to the rating printed on the part (often ±6%).
- **Check compressor amp draw and windings.**
  - Measure ohms across common, start and run.
  - Check winding-to-ground for a shorted or grounded compressor.
  - Allow time for a hot overload to reset before condemning the compressor.
- **Connect gauges or digital probes.**
  - Read suction and liquid pressures.
  - Calculate superheat and subcooling against Rheem's charging chart. Most Rheem ACs with TXVs charge by subcooling, and the target is on the unit label.
- **Leak-search if the charge is low,** using an electronic detector, bubble solution or a nitrogen pressure test, then quote the leak repair separately from the recharge.
- **Inspect the condenser and evaporator coils** for dirt or ice.
- **Read any control-board or EcoNet diagnostic codes** on communicating models.

**Red flags in a quote:**

- A recharge with no leak search.
- A "bad compressor" verdict without winding readings or a capacitor test.
- A recommendation to replace the whole system without any measured pressures or electrical readings.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outdoor fan and compressor running, vents warm | Low refrigerant from a leak | Turn system off; replace filter | Leak search, repair, evacuate and recharge to subcooling spec |
| Outdoor fan spins, no compressor hum | Weak capacitor or tripped overload | Turn off; wait 1 hour; try once | Test and replace capacitor; check compressor windings |
| Outdoor unit hums but nothing spins | Failed capacitor or stuck compressor | Turn off immediately | Replace capacitor; test compressor for locked rotor |
| Outdoor unit completely silent, indoor fan on | Tripped breaker, contactor, or float switch | Check breaker and disconnect; check condensate | Test contactor and 24V control; replace contactor |
| Ice on copper line or indoor coil | Low airflow or low charge | Replace filter; thaw with fan On, cooling Off | Check charge and blower; clean coil |
| Cools briefly, then warm | Overload tripping, dirty condenser | Clear debris around outdoor unit | Clean condenser coil; check head pressure and charge |
| Warm air right after thermostat swap | Wrong mode or wiring/configuration | Verify Cool mode and fan Auto | Correct thermostat wiring and configuration |
| Tripping breaker with warm air | Shorted compressor or wiring | Reset once only | Diagnose short; see breaker page |

## Repair costs

Typical 2026 US ranges, parts and labor. Prices vary by region, refrigerant type and warranty status.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Service call / diagnostic | $80–$200 (often credited toward the repair) |
| Capacitor replacement | $150–$400 |
| Contactor replacement | $150–$350 |
| Condenser coil cleaning | $100–$250 |
| Leak search | $200–$500 |
| Minor leak repair (Schrader valve, flare, braze joint) | $200–$700 |
| Refrigerant recharge, R-410A | $200–$600 |
| Refrigerant recharge, R-454B | $250–$700 (newer, can cost more) |
| Refrigerant recharge, R-22 | $500–$1,500+ (phased out, scarce) |
| Evaporator coil replacement | $1,200–$2,800 (parts often under warranty; labor and refrigerant extra) |
| Condenser coil replacement | $1,200–$2,500 |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$3,500 (less if parts are under warranty) |
| Full condenser replacement | $3,500–$7,500+ |

On an older R-22 system or a system more than 12–15 years old, a major leak or compressor failure often makes replacement the better value. Ask for both prices.

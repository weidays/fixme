## What this code means

"Ice on indoor coil" is not a numbered fault code. It is a visible condition: frost or ice forms on the evaporator coil behind the front grille of your American Standard indoor head while it runs in Cool or Dry mode.

It happens when the coil surface drops below 32°F. The two usual reasons are:

- **Too little air moving across the coil**, or
- **Too little refrigerant in the system.**

This page covers only the ductless indoor head (wall-mount or ceiling cassette). Ducted central systems are covered in the separate frozen-evaporator article.

Many American Standard mini-split boards have indoor-coil anti-freeze protection. When the coil sensor reads too cold, the board shuts off the compressor while the fan keeps running. The unit then restarts by itself once the coil warms up, so the protection auto-resets. Whether a code appears, and what it looks like, depends on the model.

If icing keeps coming back, the protection is only hiding the underlying problem.

## Common causes, ranked by probability

1. **Dirty or clogged indoor filters.** Mini-split mesh filters load up fast, and restricted airflow is the most common cause of a frozen head.
2. **Dirty indoor coil or blower wheel.** Dust, pet hair, or mold buildup on the coil face or the squirrel-cage blower cuts airflow even when the filters are clean.
3. **Low refrigerant charge from a leak.** Leaks are often at the flare connections on the line set or at the coil. Mini-splits are not supposed to "use up" refrigerant, so low charge means there is a leak.
4. **Indoor fan problems.** A failing indoor fan motor, a fault on the indoor board, or the fan running at its lowest speed for long periods all reduce airflow.
5. **Running in Cool at very low outdoor or indoor temperatures.** This includes low set points (around 60–62°F) on cool nights. Low head pressure lets the coil temperature fall.
6. **Restriction in the refrigerant circuit.** A sticking electronic expansion valve (EEV), a kinked line set, or a blockage starves the coil.
7. **Faulty indoor coil (pipe) temperature sensor (contributing factor).** A bad sensor does not cause icing by itself. If it reads wrong, the anti-freeze protection may never trigger, so ice from one of the causes above keeps building unchecked. This is model-dependent.

## Safe checks before you call anyone

- **Thaw first.** Set the remote to Fan Only, or turn the unit off. Lay towels under the head. Do not chip or scrape the ice, because the fins and tubing damage easily.
- **Check the remote settings.** Make sure the unit is in Cool (not Dry), set to roughly 70°F or higher while troubleshooting, with the fan on Auto or Medium/High rather than Low or Quiet. Replace the remote batteries if the display is dim.
- **Clean the filters.**
  1. Lift the front panel.
  2. Slide out the mesh filters.
  3. Rinse them with lukewarm water.
  4. Let them dry completely.
  5. Reinstall them and snap the panel closed.

  Plan to repeat this every 2–4 weeks during heavy use.
- **Look at the louvers and the space around the head.** Check that the louvers move freely and that nothing (curtains, furniture, shelves) blocks the airflow around the indoor unit.
- **Check the condensate drain.** Make sure the drain hose outlet outside is not crushed or blocked, so the meltwater can drain away.
- **Reset the power once.** Turn the breaker or outdoor disconnect off for 5 minutes, then back on, and restart in Cool.
- **Watch it for a few hours.** If ice comes back with clean filters and correct settings, stop running Cool and call a technician.

Do not remove the cabinet cover, spray coil cleaners into the unit, or add refrigerant yourself.

## How a technician will diagnose it

These are the steps you should expect from a competent technician, so you can sanity-check a quote:

- **Airflow.** They inspect the coil face and blower wheel. Cleaning these may require removing the chassis or doing a bagged coil wash. They also confirm the indoor fan reaches its rated speeds.
- **Refrigerant pressures.** They connect gauges at the outdoor service port and measure suction pressure and superheat (or subcooling, depending on the system). They compare the readings to the American Standard charge chart for your model and line-set length.
- **Leak search.** If the charge is low, they use an electronic detector, bubble solution, or a nitrogen pressure test. Flare joints are checked first. A proper repair includes evacuating the system to a deep vacuum and weighing in the charge. Simply "topping off" is not a proper repair.
- **Sensors and controls.** They measure the indoor coil sensor's resistance against the temperature chart. They read the fault history or diagnostic codes on the indoor and outdoor boards, and they check how the EEV operates.
- **Line set.** They look for kinks or crushed sections, especially where the line passes through the wall.

## Symptom, cause and what to do

| Symptom | Likely cause | DIY action | Technician job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ice on coil, weak airflow, filters gray or matted | Dirty filters | Thaw, then wash and dry the filters | None if the problem resolves |
| Ice returns even with clean filters, dusty coil face visible | Dirty coil or blower wheel | Keep filters clean; stop using Cool | Deep coil and blower cleaning |
| Frost that begins at the liquid-line or distributor inlet side of the coil, hissing sound, oily spot at a flare | Refrigerant leak | Turn off Cool and call a technician | Leak search, repair, vacuum, recharge |
| Fan slow or erratic, ice spreads across the coil | Indoor fan motor or board fault | Set fan to Auto/High; one power reset | Test and replace the fan motor or board |
| Icing only on cool nights with a low set point | Low-load operation | Raise the set point to roughly 70°F or higher, or turn the unit off at night | Verify the charge if it persists |
| Heavy ice with no protection shutdown | Failed coil sensor letting ice build unchecked from another cause | Turn off and call | Test and replace the sensor; find the underlying cause of the icing |
| Partial frost plus poor cooling, normal airflow | EEV or line restriction | Turn off and call | Check the EEV and line set; repair the restriction |

## Repair costs

These are typical US ranges and will vary by region and model.

| Repair | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Filter cleaning (DIY) | $0 |
| Replacement filters | $15–$40 |
| Service call and diagnosis | $100–$200 |
| Professional indoor coil and blower deep clean | $150–$400 per head |
| Leak repair at a flare plus evacuation and recharge | $300–$900 |
| Coil leak or major leak repair with recharge | $800–$1,500+ (sometimes replacing the head makes more sense) |
| Indoor fan motor | $250–$600 installed |
| Indoor coil temperature sensor | $120–$300 installed |
| Indoor control board | $300–$700 installed |
| EEV replacement (usually in the outdoor unit), including refrigerant recovery, brazing and recharge | $600–$1,500 |
| Indoor evaporator coil replacement, including refrigerant recovery, brazing or flare work, nitrogen pressure test, evacuation and recharge | $1,000–$2,500+ (often prompts replacing the whole head instead) |

Parts may be covered by the American Standard warranty if the unit is registered. Labor usually is not covered.
